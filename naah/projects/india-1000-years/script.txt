COLD OPEN
In 1739, a foreign king walked into Delhi, stayed 57 days, and left with so much treasure that
he cancelled taxes back home in Persia for three whole years.
[On-screen: Silhouetted king walking out of Delhi's gates, treasure train
stretching past the horizon. Caption slams in: "PERSIA — NO TAXES FOR 3
YEARS"] Not the British. Not a Mongol. A guy most people have never heard of.

And here's the thing. That wasn't even India's worst century.
For most of the last thousand years, India was the richest place on Earth. At one point, this
one country produced about a quarter of everything made on the entire planet. Two
hundred years later, it produced around three percent.
[On-screen: World GDP pie chart. India's wedge is a quarter of the circle.
Year counter runs 1700 → 1950 and the wedge shrinks to a sliver. Hold on the
sliver.] It was invaded, looted, split in half, and starved, and it is now the most populated

country in the world and one of the fastest growing economies on it.
So how does a place get robbed that many times and keep standing back up? To understand
that, we have to go back to a temple on the coast that kept getting emptied out.

ACT 1 — THE RICHEST HOUSE ON THE STREET WITH NO LOCK ON THE
DOOR
Around the year 1000, India was not a country. Let me be clear about that, because
everything else depends on it. [On-screen: Map of the subcontinent, c.1000 AD,
fracturing into 40+ differently coloured blobs with borders wobbling against
each other.]

Look at this map. See all those separate colours? Hundreds of kingdoms, dozens of
languages, and a warrior class called the Rajputs who were genuinely some of the bravest
fighters in the world and absolutely could not stop fighting each other.
Rajputs had a code of honour. You fought fair. You gave warning. You didn't hit a man who'd
already lost. It was beautiful, and it was about to get very expensive.
Because up in Afghanistan, a ruler named Mahmud of Ghazni had noticed something.
India's temples weren't just places of worship. They were banks. Kings donated gold to
them for centuries. Solid gold statues, jewelled doors, vaults nobody had ever touched.

So Mahmud came down through the mountain passes and robbed them. Then he did it
again. Then again. Seventeen separate raids over about thirty years. [On-screen: Temple
interior. Gold doors, jewelled idol. Corner counter ticks: "RAID 1 OF 17" and
speed-runs to 17.]

His most famous target was the temple of Somnath on the western coast in 1025, and the
gold he carried out of that single building funded an entire empire back in Afghanistan.
He never tried to stay. He just came, emptied the place, and went home. Seventeen times.
Nobody stopped him, because there was no "nobody" to stop him. There were just a
hundred kings who all assumed it was somebody else's problem.
About 170 years later, a different Afghan ruler showed up with a different plan. His name
was Muhammad Ghori, and he didn't want the gold. He wanted the land.
He ran into the strongest king in northern India, Prithviraj Chauhan, at a place called Tarain
in 1191. Prithviraj crushed him. Completely. Ghori was captured alive on the battlefield.
And then, according to the stories, Prithviraj did the honourable thing.
[SKETCH] — Sir, we've captured the invader. — Excellent. Bring him here. — He's right
here. On his knees. Weeping, actually. — Right. Let him go. — I'm sorry? — He's beaten. It's
dishonourable to kill a beaten man. — He came here specifically to take your kingdom. —
And he failed. Give him a horse. — A horse? — He can't walk back to Afghanistan. — Sir,
what if he comes back? — Then I'll beat him again. — ...Should I write that down
somewhere?
[On-screen: Rajput camp at dawn, tents still closed, unsaddled horses — then
enemy cavalry already inside the frame.]

He came back. One year later, same field, and this time Ghori attacked at dawn before the
Rajput army had formed up, which was not how it was supposed to be done. Prithviraj lost,
and northern India lost with him.
Ghori went home to Afghanistan and handed his new Indian territory to a trusted
commander to run. That commander was not a prince. He was not a noble. He had been
bought and sold as property.

ACT 2 — THE SLAVE WHO BECAME KING
The man's name was Qutb al-Din Aibak, and he had been a slave.
Now, that needs explaining, because slave in this system doesn't mean what you're
picturing. Central Asian rulers deliberately bought boys, raised them, trained them as elite
soldiers and administrators, and promoted them on merit. The whole point was that a slave
had no powerful family behind him, so he owed everything to you. It was an insurance
policy.

The insurance policy took over. In 1206, when Ghori was assassinated, Aibak simply
declared himself Sultan of Delhi. A man who had been sold for money now ran northern
India.
To make sure nobody forgot it, he started building a victory tower in Delhi. [On-screen:
Tilt up the full height of the tower, tiny human figures at the base for
scale.]

It's called the Qutb Minar, it's 73 metres of red sandstone, it's still standing today, and it is
still the tallest brick minaret on Earth. He didn't live to see it finished. In 1210, he died
playing polo. He fell off his horse and the saddle went through him. One of the most
powerful men in Asia, taken out by a sport.
What he left behind is called the Delhi Sultanate, and it would run northern India for over
300 years.
And here's a detail I genuinely did not expect to find. In 1236, the throne went to a woman.
[On-screen: Throne room. Row of scowling nobles. A woman in a tunic, no veil,
walks past all of them and sits.]

Her name was Razia. Her father, the sultan, had four sons and looked at all of them and
concluded, out loud, that his daughter was better than the lot. So he named her his heir. The
nobles hated it. Razia ignored them, dropped the veil, wore a tunic, rode at the front of her
own army, and ran the Sultanate for about four years.
Four years, and then the nobles who had never accepted her finally got her. She was
overthrown and killed in 1240. It would be almost seven hundred years before another
woman ran anything in India at that level.
The Sultanate survived her. But it was about to face the single most frightening thing in the
medieval world, arriving from the northwest with a hundred thousand horses.

ACT 3 — THE SULTAN WHO BEAT THE MONGOLS AND THEN FIXED THE
PRICE OF ONIONS
The Mongols were coming. They had already flattened Baghdad, Persia, and most of China.
When they turned toward India, everyone assumed Delhi was finished.
The man sitting on the throne was Alauddin Khalji, and he was ruthless in a very specific,
very effective way. He took the throne by inviting his own uncle, who was also the sultan, to
a friendly riverside meeting and having him beheaded there. Family occasions in this
dynasty were tense.
But the Mongols found him extremely annoying. [On-screen: Map. Thick Mongol
arrows stab down from the northwest repeatedly and each one snaps against a
line of forts. Tally in corner: "MONGOL INVASIONS REPELLED — 1, 2, 3..."]

Between roughly 1297 and 1308, they invaded again and again. Once they got all the way to
Delhi itself and camped outside the walls. Alauddin beat them back every single time. He
built forts along the invasion routes, kept a permanent standing army instead of calling up
soldiers at harvest time, and India became one of the very few places the Mongols tried
repeatedly and never took.
But to keep a permanent army, you have to pay a permanent army. And soldiers need to eat.
And Alauddin could not afford both.
So he invented something the world had basically never seen at this scale. He fixed the
price of everything.
Not suggested. Fixed. Grain, cloth, horses, cattle, sugar, vegetables, slaves, everything got
an official price set by the state.
[On-screen: Market stalls, each with a government price board nailed above
it. Inspector in the background writing in a ledger, unblinking.] Then he built

an entire inspection bureaucracy to enforce it, with government-run markets and
undercover agents sent to buy things and check nobody was overcharging.

[SKETCH] — Right. Wheat is now this price. — And if the harvest is bad? — Still that price.
— And if the harvest is amazing? — Still that price. — Sir, that's not really how markets— —
I know exactly how markets work. That's the problem. — What if a merchant cheats? —
Then I take from him exactly what he overcharged. — Money? — Flesh. Same weight. —
...I'll go and tell the merchants. — Tell them warmly.

It worked. Prices in Delhi stayed flat for years, and his army got paid on a small salary
because that salary actually bought things. It is one of the most aggressive economic
experiments of the entire medieval world and it happened in Delhi in the year 1300.
Alauddin died in 1316, and within a couple of decades the throne went to a man who was, by
every account, brilliant. Genuinely learned. Spoke multiple languages, studied
mathematics, philosophy, medicine.
He was also about to have two ideas so catastrophically bad they're still taught in Indian
schools as a warning.

ACT 4 — THE GENIUS WHO BROKE HIS OWN EMPIRE TWICE
His name was Muhammad bin Tughlaq, and idea number one was that Delhi was in the
wrong place.
He had a point, honestly. The empire had expanded south, and Delhi was stuck up in the far
north, weeks of travel from half his territory. A capital in the middle would be easier to run.
Reasonable.
[On-screen: Map. A single line labelled "THE ENTIRE CITY" crawls 1,100 km
south. Small figures drop off the line as it moves.]

His solution was to move the capital roughly 1,100 kilometres south to a city he renamed
Daulatabad. And when he said move the capital, he did not mean move the government. He
meant move the city. The nobles, the clerks, the poets, the shopkeepers, ordinary families.
Everybody. Across the country. In the heat.
Enormous numbers of people died on the road. And when the survivors arrived and settled
in, he looked at the situation, realised Daulatabad was too far from the northern frontier to
defend against invasions, and ordered everyone to march back to Delhi.
Both directions. On foot. For nothing.
Idea number two was money. Silver was running short worldwide, so he decided to issue
token currency: brass and copper coins that were officially worth the same as silver ones.
Which is actually how modern money works, so in principle, ahead of his time.
In practice, he forgot one small thing.
[On-screen: Split screen — royal mint stamping coins on the left; a man in a
kitchen melting a pan over a fire on the right. Both produce identical
coins.]

[SKETCH] — The new coins are a huge success, Your Majesty. Circulation is enormous. —
Wonderful. The treasury must be full. — Full of coins, yes. — Excellent. — Not ones we
made. — Sorry? — Every household with a furnace is making them, sir. My neighbour made
forty this morning. He's a baker. — Can they do that? — It's brass, sir. It's a pan. — ...And
they're paying their taxes with these? — Enthusiastically. — How enthusiastically? — We've
been paid four times this year by a man who owns one goat.

He had to cancel the whole scheme and buy the fake coins back with real silver, which
emptied the treasury he was trying to fill. The historian Ibn Battuta, a Moroccan traveller
who actually worked as a judge in Tughlaq's court, wrote all of it down, and his account is
one of the great eyewitness documents of the medieval world.
Quick pause. If you're enjoying this, a like genuinely helps more than a wheelbarrow of
brass coins. And unlike the coins, it's free.
And stay with me, because everything so far has been invaders taking gold and going home.
In about fifteen minutes, a trading company in London with shareholders and a quarterly
dividend is going to walk in, buy this entire country with a bribe, and then do something no
invader in a thousand years had managed.
[On-screen: Two-second flash-forward — a ledger page, a Company flag, a
signature. Then snap back to the Sultanate.]

The Sultanate never recovered. Provinces broke away one after another. And down in the
south, in the middle of that chaos, two brothers founded a city that would become, for a
while, the richest place any European had ever laid eyes on.

ACT 5 — THE CITY OF DIAMONDS SOLD IN THE STREET
The empire was called Vijayanagara, founded in 1336, and its capital sat among enormous
boulder fields in southern India at a place now called Hampi.
I want to give you the descriptions from people who actually went there, because they don't
sound real.
[On-screen: Hampi's boulder landscape, then a slow push down a market street
lined with gem trays and rose baskets.]

A Persian ambassador, Abdur Razzaq, arrived in the 1440s and reported that he had seen
nothing like it anywhere on Earth. He described bazaars where jewellers sold pearls, rubies,
emeralds and diamonds openly in the market, out on display, the way you'd sell vegetables.
Later, a Portuguese visitor named Domingo Paes described rose sellers at every corner of
the city, because people there simply could not get through a day without fresh roses.
Fresh roses. Every single day. As a basic necessity.
The empire ran on trade, exporting cotton, spices and iron, and importing warhorses by the
thousand from Arabia. At its height the capital may have held several hundred thousand
people, which would have made it one of the largest cities in the world at the time. Second
only to Beijing by some estimates.
It lasted for a bit over two centuries, mostly by playing its neighbours off each other. The
problem with playing your neighbours off each other is that eventually your neighbours
have a conversation.
In 1565, five neighbouring sultanates put aside their own rivalries, combined their armies,
and met Vijayanagara at Talikota. The empire's commander was cut down, his army broke,
and the victorious armies walked into the undefended capital.
They didn't loot it for a day. They stayed for months, systematically dismantling one of the
wealthiest cities on the planet. [On-screen: Same market street shot from earlier,
now dissolving to the modern ruins in identical framing. Hold the match cut.]

What's left at Hampi today is about 25 square kilometres of ruined temples, stone chariots
and empty market streets, and you can walk through them right now.
Meanwhile, about 2,000 kilometres north, a young man in Kabul was having a miserable
decade. He had inherited a small kingdom at eleven, lost it, taken it back, lost it again, and
was now broke and out of options.
He was also the great-great-great-grandson of Timur, and on his mother's side, a descendant
of Genghis Khan. And he had gunpowder.

ACT 6 — THE HOMESICK CONQUEROR WHO COMPLAINED ABOUT THE
FRUIT
His name was Babur, and in 1526 he brought about 12,000 men down into India to fight the
Sultan of Delhi, who had somewhere around 100,000 and a hundred war elephants.
He won in a single morning.
[On-screen: Top-down tactical diagram — chained cart line, gunners behind,
cannon in the gaps. Enemy mass charges in, elephants rebound and stampede
backwards through their own infantry. Numbers on screen: 12,000 vs 100,000.]

At the First Battle of Panipat, Babur chained his supply carts together into a long barrier,
put matchlock gunners behind them, and hid field cannons in the gaps. Then he let the
enemy charge into it. War elephants, it turns out, do not react well to artillery. They
panicked and stampeded backwards through their own infantry.
That morning created the Mughal Empire, which would run most of the subcontinent for
the next two hundred years.
And what did the founder of the greatest empire in Indian history think of India? [Onscreen: Baburnama pages turning, with the complaints highlighted one by one
like a bad hotel review. Star rating in the corner drops.]

He wrote it down in his diary, and honestly, it's one of my favourite documents in all of
history, because the man is relentlessly negative.
[SKETCH] — Your Majesty, you rule Hindustan. The richest land on Earth. — There's no
ice. — ...Sir? — No ice. No cold water. In Kabul you could have snow in your cup. — We have
the wealth of a continent. — The melons are bad. — Sir. — There are no good melons. No
good grapes. The horses are bad. The bread is bad. — Would you like to list anything
positive? — There's a great deal of gold. — There we go. — And absolutely nothing worth
spending it on. — Shall I send for a melon? — From Kabul. — That's four months away, sir. —
I'll wait.

Babur died four years later. His son Humayun promptly lost the whole empire to an Afghan
rival, spent fifteen years in exile, got it back, and then in 1556 died by falling down his own
library stairs.
Which handed the empire to a thirteen-year-old boy. And that boy turned out to be one of
the most remarkable rulers anyone has ever produced.
His name was Akbar, and he ruled for forty-nine years. He was almost certainly illiterate,
possibly dyslexic, and never learned to read. So he had books read aloud to him, constantly,
for decades, and built one of the great libraries of the world for a man who couldn't use it.
What he actually did was harder than conquest. He ran a Muslim empire over a mostly
Hindu population, and instead of ruling against that, he built around it. He abolished the

jizya, the tax non-Muslims had to pay. He married Rajput princesses and, crucially, brought
their families into his government as generals and governors rather than servants. He put
Hindus in the highest offices in the land.
Then he went further and started inviting Hindu, Jain, Christian, Zoroastrian and Muslim
scholars into a hall to argue theology in front of him, week after week, while he sat and
listened.
His grandson had different priorities. He would spend twenty-two years and a fortune
building a tomb for his wife.

ACT 7 — A BILLION DOLLARS OF MARBLE AND THE SON WHO WATCHED
IT FROM A CELL
Shah Jahan's wife Mumtaz Mahal died in 1631 giving birth to their fourteenth child. He
built her the Taj Mahal.
[On-screen: 22-year construction time-lapse — scaffolding climbing, elephant
trains hauling marble, dome closing. Year counter 1632 → 1653.]

Around 20,000 workers. Twenty-two years. White marble hauled by a reported thousand
elephants, jade from China, turquoise from Tibet, lapis lazuli from Afghanistan. Modern
estimates of the cost, adjusted forward, tend to land somewhere around a billion dollars. For
a grave.
And here's the detail that gets me. It's not just pretty, it's mathematically obsessive. [Onscreen: Technical diagram. Minaret lean exaggerated, dotted fall-lines shown
landing away from the tomb.]

The four minarets lean very slightly outward, so that in an earthquake they would fall away
from the tomb rather than onto it. Someone thought about that in the 1640s.
He also commissioned the Peacock Throne, a solid gold seat encrusted with emeralds,
rubies and diamonds, including the Koh-i-Noor. It took seven years to make and reportedly
cost about twice as much as the Taj Mahal. Remember it. It's leaving the country shortly.
Then in 1657 Shah Jahan got sick, and his four sons did what Mughal sons did, which was
fight each other to the death over the throne. The winner was his third son, Aurangzeb, who
executed his eldest brother, hunted down the other two, and then made a decision that tells
you everything.
He didn't kill his father. He locked him in the fort at Agra for the last eight years of his life, in
a room with a view of the Taj Mahal across the river.
[On-screen: Framed through a barred window — the Taj, small and white and far
away across the water. Hold uncomfortably long. No music.]

Aurangzeb ruled for forty-nine years and pushed the Mughal Empire to its largest size ever.
[On-screen: Mughal territory blooming to its maximum extent, then the
treasury bar underneath it draining to empty at the same time.]

Look at this map. Almost the entire subcontinent under one flag, for the only time before
the British.
It was also the beginning of the end, for two reasons.
The first was policy. He reversed Akbar's approach, brought back the jizya tax on nonMuslims, and took a harder religious line. In an empire where the overwhelming majority of
his subjects were Hindu, that turned a lot of quiet regions loud.
The second was the Deccan. He went south to finish off his enemies and spent the last
twenty-seven years of his life down there, campaigning. He won battles constantly and
never won the war, because his main opponents didn't fight wars the way he did. They were
Marathas, led by a man named Shivaji, and they specialised in fast cavalry raids, hill forts,
and vanishing.
Aurangzeb died in 1707 at eighty-eight, in a tent, in the Deccan, having drained the richest
treasury in the world. He left instructions that his own burial be paid for out of money he'd
earned personally copying manuscripts.
Thirty-two years later, a Persian king would walk into Delhi and take everything that was
left.

ACT 8 — 57 DAYS, AND A TAX HOLIDAY IN PERSIA
His name was Nadir Shah, and he'd noticed what everybody had noticed, which was that
the Mughal Empire was now a very rich man with no lock on his door.
He crushed the Mughal army at Karnal in 1739 and entered Delhi. At first it was orderly.
Then a rumour spread through the city that Nadir Shah had been killed, and a mob attacked
his soldiers. Several thousand were killed.
His response was to order a general massacre. For one day, his army killed in the streets of
Delhi. Estimates run to around 30,000 people.
[On-screen: Restrained. Empty streets, overturned market stalls, smoke. Do
not animate the killing.]

Then he spent the next weeks emptying the city. The imperial treasury. The jewels. And the
Peacock Throne, with the Koh-i-Noor still in it, [On-screen: Animated route line for
the Koh-i-Noor — Delhi → Persia → Afghanistan → Punjab → London, ending on the
Tower of London display case.]

which is why that diamond then travelled from Persia to Afghanistan to Punjab and
eventually to London, where it sits in the Tower of London today.

He took so much back to Persia that he cancelled taxation there for three years. One
robbery. Three years of a national tax holiday.
The Mughal Empire technically continued for another 120 years, but only technically. Real
power broke apart across the map. The Marathas, those raiders from the Deccan, became
the biggest force in India and pushed all the way north to Delhi.
[On-screen: Reuse the exact Panipat map frame from Act 6, now labelled 1761.
Same ground, different armies.]

And then in 1761, at Panipat, the same field where Babur had founded the empire, the
Marathas met an Afghan invasion force and lost catastrophically. Tens of thousands dead in
a single day. It didn't destroy them, but it broke the momentum at the exact moment they
might have replaced the Mughals entirely.
So now picture the board. The old empire is a shell. The strongest challenger has just been
mauled. Dozens of regional powers are circling each other, and every one of them needs
money and modern weapons to fight the others.
And sitting on the coast, with money and modern weapons, is a business.
Not a country. A business, with shareholders, in London.

ACT 9 — THE COMPANY THAT BOUGHT A COUNTRY
The East India Company arrived in the 1600s to trade. Cotton, silk, spices, saltpetre, and tea.
For about 150 years, that's genuinely all it did, from a few fortified coastal warehouses, with
permission from Indian rulers.
Then it noticed that Indian rulers were now fighting each other constantly, and that both
sides in any given fight would pay well for trained troops and artillery. So the Company
started renting out soldiers. Then it started picking which side won. Then it started picking
who the ruler was.
The turning point was Bengal in 1757. Bengal was the richest province in India, and its
young ruler, Siraj-ud-Daulah, was fed up with the Company fortifying its base without
permission, so he marched on it and took it.
The Company sent Robert Clive with about 3,000 men to take it back. Siraj had around
50,000.
[On-screen: Two troop blocks sized honestly — a tiny red square beside a huge
one.]

Clive didn't beat that army. He bought it.
[SKETCH] — Their commander is named Mir Jafar. He commands roughly a third of their
forces. — And is he happy in his work? — He is not. He would rather be ruler himself. —
How much does "rather" cost? — Sir, we have three thousand men. They have fifty. —

Which is why I'm asking about the price rather than the tactics. — And if he takes the
money and fights anyway? — Then we're in exactly the situation we're already in. — That's
grimly logical. — Write to him. Be generous. Mention the throne. — Do we intend to actually
give him the throne? — We intend to give him the chair.
[On-screen: Battle map. One third of the large block greys out and does not
move. The rest collapses.]

At Plassey, Mir Jafar's section of the army simply stood still and watched. Siraj's forces
collapsed. Mir Jafar got the throne and the Company got Bengal, which within a few years
meant the Company had the right to collect the taxes of 30 million people.
A corporation with shareholders now had tax authority over a population larger than
Britain's. And it had its own army, which grew to roughly a quarter of a million men, about
twice the size of the British Army itself.
Then came 1770. The rains failed in Bengal, and famine hit. Around 10 million people died,
roughly a third of the province.
And during it, the Company's tax collection didn't stop.
[On-screen: Split frame — empty village on one side, a London ledger column
of rising revenue figures on the other. Keep the famine side restrained:
doors, empty grain baskets, no bodies.] In some districts it went up, because the

Company had revenue targets and shareholders expecting a dividend.
That's the point where a lot of people back in Britain started asking what exactly this thing
was, and Parliament began slowly taking control of it. But it took another eighty-seven
years, and it took soldiers refusing to bite a cartridge.

ACT 10 — THE GREASE AND THE LAST EMPEROR
By 1857, most of the Company's army in India was Indian. Hundreds of thousands of Indian
soldiers, called sepoys, with a thin layer of British officers on top.
There was already a long list of grievances. Kingdoms were being annexed on legal
technicalities whenever a ruler died without a biological son. Soldiers were being sent
overseas, which broke caste rules for many of them. Pay and promotion were capped.
Then the Company issued a new rifle. To load it, you had to bite open a paper cartridge that
was greased to keep it waterproof. [On-screen: Extreme close-up. A paper cartridge.
Teeth approaching it. Freeze one frame before contact.]

And a rumour spread through the barracks that the grease was made from cow fat and pig
fat.

Cows are sacred to Hindus. Pigs are forbidden to Muslims. One cartridge managed to offend
essentially everybody.
Whether the rumour was true has been argued about for 170 years. It didn't matter. Men
believed it, and they believed it because they already believed everything else.
In March 1857 a sepoy named Mangal Pandey attacked his officers and was hanged. By
May, regiments were rising across northern India. And here's the part that made the British
genuinely afraid: the rebels marched to Delhi, walked into the Red Fort, and declared the
old, powerless, 82-year-old Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar their leader.
Zafar was a poet. He wrote in Urdu, he was frail, and he had been living on a British pension
in a palace he did not control. He did not ask for this. He was handed an empire he had no
ability to run.
The fighting lasted more than a year and was savage on both sides. The British retook Delhi,
and the reprisals were brutal. Zafar's sons were shot. [On-screen: Old man in a bare
room in Rangoon, writing. Wide shot, mostly empty frame.]

He was put on trial and exiled to Rangoon, in Burma, where he died in 1862 and was buried
in an unmarked grave so that his tomb could never become a shrine.
He wrote a line in exile about the misfortune of a man who cannot find even two yards of
ground for burial in the land he loved. The last Mughal emperor, descendant of Babur and
Akbar, died a prisoner in another country.
After 1857, the British Crown took India directly from the Company. Queen Victoria
became Empress of India. The Raj had begun.
And it built things. [On-screen: Rail network drawing itself across the map, line
by line — genuinely impressive, let it look impressive.]

Roughly 60,000 kilometres of railway, one of the largest networks on Earth, plus telegraph
lines, canals and universities.
But look at the other number.
[On-screen: Bring back the cold-open GDP pie chart. Same graphic, now with
the 1700 and 1950 figures side by side. Callback framing must match exactly.]

Around 1700, India produced something like a quarter of the world's economic output. By
1950, it was around four percent. Indian textiles, which had clothed the world for centuries,
were priced out by British factory cloth while Indian raw cotton was shipped out to feed
those same factories.
And then a lawyer got thrown off a train in South Africa.

ACT 11 — THE MAN WHO PICKED UP A HANDFUL OF SALT
Mohandas Gandhi was a trained barrister working in South Africa when he was thrown out
of a first-class railway carriage he had a valid ticket for, because of his skin. [On-screen:
Night platform, one suitcase, one man, single lamp. Train pulling away.]

He spent that night on a cold platform, and he spent the next twenty years developing a
method.
He came back to India in 1915 and did something the earlier nationalist leaders hadn't. They
were lawyers and professors arguing in English in Calcutta drawing rooms. Gandhi went to
villages, wore what villagers wore, and organised farmers.
Then came 1919. In Amritsar, a crowd of thousands gathered in a walled garden called
Jallianwala Bagh. [On-screen: Overhead plan of the walled garden. Highlight the
single narrow exit. Then cut to black and hold for the duration of the firing.
Do not animate it.]

A British brigadier marched troops to the entrance, blocked the only main exit, and ordered
them to fire into the crowd without warning. They fired for about ten minutes, until the
ammunition was nearly gone. The official death count was 379. Indian estimates ran well

The brigadier was relieved of command. He was also publicly celebrated by large parts of
the British press and public, and a fund was raised to give him a sizeable purse of money.
That did more for Indian independence than any speech ever delivered.
Gandhi's answer was to make British rule impossible to operate rather than impossible to
defend. The clearest example is salt.
Britain held a monopoly on salt. Indians were forbidden from collecting or making their
own and had to buy taxed salt. In a hot country, where everyone needs salt, and the poorest
need it most.
So in March 1930, at sixty years old, Gandhi walked. [On-screen: Map. Dotted line
creeping toward the coast, day counter 1→24, and the marching crowd icon
growing thicker at every village.]

He set off from his ashram with 78 followers and walked 385 kilometres to the sea at Dandi
over 24 days, and the crowd grew every day, and the world's newspapers followed the whole
way. On the morning of 6 April he waded into the shallows and picked up a lump of natural
salt from the mud.
That was the crime. Bending down and picking up salt.

Around 60,000 people were jailed. The salt tax survived a while longer, but the authority
behind it didn't.
Then came the Second World War, which India was entered into without a single Indian
being consulted. Over two million Indians volunteered and fought, the largest volunteer
army in history. And in 1943, famine hit Bengal again. Around three million people died,
while wartime policy kept grain moving out of the province and refused offers of imported
relief.
By 1945 Britain was victorious, exhausted and broke, and it was now obvious that it could
not hold India. So independence was coming.
It came with a line on a map, drawn by a man who had never set foot in the country.

ACT 12 — FIVE WEEKS TO CUT A CONTINENT IN HALF
His name was Cyril Radcliffe, he was a British lawyer, and he arrived in India in July 1947.
He had never been there before. That was considered a qualification, because it meant he
had no local loyalties. He was given roughly five weeks to draw the border that would divide
British India into India and Pakistan, splitting the provinces of Punjab and Bengal down the
middle.
[On-screen: One man, one desk, one ceiling fan, maps stamped "1941". Pull
back to show how small the room is against the size of the map.]

He worked from outdated maps and census data, in the heat, mostly in a bungalow,
deciding which villages would end up in which country.
[SKETCH] — How long do I have? — About five weeks. — For what, exactly? — Punjab and
Bengal. Where the line goes. — Have I been given accurate maps? — You've been given
maps. — Population figures? — From the last census. — Which was? — 1941. — That's six
years ago. There's been a war. — Yes. — And nobody local is advising me because— —
Because anyone local would have a preference, sir. — And I don't? — You've never been
here. That's rather the point. — Yes. I'm beginning to see the flaw in it.

The line was published on 17 August 1947, two days after independence. So for two days,
tens of millions of people were free and had no idea which country they were in.
[On-screen: The line draws itself through Punjab and Bengal. Villages on the
map blink between two colours before settling.]

What followed was one of the largest and fastest movements of people in human history.
[On-screen: Restrained. Two opposing arrow flows across the new border,

thickening. Silhouettes, belongings on heads, no violence depicted. Music
drops out entirely.]

Somewhere between 10 and 15 million people crossed the new border in a few months.
Hindus and Sikhs moving one way, Muslims the other, often with what they could carry.
And it turned into slaughter. Trains arrived at stations with everyone aboard dead.
Estimates of the death toll run from several hundred thousand to two million. Families that
had lived in the same towns for centuries were on opposite sides overnight.
Gandhi spent independence day itself in Calcutta, fasting, trying to stop the killing there.
Five months later, on 30 January 1948, he was shot dead by a Hindu extremist who believed
he had been too accommodating to Muslims.
[On-screen: Clock striking midnight. Flag rising. Then immediately intercut
one frame of the refugee column. Do not let the triumph sit clean.]

Meanwhile at midnight on 14 August, Jawaharlal Nehru had stood up and told a free India
that at the stroke of the midnight hour, while the world slept, India would awake to life and
freedom.
Both things were true on the same night. That's the thing about 1947. It is the greatest day
in modern Indian history and one of the worst, and there is no version where you get one
without the other.

ACT 13 — 565 KINGDOMS, THREE WEEKS OF MONEY, AND A ROCKET TO
MARS
Here's something most people outside India don't know. In 1947, Britain only directly ruled
about two-thirds of the place. The rest was 565 separate princely states, technically
independent, with their own rulers, and they all had to choose.
[On-screen: Map speckled with 565 separate colours, then rapidly resolving to
a single colour, state by state. Counter runs down 565 → 0. Leave Kashmir
flickering.]

Bringing them in fell to Vallabhbhai Patel, and it is one of the most underrated
administrative achievements of the century. Most joined through negotiation. A few
needed serious pressure. Hyderabad, which was the size of France, was taken by military
action in 1948. Kashmir chose India under invasion and is still contested today, which is
why India and Pakistan have fought repeatedly over it ever since.
And then India did the thing nobody expected from a country that poor. It held elections.
Universal adult suffrage from the start, in 1951, for a population that was around 85 percent
unable to read, [On-screen: A ballot paper — no words, just symbols. A hand
marks one.]

which meant ballot papers with party symbols instead of names. It worked. It's still how it's
done.
The next four decades were hard. India imported food to survive into the 1960s until the
Green Revolution turned it into a grain exporter. It fought China in 1962 and lost, fought
Pakistan repeatedly, and in 1971 intervened in the war that created Bangladesh.
In 1975, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, facing a court ruling against her election, declared a
state of Emergency. Opposition leaders jailed, press censored, elections suspended, for 21
months. And then, in 1977, for reasons historians still argue about, she called an election
anyway.
She lost. Badly. And she accepted it and left office.
Then 1991, and the moment that changed everything. India's foreign currency reserves fell
to roughly three weeks of imports. Three weeks from being unable to buy oil. [On-screen:
Crates loaded onto a plane at night. Route line Delhi → London. Caption: "47
TONNES"]

To get an emergency loan, India physically airlifted 47 tonnes of its gold reserves to London
as collateral, and it had to be done quietly because the humiliation would have brought the
government down.
Out of that came the reforms. Licence requirements scrapped, foreign investment allowed,
currency devalued. India stopped trying to make everything itself and opened up. Growth
went from around 3 percent a year to, in good years, over 8.
Then software. Indian engineers, cheap satellite bandwidth and a time zone exactly
opposite America's built an IT industry out of almost nothing. Bangalore went from a
pensioners' town to a global tech centre in about fifteen years.
And in 2014, India put a spacecraft into orbit around Mars on its first attempt, which
nobody else had ever done. It cost about 74 million dollars, which is less than the
Hollywood film Gravity cost to make.
