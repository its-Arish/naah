# The Entire History of Montana

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "But in the city of Butte"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Big, open, awed storyteller under a huge sky. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, then rough-and-tumble Wild West, wry. |
| Part 3 | Serious and steady. |
| Part 4 | Booming and greedy, larger than life; then heavy. |
| Part 5 | Brave and plain-spoken. |
| Part 6 | Calm and wide open; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 13,000 BC to 1805 · Explorers 1805 to 1862 · Gold Camps 1862 to 1876 · Last Battles 1876 to 1882 · Copper 1882 to 1917 · Big Sky 1917 to 1982 · Today 1982 to now.

## Hook

In Montana, there are more cows than people. <short pause> More than two cows for every person.

<chuckle> Until nineteen ninety-nine, many of Montana's highways had no daytime speed limit. Just "reasonable and prudent".

The first Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton ever found came out of Montana. And Montana elected the first woman ever to sit in Congress. Four years before women could vote across America.

<long pause> But in the city of Butte, there's a lake so toxic that when a giant flock of geese landed on it, thousands of them died.

<short pause> So how does a lonely state with more cows than people end up wiring up all of America?

<whispers> It starts with a hill everyone thought was worn out.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Montana is huge. <gasp> You could fit Great Britain inside it, with room to spare. Mountains in the west. Endless grass plains in the east, under a sky so wide it's called Big Sky Country.

Sixty-six million years ago, those plains were swampy forests. And in them walked Tyrannosaurus rex. <short pause> In nineteen-oh-two, a fossil hunter named Barnum Brown digs in the badlands of eastern Montana, and finds the first skeleton of T. rex ever discovered.

<chuckle> And in nineteen seventy-eight, at a place now called Egg Mountain, scientists find whole nests of baby dinosaurs, with their mothers nearby. They name the dinosaur Maiasaura. The "good mother lizard".

## Part 1B

People have lived here for at least thirteen thousand years. <short pause> In nineteen sixty-eight, construction workers near the town of Wilsall uncover the burial of a little boy, buried with more than a hundred stone and bone tools, covered in red ochre.

He lived about twelve thousand eight hundred years ago. <gasp> His DNA shows he's directly related to many Native Americans living today. In twenty fourteen, he was reburied, in a ceremony led by Native tribes.

## Part 1C

For thousands of years, the plains are full of buffalo. Millions of them.

The Blackfeet, the Crow, the Assiniboine, the Gros Ventre, the Cheyenne and the Lakota follow the herds. In the mountains live the Salish, the Kootenai and the Pend d'Oreille.

Before horses, hunters drive buffalo herds toward cliffs, and over the edge. Buffalo jumps. <short pause> One near Great Falls is among the largest in North America, used for hundreds of years.

Then, in the seventeen hundreds, horses arrive from the south. <gasp> The plains nations become some of the greatest horse riders the world has ever seen.

## Part 2: Explorers

In eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark travel up the Missouri River, across Montana.

<groan> It takes them almost a month to carry their heavy boats around the Great Falls of the Missouri. Prickly pear cactus spikes go right through their moccasins.

<chuckle> Grizzly bears keep chasing them. One chases Meriwether Lewis straight into the river.

## Part 2B

Their guide and translator is a young Shoshone woman, Sacagawea, carrying her baby on her back. She was kidnapped from her people as a girl.

<short pause> In southwest Montana, the expedition finally meets a band of Shoshone. They need Shoshone horses to cross the mountains. Sacagawea sits down to translate. And she looks at the chief.

<gasp> It's her brother.

<sigh> She hasn't seen him since she was taken, five years before. She bursts into tears. The expedition gets its horses.

<short pause> On the way home, in eighteen-oh-six, Clark climbs a giant sandstone rock by the Yellowstone River, and carves his name and the date into it. <chuckle> He names the rock after Sacagawea's little boy, whom he nicknames Pomp. Pompey's Pillar.

His carving is still there. The only mark the expedition left that you can still see on the trail.

## Part 2C

Fur traders follow. Then steamboats, chugging two thousand miles up the Missouri to Fort Benton. <chuckle> People call it the world's innermost port. A steamboat harbour, in the middle of the continent.

And then, gold.

In eighteen sixty-two, prospectors find gold in a creek called Grasshopper Creek. The town of Bannack appears almost overnight. Then Virginia City. Then a place where four tired prospectors decide to give it one "last chance". Last Chance Gulch.

<laugh> They strike it rich. The camp becomes Helena, the capital. At one point, it's said to have more millionaires for its size than almost any city in the world.

## Part 2D

The gold camps are lawless. Robbers attack stagecoaches. And in Bannack, people suspect the sheriff himself, Henry Plummer, is leading a gang.

<tsk> So in eighteen sixty-four, a secret vigilante group hangs him. Some say on a gallows he'd built himself.

<short pause> The vigilantes leave a mysterious number on the doors of men they want gone. Three, seven, seventy-seven. <whispers> Nobody is quite sure what it means. Today, it's on the shoulder patch of the Montana Highway Patrol.

## Part 3: Last Battles

The miners, ranchers and railroads push onto Native land.

In June eighteen seventy-six, near the Little Bighorn River, Lieutenant Colonel George Custer attacks a huge camp of Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho people. Thousands of people, led by Sitting Bull.

<short pause> A few weeks earlier, at a Sun Dance ceremony, Sitting Bull had a vision. Soldiers falling upside down into the camp, like grasshoppers from the sky.

<gasp> Led by warriors like Crazy Horse, they defeat him completely. Custer and every soldier with him are killed.

<short pause> It's the greatest victory of the Plains nations. And it's the last.

<short pause> For more than a century, the battlefield is named after Custer. In nineteen ninety-one, it's renamed Little Bighorn Battlefield. And now there's a memorial there to the Native warriors who fought, too.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In January eighteen seventy, the army goes looking for a Blackfeet band accused of raids. On the Marias River, in the freezing dawn, soldiers attack a camp.

<breath> It's the wrong camp. A peaceful band, many of them sick with smallpox.

<long pause> About two hundred people are killed, most of them women, children and old people.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

In eighteen seventy-seven, the Nez Perce, chased by the army for more than a thousand miles, are trapped in the Bear Paw Mountains of Montana. About forty miles from safety in Canada.

<long pause> Chief Joseph surrenders, saying, "From where the sun now stands, I will fight no more forever."

## Part 3D (quiet style)

And the buffalo are destroyed. Hunters shoot them by the millions, for their hides, leaving the meat to rot. <breath> Many in the army welcome it, as a way to force the plains nations onto reservations.

By the eighteen-eighties, the great herds are gone. The nations who depended on them face starvation.

## Part 3E

Cattle take the buffalo's place. Cowboys drive huge herds north from Texas, onto Montana's free grass.

<groan> Then comes the winter of eighteen eighty-six. Blizzard after blizzard. Temperatures far below zero. Cattle freeze standing up.

A ranch foreman writes to his boss, asking how the herd is doing. Instead of a letter, a young cowboy named Charlie Russell sends back a little painting. One skinny, starving cow, with wolves waiting nearby. <short pause> He titles it: "Waiting for a Chinook". A warm wind.

<sigh> The rancher gets the message. Russell becomes one of the great painters of the American West.

## Part 4: Copper

Now, the hill.

In southwest Montana, there's a mining camp called Butte. The gold runs out. The silver isn't great either. <tsk> Most people think Butte's hill is worn out.

But an Irish immigrant named Marcus Daly buys a small silver mine there called the Anaconda.

In eighteen eighty-two, his miners dig deeper. <gasp> And they hit a vein of copper. Not a small one. One of the richest copper veins anyone has ever seen.

## Part 4B

And the timing is perfect.

Thomas Edison has just invented his electric light. America is about to put electricity into every city and every home. And electricity runs on copper wire.

<short pause> Butte digs. Thousands of miners, from Ireland, Cornwall, Finland, Serbia, Italy and China, work deep underground, in tunnels so hot the rock is too warm to touch. Butte becomes the biggest city between Minneapolis and Seattle.

For a while, Butte is one of the biggest copper producers in the world. People call it "the Richest Hill on Earth". Its copper carries power and telephone calls across America.

<short pause> Butte is a wild, crowded, round-the-clock city. The mines never stop, so the saloons never close. <chuckle> Its miners form one of the strongest unions in America. People call Butte "the Gibraltar of Unionism".

## Part 4C

The men who own the mines are called the Copper Kings. <chuckle> And they fight each other like kings.

When Montana has to choose a capital, Daly wants his own company town, Anaconda. His rival, William Clark, backs Helena. Both spend fortunes. <laugh> Voters are bribed with cash, drinks and cigars. Helena wins.

<groan> Underground, the fighting is even stranger. A third Copper King, Augustus Heinze, uses an old mining law to claim that copper veins under his rivals' land actually belong to him. Rival miners tunnel into each other's mines. They fight in the dark, with dynamite, choking smoke, and buckets of burning lime.

Then Clark wants to be a United States senator. Back then, state lawmakers chose senators. So he pays them. Lots of them.

<tsk> The Senate investigates, and he has to resign. Scandals like his help convince America to let voters choose their senators directly. The Seventeenth Amendment.

<chuckle> Clark doesn't seem too bothered. He later builds a railroad through the desert, and a little railway town grows up on land he sold. Las Vegas. Its county is still named after him. Clark County.

## Part 4D

In November eighteen eighty-nine, Montana becomes the forty-first state.

## Part 4E (quiet style)

In June nineteen seventeen, a fire breaks out deep in Butte's Granite Mountain mine.

<breath> Smoke and gas fill the tunnels. Some miners build walls to seal themselves in, and write farewell notes to their families.

<long pause> One hundred and sixty-eight miners die. It's the deadliest hard-rock mining disaster in American history.

## Part 5: Big Sky

In nineteen sixteen, Montana elects Jeannette Rankin to Congress. The first woman ever to serve there.

<short pause> She votes against America entering the First World War. And in nineteen forty-one, after Pearl Harbor, she's the only member of Congress to vote against entering the Second World War. <sigh> She has to hide in a phone booth from an angry crowd.

"As a woman, I can't go to war," she says, "and I refuse to send anyone else."

## Part 5B

In the early nineteen-hundreds, the railroads advertise free land in Montana. <chuckle> Posters promise rain will follow the plough. Tens of thousands of homesteaders pour in.

<groan> Then the rain stops. Drought, grasshoppers, and wheat prices crashing. By the nineteen-twenties, many of them have given up and left. Their empty farmhouses still stand on the prairie.

<short pause> In nineteen-ten, the mountains of the north become Glacier National Park. Back then, it has about a hundred and fifty glaciers. <sigh> Today, only a couple of dozen are left.

In the nineteen-thirties, thousands of workers build Fort Peck Dam across the Missouri. One of the biggest dams made of earth in the world.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In August nineteen forty-nine, fifteen smokejumpers parachute into Mann Gulch, to fight a forest fire.

<breath> The wind changes. The fire races up the hill faster than they can run.

<long pause> Twelve of them die, and a forest ranger who had joined them. The disaster changes how wildfires are fought across America.

## Part 5D

In nineteen fifty-five, the Anaconda company starts digging Butte's hill from the top. An open pit, more than a mile wide. The Berkeley Pit.

<sigh> It swallows whole neighbourhoods. In nineteen eighty-two, the mining stops, and the pumps are switched off.

Groundwater floods into the pit. It becomes a giant lake. Bright orange-red, full of acid and heavy metals.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

In November two thousand sixteen, during a snowstorm, a huge flock of snow geese lands on the Berkeley Pit.

<long pause> Thousands of them die.

Today, the water is pumped and treated, and workers scare birds away with noise and lasers.

## Part 5F (quiet style)

In the little town of Libby, a mine digs up a mineral called vermiculite, used in insulation. It's laced with asbestos.

<breath> For decades, dust from the mine blows over the town. Children play in piles of it.

<long pause> Hundreds of people die of lung diseases. In two thousand nine, the government declares its first-ever public health emergency over pollution, for Libby.

## Part 6: Today

<chuckle> Butte also gives America its most famous daredevil. Evel Knievel jumps motorcycles over buses, cars and fountains. And breaks, by some counts, more bones than almost anyone alive.

<short pause> Under Montana's wheat fields sit missile silos, holding nuclear missiles, ready since the Cold War.

And Montana is still big and empty. Ranches, mountains, trout rivers. Glacier National Park, where most of the glaciers have melted away. Yellowstone's northern gateway. And people looking for space.

<chuckle> High on a ridge above Butte stands a white statue of the Virgin Mary, ninety feet tall, built by miners and volunteers and lifted into place by a helicopter. Our Lady of the Rockies. <short pause> And after a nineteen ninety-two movie about two brothers fly-fishing in Montana, so many people buy fly rods that the rivers get crowded.

## Part 6B

So how does a lonely state with more cows than people end up wiring up all of America?

<whispers> It starts with a hill everyone thought was worn out.

Under Butte's tired old hill was copper. And just as America was about to switch on the lights, Montana had the metal to carry the power. <short pause> Butte's miners dug the copper that lit America's cities.

<breath> But the hill paid a price. Its poisoned lake is still there.

<chuckle> Meanwhile, the cows are doing fine. All two and a half million of them.
