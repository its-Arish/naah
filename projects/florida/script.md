# The Entire History of Florida

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 4,300 spoken words, so roughly 24 to 27 minutes when recorded.

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

## Hook

The Gatorade in your gym bag? Invented in Florida... for a college football team called the Gators.

Disney World? Built on swampland bought in SECRET, by companies with fake names.

And the oldest city in the mainland United States built by Europeans? Not in Massachusetts. Not in Virginia. <short pause> St. Augustine, Florida. Founded by Spain fifty-five years before the Mayflower.

<gasp> Florida has been Spanish for longer than it has been American.

<long pause> But in nineteen hundred, the whole state had fewer people than Boston. Swamp. Heat. Fever.

Today, only two states have more people.

<short pause> So how does a swamp almost nobody wanted become the third biggest state in America?

<whispers> It starts with a doctor... a block of ice... and a mosquito.

## Part 1: First Floridians

Let's go back fourteen and a half thousand years.

Florida is twice as wide as it is today, because so much of the world's water is locked up in ice. And at the bottom of a sinkhole, next to a little pond, people are cutting up a mastodon.

We know because divers found their stone knife. Underwater. At the bottom of the Aucilla River. It's one of the oldest signs of people anywhere in North America.

The ice melts. The sea rises. The peninsula shrinks into the shape you know.

And the people stay.

In the north, the Timucua build villages of round houses and farm corn, beans and squash. In the Panhandle, the Apalachee farm so well that their fields will later feed a whole Spanish colony.

But in the southwest lives the most surprising kingdom in early America. The Calusa.

The Calusa don't farm. They don't need to. The sea feeds them: fish, oysters, clams, more than they can eat. And with the shells... they build. Whole islands. Mounds and canals and a capital on an island made of shells, Mound Key.

A powerful kingdom, built out of leftovers from dinner.

For thousands of years, this is Florida. Hundreds of thousands of people, maybe more. Busy rivers. Shell cities.

And then, one spring, sails.

## Part 2: La Florida

In fifteen thirteen, a Spanish captain named Juan Ponce de León lands on the east coast. It's around Easter. In Spain they call Easter the feast of flowers... Pascua Florida.

So he names the land La Florida. The flowery land.

Now, you've probably heard that he came looking for the Fountain of Youth. <chuckle> He didn't. There's no mention of it in anything from his lifetime. That story was attached to him years after he died, by writers who wanted to make him look foolish.

What he really wanted was land, gold and a governorship. He came back eight years later to start a colony... and the Calusa drove him off. He was hit by an arrow and died in Cuba.

Florida was not going to be easy.

In fifteen twenty-eight, another Spaniard, Pánfilo de Narváez, marches three hundred men inland. Out of the whole expedition, four men make it home. Eight years later. On foot. In Mexico.

In fifteen thirty-nine, Hernando de Soto lands at Tampa Bay with six hundred men, pigs and horses, and marches north... all the way into Alabama. If you watched our Alabama film, you know how that ended.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

Then the French arrive. In fifteen sixty-four, they build a fort near today's Jacksonville.

Spain sends Pedro Menéndez de Avilés to remove them.

He founds a base on the coast in September fifteen sixty-five. St. Augustine.

Then he captures the French... and kills most of them, near an inlet still called Matanzas.

In Spanish, matanzas means... slaughters.

## Part 2C

St. Augustine survives. Barely.

Pirates attack it. In fifteen eighty-six, the English sailor Francis Drake burns it to the ground.

So the Spanish build something that won't burn. The Castillo de San Marcos. Out of coquina, a stone made of tiny crushed seashells, glued together over thousands of years.

And here's the thing about shell stone. When British cannonballs hit it, the walls don't shatter. The balls just... sink in. Like throwing rocks into a giant sponge.

The fort has never been taken by force. It's still standing.

Then Spain does something nobody expects.

Enslaved Africans are escaping from the English plantations in Carolina, heading south through the swamps. Spain makes them an offer: become Catholic, defend the colony... and you're free.

In seventeen thirty-eight, the governor gives them their own town and fort, two miles north of St. Augustine. Fort Mose.

It's the first legally recognised free Black town in what is now the United States. Its captain, Francisco Menéndez, was born in West Africa.

Historians have a name for it. The first Underground Railroad... and it ran SOUTH.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

But for the people who were here first, these two hundred years are a catastrophe.

New diseases. War. Raids for slaves.

The Calusa, the Timucua, the Apalachee... peoples who had lived here for thousands of years... are almost gone by the seventeen sixties.

When Spain finally leaves, the last few go with them. To Cuba.

## Part 3: Changing flags

In seventeen sixty-two, Britain captures Havana, Spain's jewel in the Caribbean.

Spain wants it back. So, in seventeen sixty-three, it trades. Havana for Florida.

Florida is now British. They split it in two: East Florida and West Florida.

And when the American Revolution begins, the two Floridas do something interesting. <short pause> They stay loyal. Florida was British... and wanted to stay British.

It doesn't matter. When the war ends in seventeen eighty-three, Britain hands Florida back to Spain.

Spanish again. Twenty years later than before, and much weaker.

Meanwhile, a new group is making Florida home. Creek families moving south from Georgia and Alabama. People escaping slavery, who join them. Together they become a new people with a new name: the Seminole.

The United States doesn't like this. People escaping slavery are finding freedom in Florida. So in eighteen eighteen, General Andrew Jackson marches in, without permission, burns Seminole towns and seizes Spanish forts.

Spain realises it can't hold Florida anymore.

In eighteen nineteen, it signs Florida over. The United States doesn't actually pay Spain for it. It agrees to cover up to five million dollars of claims that Americans had against Spain.

And in eighteen twenty-one, the flag changes for the last time.

## Part 4: Territory

Andrew Jackson becomes Florida's first American governor. He lasts about three months, hates it, and goes home to Tennessee.

There's a bigger problem. Florida has two old capitals, St. Augustine and Pensacola, and they're four hundred miles apart. In the eighteen twenties, travelling between them takes weeks.

So two men set out, one from each city, to find the spot in between. They pick an old Apalachee site in the hills. Tallahassee. A capital chosen because it was halfway.

Settlers pour in. And they want the Seminoles' land.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

The government orders the Seminoles to move west, to what is now Oklahoma.

Many refuse.

From eighteen thirty-five to eighteen forty-two, the Second Seminole War is the longest and most costly war the United States ever fought against Native Americans.

In eighteen thirty-seven, the Seminole leader Osceola comes to peace talks under a white flag of truce.

The army arrests him.

He dies in prison three months later.

Thousands of Seminoles are shipped west. But a few hundred never leave. They disappear into the Everglades... and stay.

## Part 4C

The Seminoles who stayed never surrendered. Today, the Seminole Tribe of Florida calls itself the Unconquered.

And remember that? Because in two thousand seven, that tribe did something nobody saw coming.

It bought the Hard Rock Café. The whole company. For nine hundred and sixty-five million dollars.

Every Hard Rock T-shirt you've ever seen? Owned by the Seminoles.

## Part 5: Fever

Meanwhile, Florida joins the Union. March third, eighteen forty-five. The twenty-seventh state.

And now we meet our doctor.

His name is John Gorrie. He lives in Apalachicola, a busy cotton port on the Gulf coast. And every summer, Apalachicola has a visitor nobody wants.

Yellow fever.

It arrives with the heat. It turns people yellow. In a bad year, it kills a huge share of the people who catch it.

Doctors think the fever comes from bad air and heat. Nobody yet knows the real killer is tiny... and buzzing.

So Gorrie tries to fight the heat itself. He hangs blocks of ice in his patients' rooms. But ice has to be shipped all the way from frozen lakes up north, and it's expensive and it melts.

So in the eighteen forties, a country doctor in a swamp town decides: I'll MAKE ice.

He builds a machine. It squeezes air, lets it expand, and the expanding air gets cold. Cold enough to freeze water.

In eighteen fifty-one, he gets a patent for one of the first ice machines in the world.

<sigh> And nobody wants it. Investors walk away. People laugh at him. The ice merchants up north are not amused.

He dies in eighteen fifty-five, broke.

Today, his statue stands in the United States Capitol. Florida chose him. Because Gorrie had the right idea. Florida didn't need less heat... it needed COLD air.

It would just take another hundred years.

## Part 6: War (quiet style)

By eighteen sixty, nearly half the people in Florida are enslaved. They work the cotton and sugar plantations of the north.

In January eighteen sixty-one, Florida becomes the third state to leave the Union.

Florida is small. It sends soldiers, but mostly it sends food. Beef. Salt. Cattle driven north to feed the Confederate army.

In March eighteen sixty-five, at a place called Natural Bridge, a small Confederate force turns back a Union attack. Tallahassee becomes the only Confederate capital east of the Mississippi that the Union never captured.

It changes nothing. The war ends weeks later.

On May twentieth, eighteen sixty-five, in Tallahassee, a Union officer reads the Emancipation Proclamation aloud.

Freedom.

Florida still celebrates that day.

## Part 7: Rails

After the war, Florida is still mostly empty. Mostly swamp. Mostly mosquitoes.

Then a millionaire gets bored.

Henry Flagler made his fortune helping John D. Rockefeller build Standard Oil. In the eighteen eighties, he visits St. Augustine on his honeymoon and has an idea.

What if rich northerners came here for the winter?

So he builds a palace of a hotel in St. Augustine, named after... Ponce de León. Then he needs a way to get people there. So he buys a railroad. Then he extends it. South. And south. And south.

Every time the track reaches a new spot, he builds another hotel. Palm Beach. Then even further.

Further south lives a widow named Julia Tuttle. She owns land on a river at the very bottom of Florida, and she's been begging Flagler to bring his railroad.

He keeps saying no.

Then, in the winter of eighteen ninety-four, a huge freeze kills orange groves all across northern Florida. And the story goes, Julia Tuttle sends Flagler a bouquet of orange blossoms, from her land, untouched by the frost.

<short pause> The railroad arrives in eighteen ninety-six. The town incorporates that summer. The residents want to name it after Flagler.

He says no thanks. Use the old native name.

Miami.

On the other coast, Henry Plant builds railroads into Tampa, and Cuban cigar makers move in and build a whole neighbourhood, Ybor City. For a while, Tampa is one of the cigar capitals of the world.

And Flagler still isn't done. He wants to go to Key West. A hundred and twenty-eight miles out... across the SEA.

People call it Flagler's Folly.

In January nineteen twelve, Flagler, eighty-two years old and almost blind, rides the first train into Key West. Over the water. A railroad to an island.

Twenty-three years later, a hurricane destroys it. But the bridges survive. Today, you drive them. It's called the Overseas Highway.

## Part 8: Boom

The nineteen twenties. Cars. Jazz. Money. And everybody wants a piece of Florida.

A businessman named Carl Fisher takes a mangrove swamp, pumps sand out of the bay, and builds Miami Beach. He advertises it with billboards in Times Square.

People pour in. Land prices explode. Lots sell, and resell, and resell again, sometimes in a single day. Young salesmen called binder boys sell land they've never seen, to buyers who'll never see it.

Some lots turn out to be underwater. <chuckle> People buy them anyway.

And then, September nineteen twenty-six. A hurricane hits Miami head on.

The bubble pops. Fortunes vanish. Banks fail. Florida falls into the Great Depression three years before the rest of the country.

## Part 8B (quiet style)

These years have darker stories too.

In January nineteen twenty-three, a white mob destroys Rosewood, a small Black town in north Florida. Homes burned. People killed. Families hidden for days in the swamps.

No one is ever charged.

Seventy years later, in nineteen ninety-four, Florida becomes the first state to pay compensation to the survivors of a racist attack.

## Part 8C (quiet style)

And in September nineteen twenty-eight, a hurricane pushes the water of Lake Okeechobee over its low mud walls.

More than two thousand five hundred people drown.

Most of them are Black farmworkers, living in the fields around the lake.

After the storm, Florida builds a giant wall around the lake. It's still there.

## Part 9: War and cool air

The nineteen forties. America is at war again. And this time, the war comes to Florida's beaches.

German submarines hunt off the coast. In nineteen forty-two, people on Florida beaches watch burning ships on the horizon.

In June nineteen forty-two, four German saboteurs paddle ashore at Ponte Vedra Beach, near Jacksonville, with explosives and American money.

They're caught within weeks.

Meanwhile, the army needs places to train... all year round. Florida has sunshine and empty land. Bases go up everywhere. Miami Beach hotels become barracks. Hundreds of thousands of young men from all over the country spend the war in Florida.

And many of them think: when this is over... I'm coming back.

But there's still a problem. Florida summers are brutal. And the mosquitoes are worse.

Remember our doctor? His dream finally comes true.

After the war, air conditioners get cheap. Window units. Then whole houses. Office buildings. Cars.

At the same time, Florida declares war on the mosquito. Draining. Spraying. Whole counties set up mosquito control districts.

And the swamp... becomes livable.

In nineteen fifty, Florida has under three million people. By nineteen sixty... almost five million. One Florida historian put it simply: Florida as we know it would not exist without air conditioning.

Gorrie's cold air. A war on the mosquito. That's the swamp's secret.

## Part 10: Rockets

On July twenty-fourth, nineteen fifty, on an empty stretch of coast called Cape Canaveral, a rocket lifts off. It's the first launch from the Cape.

Why here? Because it's on the east coast, so rockets fly out over the ocean, not over towns. And because it's so far south, the Earth's spin gives every rocket a free push.

Through the nineteen fifties and sixties, the Cape becomes America's gateway to space.

And on July sixteenth, nineteen sixty-nine, the Saturn V, designed in Huntsville, Alabama, lifts off from Florida. Four days later, two humans walk on the Moon.

Designed in Alabama. Launched from Florida. It took both.

## Part 10B

But five years before that launch, back in St. Augustine, America's oldest city is fighting a very different battle.

In nineteen sixty-four, Black residents are still kept out of the city's restaurants, hotels and beaches. Local activists invite Dr. Martin Luther King Junior to help.

King is arrested on the steps of a motel restaurant. It's his only arrest in Florida.

A week later, Black and white protesters jump into that motel's whites-only swimming pool together. The manager pours acid into the water.

The photo goes around the world.

Two weeks later, President Johnson signs the Civil Rights Act of nineteen sixty-four.

The oldest city in the country... helped change it.

## Part 11: The Mouse

Back to the secret.

In November nineteen sixty-three, Walt Disney flies over central Florida in his private plane. He looks down at orange groves, cattle land, swamp. And a place where two new highways are about to cross.

He says: this is it.

But Walt knows that if anyone finds out Disney is buying, prices will skyrocket. So his lawyers create fake companies with boring names. Bay Lake Properties. Reedy Creek Ranch. One is called the Ayefour Corporation. <chuckle> Say it out loud. It's named after the new highway, I-four.

Through those companies, Disney quietly buys more than twenty-seven thousand acres. That's about twice the size of Manhattan. For about five million dollars.

In October nineteen sixty-five, an Orlando newspaper reporter figures it out. The headline reveals the mystery buyer... Disney.

Walt never sees the park. He dies in December nineteen sixty-six.

His brother Roy finishes it, and when it opens on October first, nineteen seventy-one, he names it Walt Disney World. For his brother.

And that Gatorade? It's from the same years. In nineteen sixty-five, at the University of Florida, a kidney doctor named Robert Cade is asked why the football players keep collapsing in the heat. So he mixes water, salt, sugar and lemon. The Gators start winning second halves.

They call the drink Gatorade. The university has earned well over a hundred million dollars from it.

## Part 11B

At the other end of the state, Miami is being transformed too.

In nineteen fifty-nine, a revolution in Cuba brings Fidel Castro to power. Hundreds of thousands of Cubans leave. Many go just ninety miles... to Miami.

They arrive with almost nothing. Doctors work as dishwashers. Lawyers drive taxis. And within a generation, they build Little Havana, banks, businesses... a whole new Miami.

In nineteen eighty, in just six months, about a hundred and twenty-five thousand more Cubans arrive by boat in the Mariel boatlift.

Haitians. Colombians. Venezuelans. Puerto Ricans. Miami becomes a city where you can live your whole life in Spanish.

Remember: Florida was Spanish for more than two hundred years. In a way, it never stopped.

## Part 12: Today

Today, Florida is something nobody in nineteen hundred could have imagined.

In twenty fourteen, it passed New York to become the third biggest state in the country. Around twenty-three million people.

It still has hurricanes. Andrew, in nineteen ninety-two, flattened whole neighbourhoods south of Miami, and Florida rewrote its building rules because of it.

It still decides elections. In two thousand, the presidential race came down to Florida, and Florida came down to five hundred and thirty-seven votes.

The rockets still fly from the Cape. In April twenty twenty-six, Artemis two lifted off from Florida and carried four astronauts around the Moon.

And the Everglades, the swamp everyone once wanted to drain, is now one of the most protected places in America.

## Part 12B

So, how does a swamp almost nobody wanted become the third biggest state in America?

A railroad across the sea. A rocket. A mouse.

But none of it works... without what a country doctor was dreaming of in a fever town in eighteen forty-one.

Cold air.

And a war on the smallest enemy Florida ever had. The mosquito.

<short pause> Fourteen and a half thousand years ago, people stood at the edge of a sinkhole in this swamp and thought: we can live here.

They were right.

Florida. Spanish longer than it's been American. Older than the Mayflower. Built on a swamp.

<chuckle> And next time you open an ice cold Gatorade... that's two pieces of Florida history in one bottle.

The cold... and the Gators.
