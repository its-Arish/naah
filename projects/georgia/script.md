# The Entire History of Georgia

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,600 spoken words, so roughly 14 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen sixty-four"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, curious storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Hopeful, idealistic, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Brisk, then serious and steady. |
| Part 4 | Heavy, then rising. |
| Part 5 | Bright and fizzy, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Warm, proud, hopeful, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Mound Builders 10,000 BC to 1733 · The Experiment 1733 to 1751 · Colony and Revolution 1751 to 1788 · Cotton 1793 to 1860 · Civil War 1861 to 1865 · Atlanta Rises 1865 to 1954 · Civil Rights 1954 to 1970 · Today 1970 to now.

## Hook

Coca-Cola? <short pause> Invented in Atlanta. As a medicine.

Georgia is "the Peach State". <chuckle> But it doesn't even grow the most peaches.

The world's busiest airport? Atlanta.

<long pause> In eighteen sixty-four, an army burned Atlanta, and marched all the way to the sea.

But the strangest thing? Georgia began as the only colony in America that banned slavery.

<short pause> So how does the colony that banned slavery become one of the biggest slave states in the country?

<whispers> It starts with a man whose friend died in a debtors' prison.

## Part 1: Mound Builders

A thousand years ago, Georgia has towns with great earthen pyramids.

At Ocmulgee and Etowah, people of the Mississippian culture build flat-topped mounds, some taller than a six-storey building, with temples and chiefs' houses on top. At Etowah, archaeologists found a pair of marble statues, a man and a woman, buried with the town's leaders. And copper plates showing dancers dressed as birds.

<short pause> In fifteen forty, the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto marches through, looking for gold. His army brings war and disease. The great mound towns fade.

Later, the land belongs mostly to two great nations. The Muscogee, or Creek, in the south and centre. And the Cherokee in the mountains of the north.

<short pause> In the far south lies a giant swamp, full of alligators, black water and floating islands of peat that wobble when you step on them. The Okefenokee. <chuckle> The name is often translated as "land of the trembling earth".

And near Atlanta, a single dome of granite rises out of the forest, more than eight hundred feet high. Stone Mountain.

## Part 1B

On the coast, the Spanish build missions on the sea islands, among the Guale people, for more than a hundred years. <sigh> Pirate raids and English attacks from Carolina finally drive them out.

## Part 2: The Experiment

In seventeen twenty-eight, in London, a man named Robert Castell is thrown into a debtors' prison. In England back then, if you couldn't pay what you owed, you could be locked up until you did. <tsk> And you had to pay the jailers for your own food and bed.

Castell can't pay for a clean room. He's put in a cell with a man who has smallpox. <long pause> He catches it. And he dies.

His friend is a member of Parliament named James Oglethorpe. Oglethorpe is furious. He leads an investigation into England's terrible prisons, and frees hundreds of prisoners.

<short pause> And then he has a bigger idea. A new colony in America, for England's "worthy poor". People who need a second chance.

## Part 2B

In seventeen thirty-two, King George the Second grants the charter. The colony is named after him. Georgia.

It's run by a board of trustees, who aren't allowed to make any money from it. Their motto, in Latin: Not for ourselves, but for others.

In February seventeen thirty-three, Oglethorpe lands on a bluff above the Savannah River with about a hundred and fourteen settlers. He makes friends with the local Yamacraw leader, Tomochichi, with the help of a brilliant interpreter, Mary Musgrove, who is part Creek and part English.

<short pause> The next year, Oglethorpe takes Tomochichi to London. He meets the king, and the Archbishop of Canterbury. Londoners crowd the streets to see him. <chuckle> Tomochichi asks why the English build houses that will last longer than the people living in them.

And he lays out the city of Savannah. A grid of streets with little green squares every few blocks. <short pause> You can still walk them today.

<chuckle> Funny thing, though. Very few actual debtors ever come. The famous "debtors' colony" mostly wasn't.

## Part 2C

But plenty of others do. Within months, a ship arrives with forty-two Jewish settlers, many fleeing persecution in Portugal. Then German Protestants from Salzburg, who'd been thrown out of their homeland. Scottish Highlanders, in kilts, settle in the south to guard the border.

<chuckle> And a young English minister named John Wesley comes to preach. It goes badly. He falls in love, gets into a court fight with the woman's new husband, and sneaks out of Savannah at night. <short pause> Back in England, he founds the Methodist church.

## Part 2D

And Georgia has rules. Strange rules.

No big plantations. Each family gets a small farm. No rum. <chuckle> Which is very unpopular.

And from seventeen thirty-five... no slavery. Georgia is the only one of the thirteen colonies to ban it by law.

<short pause> Why? Oglethorpe and the trustees want small farmers who work their own land. They want settlers who can fight, to protect South Carolina from the Spanish in Florida. And they worry that enslaved people would escape to Spanish Florida, which promised them freedom.

## Part 2E

And the Spanish do come. In seventeen forty-two, a Spanish army lands on St. Simons Island. Oglethorpe's soldiers ambush them in the marshes.

<gasp> The Spanish retreat. It's called the Battle of Bloody Marsh. Georgia survives.

## Part 2F

But next door, in South Carolina, planters are getting rich growing rice with enslaved labour. And Georgia's settlers are jealous.

They complain. They petition. They smuggle. They call themselves the Malcontents. <tsk> For years.

<long pause> And in seventeen fifty-one, the trustees give up. Slavery becomes legal in Georgia.

Remember that. A colony built to give poor people a second chance... just chose slavery.

## Part 3: Colony and Revolution

Georgia becomes a royal colony. It grows rice and indigo on the coast, worked by thousands of enslaved Africans.

<short pause> On the isolated sea islands, like Sapelo, their descendants keep African words, songs, foods and crafts alive for centuries. The Gullah Geechee. Some families on Sapelo still live on land their ancestors farmed after freedom.

In the Revolution, the British capture Savannah in seventeen seventy-eight, and hold it for years.

In seventeen seventy-nine, the Americans and the French try to take it back. <gasp> Among the French troops are hundreds of free Black volunteers from Saint-Domingue, in the Caribbean. One of them is a young drummer boy named Henri Christophe. He'll later become king of an independent Haiti.

The attack fails. But a monument to the Haitian soldiers stands in Savannah today.

In seventeen eighty-eight, Georgia becomes the fourth state.

<chuckle> One of the men who signed the Declaration of Independence for Georgia, Button Gwinnett, is killed in a duel the next year. He signed so few documents before he died that today his autograph is one of the rarest in America. A single signature has sold for hundreds of thousands of dollars.

## Part 3B

<chuckle> And then Georgia's lawmakers do something spectacularly corrupt.

In seventeen ninety-five, they sell thirty-five million acres of western land, most of today's Alabama and Mississippi, to land companies for about a penny and a half an acre. <short pause> Almost every lawmaker who votes yes has been bribed.

Voters are furious. They throw the lawmakers out, and the next legislature publicly burns the law, using a magnifying glass and the sun, "fire from heaven". The Yazoo Land Fraud.

## Part 3C

Then, in seventeen ninety-three, on a plantation near Savannah, a young man from Connecticut named Eli Whitney builds a machine that pulls the sticky seeds out of cotton. Fast.

<gasp> The cotton gin.

Before, cleaning seeds out of a pound of cotton took a person a whole day. Now, one machine can clean fifty pounds a day.

<sigh> Whitney patents it. But the machine is so simple that planters just build their own copies. He spends years in court, and makes very little money.

Suddenly, cotton is hugely profitable. Planters want more land, and more enslaved workers, to grow it. Cotton spreads across Georgia, and the whole South.

<long pause> By eighteen sixty, more than four hundred and sixty thousand people in Georgia are enslaved. Almost half of everyone in the state.

## Part 3D

And the land they want belongs to the Cherokee.

The Cherokee have done everything the government told them to do. They have farms, mills and roads. A written constitution, and a capital at New Echota.

A Cherokee man named Sequoyah, who can't read or write any language, invents a whole writing system for Cherokee, one symbol for each sound. <gasp> Within a few years, most Cherokee can read and write in their own language. They print their own newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix.

<short pause> Their elected principal chief, John Ross, is only one-eighth Cherokee by ancestry, but he fights for his nation, in Washington and in the courts, for nearly forty years.

## Part 3E

Then, around eighteen twenty-nine, gold is found near Dahlonega. One of America's first gold rushes. Thousands of miners pour into Cherokee land. <chuckle> The government even builds a mint there, to turn the gold into coins.

Georgia holds a lottery, and gives away Cherokee land to white settlers. Before the Cherokee have even left.

The Cherokee fight Georgia all the way to the Supreme Court. And in eighteen thirty-two, the court rules on their side.

<sigh> It doesn't matter. Georgia ignores the ruling. And the president, Andrew Jackson, does nothing to enforce it.

## Part 3F (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-five, a small group of Cherokee, without the nation's approval, sign a treaty giving away their land.

<breath> In eighteen thirty-eight, soldiers force the Cherokee out of their homes at gunpoint and hold them in camps.

Then they are marched west, more than a thousand miles, to what is now Oklahoma.

<long pause> Thousands die on the way. The Cherokee call it "the trail where they cried". We call it the Trail of Tears.

## Part 4: Civil War

In eighteen sixty-one, Georgia leaves the Union to protect slavery.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

Near the town of Andersonville, the Confederacy builds a prison camp for captured Union soldiers. It's overcrowded, with almost no shelter, and a filthy stream for water.

<long pause> Nearly thirteen thousand prisoners die there. After the war, its commander is executed for war crimes.

## Part 4C

In eighteen sixty-four, Union General William Tecumseh Sherman captures Atlanta, the South's railroad hub. When his army leaves, much of the city burns.

Then he marches sixty thousand soldiers across Georgia to the sea, destroying railroads, farms and supplies on the way. <chuckle> His soldiers heat railroad rails over bonfires and wrap them around trees. People call them Sherman's neckties.

Thousands of freed people follow his army.

In December, he captures Savannah, and sends President Lincoln a telegram offering him the city as a Christmas present.

<short pause> Savannah, the city Oglethorpe built, survives.

<short pause> So far: a colony of second chances that chose slavery. A machine that made slavery richer. A nation forced out at gunpoint. And a burned city.

<breath> Now watch what Georgia does next.

## Part 4D

And in January eighteen sixty-five, Sherman issues an order. Land along the coast of Georgia and South Carolina, taken from planters, will be divided among freed families. Forty acres each. Some get army mules too.

<short pause> Forty acres and a mule.

<sigh> A few months later, Lincoln is dead. The new president, Andrew Johnson, gives the land back to the planters. Most freed families lose it.

## Part 5: Atlanta Rises

Atlanta rebuilds so fast it takes a phoenix as its symbol. Its motto is one Latin word. "Resurgens". Rising again.

In eighteen eighty-six, an Atlanta pharmacist named John Pemberton invents a syrup he sells as a "brain tonic". At first, it even contains a little cocaine, from the coca leaf. That's where the name comes from.

<chuckle> Coca-Cola. He sells it at a soda fountain for five cents a glass. In the first year, he sells about nine glasses a day.

Then a businessman named Asa Candler buys the company, and gives out coupons for free Coke. Everywhere. Within a few decades, it's the most famous drink in the world.

<chuckle> Today, Coca-Cola keeps its secret recipe in a vault, in a museum in Atlanta. Visitors line up to look at the door.

<short pause> In nineteen twelve, in Savannah, a woman named Juliette Gordon Low gathers eighteen girls together for hikes, camping and first aid. <chuckle> She's nearly deaf, and full of energy. It's the start of the Girl Scouts of America.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

But after the war comes Jim Crow. Segregation. Black Georgians are blocked from voting, and many face terrible violence.

<long pause> In nineteen fifteen, on top of Stone Mountain, near Atlanta, a group of men relight a cross, and restart the Ku Klux Klan.

<breath> Nine years earlier, in nineteen-oh-six, after newspapers print false stories about attacks on white women, white mobs rampage through Atlanta for days, attacking Black people in the streets. <long pause> Dozens are killed.

## Part 5C

And in nineteen twenty-nine, in Atlanta, a baby is born in a house on Auburn Avenue. His father is the preacher at Ebenezer Baptist Church.

<short pause> Martin Luther King Jr.

He grows up to lead the civil rights movement, with marches, boycotts, and a dream. He wins the Nobel Peace Prize at thirty-five. And Atlanta becomes the movement's headquarters.

<short pause> Atlanta is also home to the biggest group of historically Black colleges in America. Morehouse, where King studied. Spelman. Clark Atlanta. For more than a century, they train Black doctors, lawyers, teachers and leaders.

<short pause> Atlanta calls itself "the city too busy to hate". In nineteen seventy-three, it elects its first Black mayor, Maynard Jackson. Atlanta's giant airport is named after him, and another mayor.

## Part 5D

In nineteen sixty-one, in Albany, Georgia, hundreds of Black citizens march and go to jail, over and over, protesting segregation. Their freedom songs ring out from the jail cells. <short pause> They don't win right away. But the movement learns from Albany, and wins elsewhere.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

In April nineteen sixty-eight, Martin Luther King Jr. is killed in Memphis.

<breath> His body is brought home to Atlanta. His coffin is carried through the streets on a simple farm wagon, pulled by two mules. <long pause> More than a hundred thousand people walk behind it.

## Part 6: Today

Then Georgia keeps surprising everyone.

In nineteen seventy-four, in Atlanta, Hank Aaron breaks Babe Ruth's home run record. <sigh> He's sent piles of hate mail and death threats on the way. He keeps hitting.

<short pause> And Ray Charles, born in Albany, Georgia, records a song about his home state. In nineteen seventy-nine, Georgia makes "Georgia on My Mind" its official state song. <short pause> Ray Charles sings it to the legislature.

<chuckle> Every April, in Augusta, the best golfers in the world play the Masters, and the winner gets a green jacket.

In nineteen seventy-seven, a peanut farmer from the tiny town of Plains, Jimmy Carter, becomes President of the United States.

<chuckle> Georgia grows nearly half of all the peanuts in America. So the peanut-farmer president fits right in. Later, he wins the Nobel Peace Prize, and lives to a hundred.

## Part 6B

In nineteen ninety-six, Atlanta hosts the Olympics.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

<breath> During the games, a pipe bomb explodes in the crowd at Centennial Olympic Park. Two people die. Over a hundred are hurt. <short pause> The games go on.

## Part 6D

The CDC, which fights diseases around the world, is headquartered in Atlanta. So is Delta. So is Home Depot. So is CNN.

<chuckle> Georgia makes so many movies and TV shows that people call it "Y'allywood". Superheroes save the world on Georgia streets every week.

<chuckle> And Atlanta becomes one of the capitals of hip hop. OutKast. Ludacris. Whole new sounds born in Atlanta studios.

And there's a law about onions. A sweet onion can only be called a Vidalia onion if it's grown in certain counties of Georgia.

## Part 6E

So how does the colony that banned slavery become one of the biggest slave states in the country?

<short pause> It starts with a man whose friend died in a debtors' prison.

Oglethorpe wanted a colony of small, free farmers. No slavery. But his settlers looked across the river at South Carolina's wealth, and fought until they got it. Then the cotton gin made slavery the engine of Georgia.

<long pause> And a century later, the grandchildren of the enslaved led the movement that tore down segregation. From Atlanta.

The colony that was meant to give people a second chance... finally did.

<chuckle> And it took a soft drink and a peanut farmer to make the rest of the world notice.
