# The Entire History of Washington State

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,550 spoken words, so roughly 14 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And during World War Two"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller by the sea; thunderous for the Ice Age floods. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Fast, greedy, excited gold-rush energy. |
| Part 4 | Building, industrial, then serious. |
| Part 5 | Bright and swinging, then grave. |
| Part 6 | Modern, confident, a little rebellious; calm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 15,000 BC to 1792 · Fur and Forts 1792 to 1853 · Territory 1853 to 1889 · Gold Rush 1897 to 1910 · Planes and Dams 1916 to 1945 · Jet Age 1945 to 1980 · Tech 1980 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Starbucks? <short pause> Started in Seattle. Amazon? Started in a garage near Seattle. Costco, Boeing, and Microsoft's headquarters? All Washington.

<chuckle> The United States and Britain nearly went to war here... over a pig.

In nineteen eighty, a volcano in Washington blew more than thirteen hundred feet off its own top.

<long pause> And during World War Two, Washington secretly made the plutonium for the atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki.

<short pause> So how does a rainy logging town at the edge of the map become home to some of the biggest companies on Earth?

<whispers> It starts with a ship carrying a ton of gold.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Fifteen thousand years ago, at the end of the Ice Age, a giant lake in what's now Montana is held back by a wall of ice.

<gasp> Then the ice dam breaks.

A wall of water, hundreds of feet high, roars across eastern Washington at highway speeds. It strips the soil down to bare rock and carves giant canyons in days. Then the ice dam forms again. And breaks again. Dozens of times.

<short pause> Scientists call them the Ice Age Floods. The land they scoured is still there. The Channeled Scablands. <chuckle> For decades, the geologist who first figured it out was laughed at. Then, finally, everyone agreed he was right.

<short pause> People were here to see it. In nineteen ninety-six, two college students wading in the Columbia River at Kennewick find a human skull. <gasp> The man it belonged to lived about nine thousand years ago. For twenty years, scientists and tribes fight in court over him. DNA finally shows he's related to Native Americans today. In twenty seventeen, he's reburied by five tribes, in a secret place.

## Part 1B

For thousands of years, the coast of Washington is one of the richest places in North America. Not in gold. <short pause> In salmon.

Every year, millions of salmon swim up the rivers. The Coast Salish peoples, the Makah, the Quinault and many others, build cedar plank houses big enough for several families, and giant canoes carved from a single cedar tree.

They hold potlatches, great feasts where a chief shows his wealth... by giving it away.

The Makah paddle out into the open ocean and hunt whales by hand, with harpoons.

<short pause> In nineteen ninety-nine, after seventy years without a hunt, Makah whalers paddle out again in a cedar canoe, and take a grey whale, the old way. Protesters are furious. The Makah say it's their treaty right, and their culture. The argument goes on for years.

## Part 1C

On the dry side of the mountains, the Yakama, the Nez Perce, the Spokane and the Colville peoples fish the Columbia River. At Celilo Falls and Kettle Falls, people come together to fish and trade, for thousands of years.

About three hundred years ago, a mudslide buries part of a Makah village at Ozette. In the nineteen seventies, a storm uncovers it.

<gasp> Inside are houses, baskets, harpoons, carved boxes, even whale bones. Thousands of objects, perfectly preserved. A Native American Pompeii.

## Part 2: Fur and Forts

In seventeen ninety-two, two ships arrive. An American captain, Robert Gray, sails into a huge river and names it after his ship, the Columbia. A British captain, George Vancouver, maps the great inland sea and names it after one of his officers. <short pause> Puget Sound.

In eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark paddle down the Columbia to the Pacific.

For decades, Britain and America both claim the land. The British Hudson's Bay Company runs a giant fur fort on the Columbia, at Fort Vancouver.

<short pause> Its chief, a giant of a man with white hair named Dr. John McLoughlin, is supposed to keep Americans out. <chuckle> Instead, when exhausted American families stumble off the Oregon Trail, he gives them food, seed and tools on credit. His bosses in London are not pleased.

Then American settlers start arriving on the Oregon Trail. In eighteen forty-six, the two countries split it, along the forty-ninth parallel.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

Missionaries come too. In eighteen thirty-six, Marcus and Narcissa Whitman build a mission among the Cayuse people.

<breath> Then measles, brought by wagon trains, sweeps through the Cayuse. Half of them die. White children, treated by Dr. Whitman, mostly recover.

<long pause> In November eighteen forty-seven, some Cayuse men, believing the missionaries are killing their people, attack the mission. The Whitmans and eleven others are killed.

## Part 2C

In eighteen fifty-one, a small group of settlers lands on a rainy beach on Puget Sound.

They name their town after a local leader who is friendly to them. Chief Si'ahl, of the Duwamish and Suquamish.

<short pause> Seattle.

<short pause> Chief Si'ahl is said to have given a famous speech about the land and the dead. <tsk> But the most famous version was written down thirty years later, from memory, by a settler. And a much later version, quoted on posters all over the world, was written by a screenwriter in the nineteen-seventies. <short pause> Nobody knows exactly what he really said.

And it is, at first, mostly mud and trees. Its first big business is a sawmill. Logs are skidded down a steep hill to the mill. The road is called Skid Road. <chuckle> That's where the phrase "skid row" comes from.

## Part 2D

Seattle has a problem. Lots of lonely men. Very few women.

<chuckle> So in eighteen sixty-four, a young man named Asa Mercer sails east, and comes back with a group of young women from New England, to work as teachers and seamstresses. And to marry. The Mercer Girls. Almost all of them find husbands.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

In eighteen fifty-four and fifty-five, the territory's governor pressures tribes into treaties giving up most of their land. Many leaders feel cheated.

<breath> War breaks out. When it's over, the Nisqually leader Leschi is hanged.

In two thousand four, a special historical court ruled that his hanging was wrong.

## Part 2F

And then there's the pig.

In eighteen fifty-nine, on San Juan Island, which both Britain and America claim, an American farmer finds a pig eating his potatoes. It belongs to a British company. <short pause> He shoots it.

<chuckle> Things escalate. Fast. Soon, hundreds of American soldiers face British warships with more than two thousand men.

Nobody fires. The two sides share the island for twelve years, while the German Emperor decides who gets it. In the end, America does.

<laugh> The only casualty of the Pig War... was the pig.

<short pause> So far: a coast rich in salmon, a city named after a chief, a war over a pig. But Washington is still a muddy corner of the map.

<breath> That's about to change. Fast.

## Part 3: Territory

In eighteen fifty-three, it becomes Washington Territory. Congress wanted to call it Columbia. But they worried people would confuse it with the District of Columbia. <short pause> So they named it Washington instead.

<laugh> Now people confuse it with Washington, D.C.

In November eighteen eighty-nine, Washington becomes the forty-second state.

That same year, in June, a glue pot boils over in a Seattle carpenter's shop. Fire spreads through the wooden town. Most of downtown burns.

Seattle rebuilds in brick and stone. And it raises the streets a storey higher. <chuckle> The old shopfronts at the bottom are left underground. You can still tour them today.

## Part 3B

But Seattle is still a small, struggling town.

And then, on July seventeenth, eighteen ninety-seven, a steamship called the Portland sails into Seattle's harbour from Alaska.

A newspaper reporter has rushed out on a tugboat to meet it, and wires back the news. <gasp> "A ton of gold is coming to Seattle."

<chuckle> Actually, it's closer to two tons.

The Klondike Gold Rush begins.

## Part 3C

The gold is far away, in the Yukon, in Canada. To get in, Canada says every miner needs to bring about a year's worth of supplies. Roughly a ton of gear.

And Seattle realises... <short pause> it doesn't need to find the gold. It just needs to sell the supplies.

Seattle tells the world it's the gateway to the gold fields. Shops pile flour, boots, tents and stoves on the sidewalks. <laugh> Even Seattle's mayor resigns by telegram, and heads north.

Most miners come home with nothing. But by the following spring, Seattle's merchants have sold millions of dollars' worth of goods. More, by one count, than the gold dug out of the Klondike in the same time.

A Swedish miner named John Nordstrom comes back from the Klondike and opens a shoe store in Seattle.

<short pause> Seattle never found the gold. It sold the shovels.

<short pause> In nineteen-oh-seven, Seattle opens a farmers' market on the waterfront, Pike Place Market. <chuckle> Today, its fishmongers throw whole salmon to each other across the counter, while tourists cheer.

## Part 3D

And in nineteen-oh-seven, two Seattle teenagers borrow a hundred dollars and start a bicycle messenger service. Delivering notes, packages, even hot meals, around the city.

<chuckle> It becomes the United Parcel Service. UPS.

## Part 4: Planes and Dams

In nineteen sixteen, a rich timber businessman named William Boeing starts building seaplanes in a red barn on a Seattle river.

Boeing becomes one of the biggest airplane makers in the world.

<short pause> And the forests. In nineteen hundred, a lumberman named Frederick Weyerhaeuser buys almost a million acres of Washington forest in a single deal. Loggers cut trees so big that it takes days to bring one down. <short pause> East of the mountains, irrigation turns desert into orchards. Today, most of the apples grown in America come from Washington.

<short pause> In nineteen nineteen, sixty thousand Seattle workers walk off the job at once. The Seattle General Strike. The first city-wide general strike in America. For five days, the city shuts down. <chuckle> The strikers run their own milk stations and kitchens to keep people fed.

## Part 4B

In the nineteen thirties, workers build the Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia. One of the largest concrete structures in the world.

<chuckle> The government even hires a young folk singer named Woody Guthrie to write songs about it. In one month, he writes twenty-six. Including "Roll On, Columbia".

Its electricity powers farms, factories... and something secret.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In November nineteen forty, the brand new Tacoma Narrows Bridge starts twisting in the wind. Up and down. Side to side. People nickname it Galloping Gertie.

<breath> One windy morning, it twists so hard that it rips itself apart and falls into the water. <short pause> No person dies. But a dog, left in a car on the bridge, is lost.

Engineers around the world study the film of it to this day.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In nineteen forty-two, Japanese American families are forced from their homes into camps. The very first to be removed, anywhere in the country, are the families of Bainbridge Island, near Seattle.

## Part 4E

In nineteen forty-three, the government takes over a quiet farming area on the Columbia River called Hanford.

Tens of thousands of workers arrive. They build nuclear reactors, using the river's cold water and the dams' power.

<whispers> They make plutonium. The plutonium inside the bomb dropped on Nagasaki came from Hanford.

<short pause> Its first reactor, the B Reactor, is the first full-scale nuclear reactor in the world. Built in about a year.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

Hanford keeps making plutonium for decades. It leaves behind enormous amounts of radioactive waste, much of it stored in old underground tanks.

<sigh> Cleaning it up is still going on. It's expected to take decades more.

## Part 5: Jet Age

After the war, Boeing builds jets. The seven-oh-seven. Then the giant seven-four-seven, built in Everett, in a factory so big it's one of the largest buildings in the world. <chuckle> It's so huge that clouds once formed inside it, near the ceiling.

In nineteen sixty-two, Seattle holds a World's Fair, and builds a tower with a flying saucer on top. <short pause> The Space Needle.

And a Seattle teenager named Jimi Hendrix learns to play guitar left-handed, on a right-handed guitar turned upside down.

<sigh> Then, around nineteen seventy, Boeing runs into trouble, and lays off tens of thousands of workers. Two men put up a billboard near the airport: "Will the last person leaving Seattle... turn out the lights." <chuckle> Seattle bounces back.

<chuckle> In nineteen seventy-four, Spokane holds its own World's Fair. At the time, it's the smallest city ever to host one.

## Part 5B

<whispers> In November nineteen seventy-one, a man in a suit buys a plane ticket in Portland, under the name Dan Cooper. On the flight to Seattle, he hands a flight attendant a note. He has a bomb.

In Seattle, he swaps the passengers for two hundred thousand dollars and four parachutes. Then he orders the plane back into the sky.

<gasp> Somewhere over southwest Washington, in the dark and rain, he opens the back door, and jumps.

<short pause> He's never found. Years later, a boy digging on a Columbia River beach finds a few thousand dollars of the ransom money. <chuckle> The rest has never turned up.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

On May eighteenth, nineteen eighty, Mount St. Helens explodes.

<breath> The whole north side of the mountain collapses in the largest landslide ever recorded. The blast flattens forests for miles. Ash falls across eleven states.

<long pause> Fifty-seven people are killed.

## Part 6: Tech

And then, Seattle goes back to selling.

In nineteen seventy-one, three friends open a coffee-bean shop near Pike Place Market. Starbucks.

In nineteen seventy-nine, two young men who grew up in Seattle, Bill Gates and Paul Allen, move their small software company back home. Microsoft.

In nineteen eighty-three, a warehouse store opens in Seattle. Costco.

<gasp> And in nineteen ninety-four, a man named Jeff Bezos starts selling books from a garage in Bellevue. Amazon. <chuckle> When an order comes in, a bell rings. Within weeks, it's ringing so often they have to turn it off.

<short pause> Bill Gates becomes, for years, the richest person on Earth. Then he and his wife start giving much of it away, to fight disease around the world.

## Part 6B

In the nineties, Seattle also gives the world grunge. Flannel shirts. Loud guitars. Nirvana and Pearl Jam.

<short pause> In nineteen ninety-nine, tens of thousands of protesters fill Seattle's streets during a world trade meeting. The police use tear gas. The meeting collapses. People still call it the Battle in Seattle.

And in twenty twelve, Washington becomes one of the first two states to legalise marijuana. Two years later, the Seahawks win the Super Bowl.

<laugh> And in nineteen sixty-nine, Skamania County passes a law making it illegal to kill a Sasquatch. Bigfoot. Just in case.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In January twenty twenty, a man near Seattle, just back from China, becomes the first confirmed case of COVID-nineteen in the United States.

<breath> Weeks later, an outbreak at a nursing home near Seattle becomes one of the first warnings to America of what was coming.

## Part 6D

So how does a rainy logging town at the edge of the map become home to some of the biggest companies on Earth?

<short pause> It starts with a ship carrying a ton of gold.

When the gold came, Seattle didn't dig. It sold. Boots, tents, flour, stoves, to anyone heading north. The city learned to get rich by supplying everyone else. Then delivering it.

A century later, it's still doing it. Coffee, software, airplanes, and anything you can put in a cardboard box.

<long pause> Seattle never found the gold.

<chuckle> It just figured out how to deliver it. Probably by tomorrow.
