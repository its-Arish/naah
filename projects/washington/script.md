## Hook

Starbucks? <short pause> Started in Seattle. Amazon? Started in a garage near Seattle. Costco, Boeing, and Microsoft's headquarters? All Washington.

The United States and Britain nearly went to war here... over a pig.

In nineteen eighty, a volcano in Washington blew more than thirteen hundred feet off its own top.

<long pause> And during World War Two, Washington secretly made the plutonium for the atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki.

<short pause> So how does a rainy logging town at the edge of the map become home to some of the biggest companies on Earth?

<whispers> It starts with a ship carrying a ton of gold.

## Part 1: First Peoples

For thousands of years, the coast of Washington is one of the richest places in North America. Not in gold. In salmon.

Every year, millions of salmon swim up the rivers. The Coast Salish peoples, the Makah, the Quinault and many others, build cedar plank houses and giant cedar canoes.

The Makah paddle out into the open ocean and hunt whales by hand.

On the dry side of the mountains, the Yakama, the Nez Perce and the Spokane fish the Columbia River at great falls, where people come together to trade for thousands of years.

## Part 1B

About three hundred years ago, a mudslide buries part of a Makah village at Ozette. In the nineteen seventies, a storm uncovers it.

Inside are houses, baskets, harpoons, even whale bones. Thousands of objects, perfectly preserved. A Native American Pompeii.

## Part 2: Fur and Forts

In seventeen ninety-two, two ships arrive. An American captain, Robert Gray, sails into a huge river and names it after his ship, the Columbia. A British captain, George Vancouver, maps Puget Sound and names it after one of his officers.

In eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark paddle down the Columbia to the Pacific.

For decades, Britain and America both claim the land. The British Hudson's Bay Company runs a giant fur fort on the Columbia.

Then American settlers start arriving on the Oregon Trail. In eighteen forty-six, the two countries split it, along the forty-ninth parallel.

## Part 2B

In eighteen fifty-one, a small group of settlers lands on a rainy beach on Puget Sound.

They name their town after a local leader who is friendly to them. Chief Si'ahl, of the Duwamish and Suquamish.

Seattle.

And it is, at first, mostly mud and trees. Its first big business is a sawmill. Logs are skidded down a steep hill to the mill. The road is called Skid Road. That's where the phrase "skid row" comes from.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In eighteen fifty-four and fifty-five, the territory's governor pressures tribes into treaties giving up most of their land. Many leaders feel cheated.

War breaks out. When it's over, the Nisqually leader Leschi is hanged.

In two thousand four, a special historical court ruled that his hanging was wrong.

## Part 2D

And then there's the pig.

In eighteen fifty-nine, on San Juan Island, which both Britain and America claim, an American farmer finds a pig eating his potatoes. It belongs to a British company. He shoots it.

<chuckle> Things escalate. Fast. Soon, hundreds of American soldiers face British warships with more than two thousand men.

Nobody fires. The two sides share the island for twelve years, while the German Emperor decides who gets it. In the end, America does.

The only casualty of the Pig War... was the pig.

## Part 3: Territory

In eighteen fifty-three, it becomes Washington Territory. Congress wanted to call it Columbia. But they worried people would confuse it with the District of Columbia. So they named it Washington instead.

<chuckle> Now people confuse it with Washington, D.C.

In November eighteen eighty-nine, Washington becomes the forty-second state.

That same year, in June, a glue pot boils over in a Seattle carpenter's shop. Fire spreads through the wooden town. Most of downtown burns.

Seattle rebuilds in brick and stone. And it raises the streets a storey higher. The old shopfronts at the bottom are left underground. You can still tour them today.

## Part 3B

But Seattle is still a small, struggling town.

And then, on July seventeenth, eighteen ninety-seven, a steamship called the Portland sails into Seattle's harbour from Alaska.

A newspaper reporter has rushed out on a tugboat to meet it, and wires back the news. "A ton of gold is coming to Seattle."

Actually, it's closer to two tons.

The Klondike Gold Rush begins.

## Part 3C

The gold is far away, in the Yukon, in Canada. To get in, Canada says every miner needs to bring about a year's worth of supplies. Roughly a ton of gear.

And Seattle realises... it doesn't need to find the gold. It just needs to sell the supplies.

Seattle tells the world it's the gateway to the gold fields. Shops pile flour, boots, tents and stoves on the sidewalks. Even Seattle's mayor resigns by telegram and heads north.

Most miners come home with nothing. But by the following spring, Seattle's merchants have sold millions of dollars' worth of goods. More, by one count, than the gold dug out of the Klondike in the same time.

A Swedish miner named John Nordstrom comes back from the Klondike and opens a shoe store in Seattle.

Seattle never found the gold. It sold the shovels.

## Part 4: Planes and Dams

In nineteen sixteen, a rich timber businessman named William Boeing starts building seaplanes in a red barn on a Seattle river.

Boeing becomes one of the biggest airplane makers in the world.

In the nineteen thirties, workers build the Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia. One of the largest concrete structures in the world. Its electricity powers farms, factories... and something secret.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In nineteen forty-two, Japanese American families are forced from their homes into camps. The very first to be removed, anywhere in the country, are the families of Bainbridge Island, near Seattle.

## Part 4C

In nineteen forty-three, the government takes over a quiet farming area on the Columbia River called Hanford.

Tens of thousands of workers arrive. They build nuclear reactors, using the river's cold water and the dams' power.

They make plutonium. The plutonium inside the bomb dropped on Nagasaki came from Hanford.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

Hanford keeps making plutonium for decades. It leaves behind enormous amounts of radioactive waste, much of it stored in old underground tanks.

Cleaning it up is still going on. It's expected to take decades more.

## Part 5: Jet Age

After the war, Boeing builds jets. The seven-oh-seven. Then the giant seven-four-seven. The world starts flying.

In nineteen sixty-two, Seattle holds a World's Fair and builds a tower with a flying saucer on top. The Space Needle.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

On May eighteenth, nineteen eighty, Mount St. Helens explodes.

The whole north side of the mountain collapses in the largest landslide ever recorded. The blast flattens forests for miles. Ash falls across eleven states.

Fifty-seven people are killed.

## Part 5C

And then, Seattle goes back to selling.

In nineteen seventy-one, three friends open a coffee-bean shop near Pike Place Market. Starbucks.

In nineteen seventy-nine, two young men who grew up in Seattle, Bill Gates and Paul Allen, move their small software company back home. Microsoft.

In nineteen eighty-three, a warehouse store opens in Seattle. Costco.

And in nineteen ninety-four, a man named Jeff Bezos starts selling books from a garage in Bellevue. Amazon.

In the nineties, Seattle also gives the world grunge music. Nirvana and Pearl Jam.

## Part 5D

So how does a rainy logging town at the edge of the map become home to some of the biggest companies on Earth?

It starts with a ship carrying a ton of gold.

When the gold came, Seattle didn't dig. It sold. Boots, tents, flour, stoves, to anyone heading north. The city learned to get rich by supplying everyone else.

A century later, it's still doing it. Coffee, software, airplanes, and anything you can put in a cardboard box.

<short pause> Seattle never found the gold.

<chuckle> It just figured out how to deliver it. Probably by tomorrow.
