# The Entire History of Oregon

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,350 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Playful and nostalgic for the first four facts; slower and serious from "But when Oregon"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, ancient storyteller, with a little volcanic drama. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous and a little cheeky. |
| Part 3 | Weary, brave pioneer voice, then serious. |
| Part 4 | Honest and steady. |
| Part 5 | Rugged, then surprised. |
| Part 6 | Quirky, inventive and upbeat; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 14,000 BC to 1792 · Beaver Country 1792 to 1840 · The Trail 1840 to 1859 · Statehood 1859 to 1900 · Timber 1900 to 1945 · Green and Weird 1945 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

"You have died of dysentery." <short pause> If you ever played The Oregon Trail, you know that line.

<chuckle> Nike? Started in Oregon. With a waffle iron.

The city of Portland got its name from a coin toss. And Oregon's flag is the only state flag with a different picture on each side.

<long pause> But when Oregon became a state, its constitution banned Black people from moving there.

<short pause> So why would hundreds of thousands of people walk two thousand miles, across deserts and mountains, to get to Oregon?

<whispers> It starts with a hat.

## Part 1: First Peoples

People have lived in Oregon for at least fourteen thousand years. <gasp> In a cave in the high desert, archaeologists have found some of the oldest shoes in the world. Sandals woven from sagebrush bark, around nine or ten thousand years old.

<chuckle> Oregon was making shoes long before Nike.

## Part 1B

About seventy-seven hundred years ago, a huge volcano in southern Oregon, Mount Mazama, explodes. <gasp> It blasts so much ash into the sky that it falls across eight states and into Canada. Some of those sandals were found buried under it.

The top of the mountain collapses into itself, leaving a giant bowl. Over centuries, it fills with rain and snow.

Crater Lake. Almost two thousand feet deep. The deepest lake in the United States, and one of the bluest on Earth.

<short pause> The Klamath people tell of a battle between the spirit of the underworld and the spirit of the sky, that ended with the mountain falling. <whispers> Some think the story is a memory of the eruption itself, passed down for thousands of years.

## Part 1C

On the Columbia River, at Celilo Falls, salmon leap up the rapids by the million. People fish from wooden platforms built over the roaring water, with long dip nets.

For thousands of years, it's one of the greatest trading places in North America. <short pause> Nations come from hundreds of miles away. They trade so much that they invent a shared trade language, Chinook Jargon. Some of its words are still used in the Northwest. <chuckle> A "skookum" thing is big and strong.

On the coast live the Chinook, the Tillamook, the Coos and many others, in big cedar plank houses. Inland, the Klamath, the Paiute, the Nez Perce and more.

## Part 2: Beaver Country

Now, the hat.

In the seventeen and eighteen hundreds, every fashionable man in Europe wants one thing. <chuckle> A tall felt hat. And the best felt in the world is made from beaver fur.

Europe's beavers have almost all been hunted out. But North America has millions.

And in the Pacific Northwest, there are beavers, and sea otters with the softest fur on Earth. <short pause> And nobody from Europe has claimed the land for sure.

In seventeen ninety-two, an American trading captain, Robert Gray, sails into the mouth of a giant river, and names it after his ship. The Columbia.

## Part 2B

In eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark reach the mouth of the Columbia. "Ocean in view! O, the joy!" Clark writes. <chuckle> He's actually looking at the river's wide mouth. The ocean is still about twenty miles away.

They have to decide where to spend the winter. So the whole group votes. <short pause> Including York, an enslaved Black man, and Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman. It's one of the first recorded votes in American history that includes a Black man and a woman.

They build a little fort on the coast. <groan> It rains almost every day. Their clothes rot. Christmas dinner is spoiled elk.

<short pause> A few men set up camp on the beach, and boil seawater in big kettles, day and night, for weeks. <chuckle> They make about three and a half bushels of salt, to take home. You can still visit the spot.

## Part 2C

Then, in eighteen eleven, a New York fur merchant named John Jacob Astor sends men to build a trading post at the mouth of the Columbia. Astoria. The first American settlement on the Pacific coast.

<tsk> His business fails. But in eighteen twelve, a group of his fur traders, heading back east, stumbles onto something precious.

A wide, gentle pass through the Rocky Mountains. South Pass. <gasp> A way wagons could cross.

They don't realise how important it is. Not yet.

## Part 2D

For the next thirty years, Britain's Hudson's Bay Company runs the fur trade from a giant fort on the Columbia, Fort Vancouver. Its boss, a huge white-haired Canadian named John McLoughlin, rules the region like a king.

Mountain men trap beavers in every stream.

<short pause> Then, around eighteen-forty, fashion changes. Men in Europe start wearing silk hats. The beaver hat goes out of style.

<chuckle> The fur trade collapses. But the trails the trappers found are still there.

## Part 3: The Trail

And Americans in the East are hearing wonderful stories about Oregon. Rich soil. Mild winters. Free land. <chuckle> Some promoters even claim the pigs there run around already cooked, with knives and forks sticking out of them.

In eighteen forty-three, around nine hundred people gather in Missouri with covered wagons and head west, through South Pass. It's the first big wagon train on the Oregon Trail.

<gasp> Over the next twenty-five years, hundreds of thousands follow on the trails west.

The trip is about two thousand miles. It takes four to six months. <breath> Most people walk the whole way, beside their wagons, to save the oxen.

A covered wagon is only about four feet wide and ten feet long. <tsk> Everything a family owns has to fit in it. Flour, bacon, tools, a stove, seeds, a Bible. Grandma's furniture gets dumped by the side of the trail when the oxen get tired.

<short pause> Out in Wyoming, there's a giant granite rock called Independence Rock. The rule is: get there by the Fourth of July, or you'll hit snow in the mountains. Thousands of travellers carve or paint their names on it. <chuckle> Many are still there.

## Part 3B

At the end, there's one last, terrible obstacle. The Cascade Mountains, and the wild Columbia River.

At first, families have to take their wagons apart, put them on rafts, and float down the rapids of the Columbia. <groan> Many are wrecked.

So in eighteen forty-six, a man named Sam Barlow builds a rough road around the side of Mount Hood. <chuckle> And he charges a toll. Five dollars a wagon.

When they get to the Willamette Valley, John McLoughlin, the British fur boss, does something his company doesn't like. He helps the starving, exhausted Americans, with food and seeds on credit. <short pause> Today, he's called the Father of Oregon.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

Thousands die on the trail. A few in accidents and river crossings. A few in conflicts.

<breath> But most die of disease. Cholera. And yes, dysentery.

Graves line the trail. On some stretches, one for every few hundred feet.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

The settlers also bring measles. In eighteen forty-seven, it sweeps through the Cayuse people, near a mission run by Marcus and Narcissa Whitman. Half of the Cayuse die.

<long pause> Many Cayuse blame the missionaries. A group of men attack the mission, and kill the Whitmans and eleven others.

<breath> A war follows. Five Cayuse men are later hanged.

## Part 3E

Britain and America both claim Oregon. <tsk> Some Americans even want the whole coast up to Alaska, and shout "Fifty-four forty or fight!"

But in eighteen forty-six, they split it peacefully, along the forty-ninth parallel. The line between the U.S. and Canada today.

And in eighteen fifty, Congress passes a law giving free land in Oregon. Three hundred and twenty acres for a single white man. Six hundred and forty for a married couple.

## Part 3F

In eighteen forty-five, two men own the land where a new town will grow. One is from Boston. The other is from Portland, Maine. Each wants to name it after his hometown.

So they flip a coin. Best two out of three.

<laugh> The man from Maine wins. Otherwise, it would be Boston, Oregon.

<chuckle> The town has so many tree stumps left in its muddy streets that people nickname it Stumptown.

## Part 4: Statehood

But the free land is only for white settlers. <sigh> And the new settlers want Oregon to be white too.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

When Oregon becomes a state, on February fourteenth, eighteen fifty-nine, it is a free state. No slavery.

<breath> But its constitution also bans Black people from moving there, living there, or owning property.

It's the only free state ever admitted to the Union with a ban like that. The words are only fully removed from the constitution in two thousand two.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

As settlers flood in, Native nations are pushed onto small reservations.

<breath> In the Rogue River Wars, many are killed. The survivors are marched and shipped far north, away from their homelands.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In the far northeast of Oregon lies the Wallowa Valley, the homeland of Chief Joseph's band of the Nez Perce.

<breath> In eighteen seventy-seven, the army orders them onto a small reservation in Idaho. Fighting breaks out. Joseph's people flee, more than a thousand miles, trying to reach Canada.

<long pause> They are caught just forty miles from the border. Starving and freezing, Joseph surrenders.

"From where the sun now stands," he is reported to have said, "I will fight no more forever."

## Part 4E

In nineteen-oh-two, Oregon becomes one of the first states to let ordinary voters write their own laws and vote on them directly. <short pause> People call it the "Oregon System". Other states copy it.

<sigh> In the nineteen-twenties, the Ku Klux Klan becomes powerful in Oregon. It helps pass a law forcing every child into public schools, aimed at Catholic schools. <short pause> The Supreme Court strikes the law down, in a case that still protects private schools across America.

And in nineteen twelve, after five failed tries, a fierce pioneer newspaper editor named Abigail Scott Duniway finally wins Oregon women the vote. <chuckle> She's so old and famous by then that the governor asks her to write and sign the proclamation herself.

## Part 5: Timber

For decades, Oregon's biggest business is timber. Giant Douglas firs, taller than a twenty-story building. Huge logging camps. Lumberjacks riding logs down rivers.

<short pause> In the nineteen-thirties, the government starts building giant dams on the Columbia River, for electricity. In nineteen forty-one, it hires a folk singer, Woody Guthrie, for one month, to write songs about them. <chuckle> He writes twenty-six. One of them, "Roll On, Columbia", becomes Washington's state song.

<gasp> During the Second World War, a Japanese submarine surfaces off the Oregon coast and launches a small seaplane. The pilot, Nobuo Fujita, drops firebombs on the forest near Brookings, hoping to start a giant wildfire.

<chuckle> The forest is too wet. The fires barely spread.

It's the only time an enemy aircraft has ever bombed the mainland United States. <short pause> Twenty years later, Fujita comes back to Brookings, to apologise. He gives the town his family's four-hundred-year-old samurai sword.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

Japan also sends thousands of balloon bombs, drifting on the winds across the Pacific.

<breath> In May nineteen forty-five, near Bly, Oregon, a pastor's wife and five children on a church picnic find one in the woods.

<long pause> It explodes. All six are killed. They are the only people killed by enemy action on the American mainland in the whole war.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

During the war, workers building ships move into a new town near Portland called Vanport. Many are Black families. <breath> In nineteen forty-eight, a dike breaks. The whole town floods in an afternoon.

At least fifteen people die, and thousands lose their homes.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-seven, a new dam floods Celilo Falls. The great fishing place that had fed people for thousands of years disappears under the water in a few hours.

## Part 6: Green and Weird

In nineteen seventy, a dead whale washes up on a beach in Florence. <chuckle> The highway department decides to blow it up. With half a ton of dynamite.

<laugh> Chunks of whale rain down on the crowd. One crushes a car. It's still one of the most famous news clips ever filmed.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-one, a man who calls himself Dan Cooper boards a plane in Portland, hijacks it with a bomb in his briefcase, collects two hundred thousand dollars, and jumps out the back with a parachute, into the dark. <whispers> He's never found.

## Part 6B

And then, the shoes.

A University of Oregon track coach named Bill Bowerman wants lighter shoes for his runners. One morning, he looks at his wife's waffle iron, and pours rubber into it. <groan> He ruins the waffle iron.

But he gets the waffle sole.

He and a former runner, Phil Knight, start a company. <chuckle> Knight sells shoes out of the trunk of his car at track meets. They name the company after the Greek goddess of victory.

Nike.

## Part 6C

Oregon keeps trying new things first. A deposit on bottles and cans, in nineteen seventy-one, so people bring them back instead of throwing them on the beach. Voting by mail, for every election. <chuckle> And there's no sales tax.

Its beaches belong to the public, every inch of them.

<laugh> In nineteen seventy-one, Oregon's governor, Tom McCall, tells the rest of America: "Come visit us again and again. But for heaven's sake, don't come here to live." <short pause> Oregon draws lines around its cities, to stop them sprawling over its farms and forests.

Today, the state builds computer chips, grows wine, and Portland is famous for coffee, bikes, food carts and being proudly weird.

<chuckle> And the old fur fort, Astoria, becomes a movie star. A whole gang of kids hunts for pirate treasure through its streets, in the nineteen eighty-five film The Goonies. Fans still turn up at the house from the movie.

## Part 6D

So why would hundreds of thousands of people walk two thousand miles, across deserts and mountains, to get to Oregon?

<whispers> It starts with a hat.

Europe wanted beaver hats. The hunt for beaver brought fur traders to the Pacific. One of them found the pass through the mountains. <short pause> And when the beaver hats went out of fashion, that pass became a road for families, chasing the promise of free land.

<breath> And that's why there's a beaver on the back of Oregon's flag.

<chuckle> One fashion trend. Two thousand miles. A whole state.
