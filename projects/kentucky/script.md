# The Entire History of Kentucky

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,400 spoken words, so roughly 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "During the Civil War"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, deep, cave-echo storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous frontier energy, a little rough. |
| Part 3 | Serious, torn and steady. |
| Part 4 | Tense and heavy, then wry. |
| Part 5 | Lively and horsey, then tough. |
| Part 6 | Proud, punchy and warm; calm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1769 · The Gap 1769 to 1792 · Bluegrass 1792 to 1860 · Divided 1861 to 1865 · Horses and Coal 1865 to 1945 · Champions 1945 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Kentucky Fried Chicken? <short pause> Started at a gas station in Kentucky. The tune of "Happy Birthday"? Written by two sisters in Kentucky.

<chuckle> There are more barrels of bourbon ageing in Kentucky than there are people.

And under Kentucky lies the longest known cave system on Earth.

<long pause> During the Civil War, Kentucky families sent their sons to fight and die on both sides.

And the two presidents who led that war, Abraham Lincoln and Jefferson Davis, were both born in Kentucky. Less than a year apart.

<short pause> So how does one state give birth to both sides of the Civil War?

<whispers> It starts with a gap in the mountains.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Under Kentucky, water has been eating through limestone for millions of years. Rain soaks into the ground, turns slightly acidic, and slowly, slowly dissolves the rock.

<gasp> It's made a maze of tunnels and caverns. Mammoth Cave. More than four hundred and twenty miles of mapped passages. And it keeps getting longer, as explorers squeeze into new ones.

People were exploring it with bundles of burning cane, four thousand years ago. They left behind their torches, their gourds, and their sandals, woven from plants. <short pause> Some of those sandals are still there, in the dark.

And long before that, giant animals came to Kentucky. <short pause> At a salty swamp called Big Bone Lick, mammoths and mastodons came to lick the salt. Some got stuck in the mud, and died there.

<gasp> Thousands of years later, President Thomas Jefferson sends William Clark, of Lewis and Clark, to dig up their bones. <chuckle> Jefferson lays them out on the floor of the White House to study them.

On the surface, the Shawnee, the Cherokee and other nations hunt in the forests and meadows, full of deer and buffalo. Few live there all year round. For many, Kentucky is a hunting ground. Shared, and fought over.

## Part 2: The Gap

To the east, the Appalachian Mountains stand like a wall between the English colonies and the West.

But there's a door. <short pause> A notch in the mountains, where Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee meet today. Buffalo used it for thousands of years. Then Native trails followed the buffalo.

The Cumberland Gap.

## Part 2B

In seventeen sixty-nine, a hunter from North Carolina named Daniel Boone goes through the gap. He spends almost two years in Kentucky, hunting, trapping, sleeping under the stars. <chuckle> His family doesn't hear from him for months at a time.

He falls in love with the place.

In seventeen seventy-five, Boone and a crew of thirty axemen cut a path through the gap, for two hundred miles. The Wilderness Road.

<gasp> And then, the people come. Tens of thousands. Then hundreds of thousands. Families walking, with packhorses, cattle and pigs, over a road so rough wagons can barely use it.

<sigh> Most of them come from Virginia and the Carolinas. And many bring enslaved people with them.

## Part 2C

The Shawnee and their allies fight hard for their hunting ground. For years, settlers live inside wooden forts.

In seventeen seventy-six, Boone's daughter Jemima and two other girls are paddling a canoe on the river when they're captured by a raiding party. <short pause> Boone tracks them through the forest for two days, and gets them back. The story later helps inspire the novel "The Last of the Mohicans".

<breath> Then Boone himself is captured by the Shawnee. He's adopted into the nation by a chief named Blackfish, and given a new name. Months later, he hears of a planned attack on his fort, escapes, and runs a hundred and sixty miles in four days to warn the settlers.

<short pause> A few weeks later, hundreds of Shawnee warriors surround Fort Boonesborough. The settlers are outnumbered. For more than a week, the two sides talk, fight, and try to tunnel under each other's walls. <phew> Then the rain collapses the tunnel, and the attackers give up.

<sigh> Boone's own son is killed in the fighting. Then another son.

In seventeen ninety-two, Kentucky becomes the fifteenth state. The first state west of the Appalachian Mountains.

## Part 2D

Kentucky's farmers grow lots of corn. Too much to carry back over the mountains, and too much to float all the way down the river before it spoils.

So they turn it into something easier to carry, that lasts forever, and is worth a lot more. <chuckle> Whiskey.

Aged in barrels of charred oak, it turns sweet, smoky and amber. It's called bourbon, maybe after Bourbon County, or maybe after Bourbon Street in New Orleans, where much of it was drunk.

<short pause> Today, around ninety-five percent of the world's bourbon is made in Kentucky.

## Part 3: Bluegrass

In the middle of Kentucky, the limestone under the soil makes the grass rich in calcium. <short pause> Horses raised on it grow strong bones.

The Bluegrass region becomes the horse capital of America. <chuckle> The grass isn't actually blue, by the way. In spring, its little seed heads give the fields a faint blue-purple shimmer. If you squint.

And on the farms, Kentucky grows hemp, tobacco and corn. Worked by enslaved people.

## Part 3B

During the War of Eighteen-Twelve, Mammoth Cave goes to war. <gasp> Its floors are full of saltpetre, a key ingredient in gunpowder. Enslaved miners dig it out by the ton, and send it east to make powder for the army.

<short pause> Later, an enslaved teenager named Stephen Bishop becomes the cave's most famous guide. He crosses a deep, bottomless-looking pit on a wooden ladder, finds underground rivers full of blind, white, eyeless fish, and draws a map of the cave that's used for decades.

<chuckle> Visitors ask for him by name.

<tsk> And in eighteen forty-two, the cave's owner, a doctor, has a strange idea. He moves patients with tuberculosis INTO the cave, into little stone huts, believing the cool, steady cave air will cure them. <sigh> It doesn't. Several of them die, and the rest leave sicker than before. The ruins of the huts are still down there.

## Part 3C

From Lexington comes one of the most powerful politicians in America, Henry Clay. He's called the Great Compromiser. <short pause> Three times, he helps patch the country together, with deals over slavery that keep the North and South from splitting apart.

<sigh> But each deal only buys time.

<short pause> Kentucky is also a place of wild religious fire. In eighteen-oh-one, at Cane Ridge, a huge outdoor camp meeting draws maybe ten or twenty thousand people. <gasp> For days, preachers preach from tree stumps and wagons, and people shout, sing, shake and faint. <chuckle> Some reportedly bark like dogs. It's one of the biggest religious gatherings America has ever seen.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

Kentucky is a slave state. But its whole northern edge is the Ohio River. On the other bank, Ohio, Indiana and Illinois are free.

<breath> So enslaved people in Kentucky can see freedom across the water. Many try to cross. In winter, some cross on the ice.

A story of one young mother doing that, with her child in her arms, ends up in the book "Uncle Tom's Cabin".

<long pause> And slave traders in Louisville sell thousands of people to the cotton plantations of the Deep South. Down the Ohio. Down the Mississippi.

It's where the phrase "sold down the river" comes from.

## Part 3E

And in a one-room log cabin in Kentucky, in eighteen-oh-nine, a boy is born. Abraham Lincoln.

<short pause> Less than a year before, about a hundred miles away, another boy was born. Jefferson Davis.

Two families. Same state. Then both families leave.

Lincoln's family crosses the Ohio River north, to Indiana. A free state. Lincoln later wrote that his father moved "partly on account of slavery".

Davis's family moves south, to Mississippi. Cotton country. Davis grows up to own a big plantation, and enslaved people.

<whispers> Two boys from the same state. One goes north. One goes south.

## Part 4: Divided

In eighteen sixty-one, the war comes. Lincoln leads the Union. Davis leads the Confederacy.

<short pause> And Kentucky? Kentucky tries to stay neutral. <chuckle> It doesn't work.

Lincoln knows how important Kentucky is. Its rivers, its horses, its men. He reportedly says, "I hope to have God on my side, but I must have Kentucky."

Kentucky stays in the Union. But it keeps slavery. <sigh> And its people split.

In October eighteen sixty-two, the biggest battle in Kentucky is fought at Perryville. In a drought, both armies are desperate for water. The fighting starts around a creek.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

Around a hundred thousand Kentuckians fight for the Union. Around thirty-five thousand fight for the Confederacy.

<breath> Brothers fight brothers. Fathers fight sons.

Even Lincoln's own wife, Mary Todd Lincoln, from Lexington, has half-brothers who die fighting for the South.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

At Camp Nelson, a Union army base in Kentucky, thousands of enslaved men arrive to join the army. Joining is their way to freedom.

<breath> Their wives and children follow them. But in November eighteen sixty-four, in freezing weather, the army throws the families out of the camp.

<long pause> More than a hundred of them die of cold and disease. After a public outcry, the army lets the families back in, and builds them a home for refugees. In early eighteen sixty-five, Congress frees the wives and children of Black soldiers.

## Part 4D

After the war, Kentucky refuses to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, which ends slavery. <tsk> It finally does it, symbolically... in nineteen seventy-six.

And in the mountains of eastern Kentucky, old wartime grudges turn into family feuds. <short pause> The most famous is the McCoys of Kentucky against the Hatfields, just across the river in West Virginia. Over a stolen pig, a forbidden romance and an election-day fight, the two families shoot at each other for years.

## Part 5: Horses and Coal

In eighteen seventy-five, Louisville holds its first big horse race. The Kentucky Derby. <gasp> About two minutes of thundering hooves, on the first Saturday in May. People call it the most exciting two minutes in sports.

<short pause> The winning jockey of that first Derby is a Black teenager, Oliver Lewis. In fact, Black jockeys win fifteen of the first twenty-eight Derbies. One of them, Isaac Murphy, wins it three times. He's one of the greatest jockeys who ever lived.

## Part 5B

In eighteen ninety-three, two sisters in Louisville, Mildred and Patty Hill, write a song for kindergarten children, called "Good Morning to All".

<chuckle> Later, someone changes the words. "Happy birthday to you."

It becomes one of the most-sung songs in the English language. <tsk> And for decades, a company charges money every time it's used in a film. Until a court rules in two thousand fifteen that the copyright claim wasn't valid.

## Part 5C

In the eastern mountains, coal mining takes over. Companies build whole towns. They own the houses, the schools and the stores, and sometimes pay miners in company money, that only works in the company store.

<groan> In the nineteen-thirties, in Harlan County, miners fight to join a union. Company guards and miners shoot at each other. The fighting is so violent that people call it "Bloody Harlan".

<short pause> In a little mountain place called Butcher Hollow, a coal miner's daughter named Loretta grows up singing. Loretta Lynn. She becomes a country music legend, singing about exactly that life.

## Part 5D

In the nineteen-thirties, a man named Harland Sanders runs a gas station in Corbin. <chuckle> He starts cooking fried chicken for hungry travellers, at his own dining table, with a secret mix of eleven herbs and spices.

The governor makes him an honorary Kentucky colonel. He keeps the title. And the white suit. And the little black tie.

<sigh> When a new highway bypasses his restaurant, he loses almost everything. So, in his sixties, he drives around the country, selling his recipe to restaurant owners, one by one. <short pause> Hundreds of them say no, the story goes.

<laugh> Kentucky Fried Chicken.

## Part 5E

In nineteen thirty-seven, the U.S. government builds a giant vault at Fort Knox, Kentucky, to store much of America's gold. Granite walls. A vault door weighing more than twenty tons.

<chuckle> And from the hills comes a new music. Fast fiddles, banjos and high, lonesome singing. Bill Monroe and his Blue Grass Boys.

Bluegrass.

## Part 6: Champions

In Louisville, in nineteen fifty-four, a twelve-year-old boy's red bicycle is stolen. <tsk> He tells a police officer he wants to beat up the thief.

The officer, who coaches boxing, tells him he'd better learn to fight first.

Six years later, the boy wins an Olympic gold medal. <gasp> Then he becomes heavyweight champion of the world. His name is Cassius Clay. He changes it to Muhammad Ali.

"Float like a butterfly, sting like a bee." <short pause> He refuses to fight in the Vietnam War, loses his title for it, and wins it back. "The Greatest".

<breath> Years later, at the Atlanta Olympics in nineteen ninety-six, the final torchbearer climbs the stairs, and there, waiting, with trembling hands, is Muhammad Ali. <short pause> He lights the Olympic flame. The whole stadium stands.

## Part 6B

<chuckle> Kentucky gives the world one more famous car, too. Since nineteen eighty-one, every Chevrolet Corvette has been built in Bowling Green, Kentucky.

And in Georgetown, Toyota builds its biggest factory in the world.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In nineteen twenty-five, a cave explorer named Floyd Collins gets trapped in a narrow passage near Mammoth Cave. A rock pins his foot.

<breath> For more than two weeks, the whole country follows the rescue on the radio and in the newspapers. Rescuers try to dig down to him.

<long pause> They reach him too late.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

In December two thousand twenty-one, a long-track tornado tears across western Kentucky in the middle of the night. The town of Mayfield is shattered.

<breath> Dozens of people are killed across the state.

## Part 6E

Today, Kentucky ages millions of barrels of bourbon, still breeds some of the fastest horses on Earth, and its cave keeps getting longer.

So how does one state give birth to both sides of the Civil War?

<whispers> It starts with a gap in the mountains.

Through the Cumberland Gap came families from the slave-holding South. <short pause> They built a slave state, right on the edge of the free North. Kentucky was the border. The meeting place.

So when two boys were born there, one family went north, toward freedom. The other went south, toward cotton. <breath> And when they grew up, they led the two sides of the same war.

Kentucky was the doorway. America walked through it in two different directions.

<chuckle> And on the way, it picked up some bourbon and a bucket of chicken.
