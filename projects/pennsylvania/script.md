# The Entire History of Pennsylvania

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,800 spoken words, so roughly 15 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused, playful for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And the bloodiest"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, warm storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Curious, a little wry, then warm and hopeful. |
| Part 3 | Rising, important, a little wry. |
| Part 4 | Serious and steady. |
| Part 5 | Big, loud, industrial energy, then grave. |
| Part 6 | Playful and inventive, then serious. |
| Part 7 | Warm, then calm and certain, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1681 · Penn's Woods 1681 to 1754 · Revolution 1754 to 1800 · Civil War 1861 to 1865 · Oil and Steel 1859 to 1920 · Sweet Inventions 1900 to 1960 · Rust Belt 1960 to 2001 · Today 2001 to now.

## Hook

Hershey's chocolate? <short pause> Pennsylvania. Crayola crayons? Pennsylvania. The Slinky, Peeps, and the groundhog who tells you if winter's over? All Pennsylvania.

There's a town in Pennsylvania where a fire has been burning under the ground since nineteen sixty-two.

<long pause> And the bloodiest battle ever fought in America happened in a small Pennsylvania farm town.

<chuckle> But the strangest thing? This whole state exists because a king couldn't pay a bill.

<short pause> So how does an unpaid bill end up giving birth to the United States?

<whispers> It starts with a king who owed a man sixteen thousand pounds.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Long before any king, this land of rivers, hills and endless forests belonged to the Lenape, the Susquehannock, the Shawnee and other nations.

The Lenape live along the Delaware River, in villages of bark longhouses. They fish for shad when the fish swim upriver in spring. They plant corn, beans and squash. They trade up and down the river, using wampum, beads made from shells.

<short pause> The Susquehannock, along the great Susquehanna River, are powerful traders and warriors, with fortified towns.

<short pause> And people have been here a very, very long time. Under a rock overhang near Pittsburgh, called Meadowcroft, archaeologists dug through layer after layer of old campfires. <gasp> The deepest ones may be sixteen thousand years old. Among the oldest signs of people anywhere in North America.

## Part 1B

In the sixteen hundreds, Swedish and Finnish settlers arrive along the Delaware, to trade for furs. Then the Dutch take over. Then the English.

<short pause> And England has a king named Charles the Second.

King Charles has a problem. <chuckle> He's broke. He's spent a fortune, and he owes money to half of London.

## Part 2: Penn's Woods

One of the people the king owes money to is an admiral named Sir William Penn. <gasp> Sixteen thousand pounds. A fortune.

When the admiral dies, the debt passes to his son. Also called William Penn.

And the son is... unusual. He's a Quaker.

Quakers refuse to bow or take off their hats to anyone, even the king. They refuse to swear oaths. They refuse to fight in wars. They believe men and women are equal before God. <tsk> In England, they get arrested for it.

Young William Penn has been thrown in prison several times. Once in the Tower of London, for writing a pamphlet.

## Part 2B

So Penn makes the king an offer. <short pause> Forget the money. Give me land in America instead.

In sixteen eighty-one, the king agrees. He hands Penn about forty-five thousand square miles. <gasp> One of the largest private land grants in history.

And the king names it. Penn wants "Sylvania". Latin for woods. The king adds "Penn", after the admiral.

<chuckle> Penn's Woods. Pennsylvania. Penn is embarrassed. He's worried people will think he named it after himself. He even tries to bribe the king's clerks to change it. They won't.

## Part 2C

Penn calls his colony a "holy experiment".

Anyone who believes in God can come and worship freely. No official church. Elected assemblies. Fair trials.

And instead of just taking land, Penn buys it from the Lenape. <short pause> Legend says he signs a great treaty of friendship with them under a giant elm tree.

He designs a city on a neat grid between two rivers, with green squares, so a fire can't spread like it did in London. He gives it a Greek name meaning "brotherly love". <short pause> Philadelphia.

<short pause> And Penn writes something else unusual into his plans. A way for the people to change the colony's own rules. <chuckle> A government that can be improved.

In seventeen-oh-one, he signs a new charter of rights for the colony. Fifty years later, to celebrate that anniversary, the colony orders a big bell for its State House. <short pause> And they choose a Bible verse to put on it. Proclaim liberty throughout all the land.

Hold on to that bell.

## Part 2D

And people pour in.

Quakers from England and Wales. German farmers, Mennonites and Amish, whose descendants are still called the Pennsylvania Dutch. <chuckle> Not Dutch. "Deutsch". German. Scots-Irish settlers heading for the frontier.

<short pause> In sixteen eighty-eight, in Germantown, a small group of German Quakers write a protest against slavery. It's the first written protest against slavery in the American colonies.

By the seventeen seventies, Philadelphia is one of the biggest and richest cities in British America.

## Part 2E

And its most famous resident arrives as a runaway teenager, with a loaf of bread under each arm. <short pause> Benjamin Franklin.

He becomes a printer. Then he starts a lending library. A fire company. A hospital. A college. He invents bifocal glasses, a stove, and the lightning rod.

<laugh> And yes, he flies a kite in a thunderstorm, to prove lightning is electricity. He's lucky it didn't kill him.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

But after Penn dies, his sons are less honest.

<breath> In seventeen thirty-seven, they produce an old deed for as much land as a man can walk in a day and a half. Then they hire the fastest runners they can find, and clear a path in advance.

<long pause> The Walking Purchase takes about twelve hundred square miles from the Lenape.

## Part 3: Revolution

In seventeen fifty-four, a young Virginia officer named George Washington fights the French in the woods of western Pennsylvania, near where Pittsburgh is today.

<sigh> He's forced to surrender his little fort, Fort Necessity. It's the only time in his life Washington ever surrenders. And the fighting helps start a world war. The French and Indian War.

Britain wins. And to pay for it... <chuckle> taxes. You know how this goes.

## Part 3B

In seventeen seventy-five, the colonies send delegates to Philadelphia. The Continental Congress.

And on July fourth, seventeen seventy-six, in the Pennsylvania State House, they approve the Declaration of Independence.

The building is now called Independence Hall. And the bell in its tower, with the words "Proclaim Liberty throughout all the Land"... <short pause> the Liberty Bell.

<chuckle> Its famous crack came later. Probably in the eighteen-forties. It's been out of tune for most of its life.

<short pause> When the British march on Philadelphia, the city's bells are smuggled out in wagons, so the British can't melt them down for cannonballs. <chuckle> The State House bell is hidden under the floorboards of a church in Allentown. For almost a year.

## Part 3C

The British capture Philadelphia. Congress runs away.

<breath> Washington's army spends the winter of seventeen seventy-seven at Valley Forge. Cold, hungry, many with no shoes. About two thousand soldiers die of disease.

But a Prussian officer, Baron von Steuben, drills them every day. <short pause> He can't speak much English. He swears at them in German and French, and gets someone to translate the swearing. <laugh> They love him.

In the spring, they march out a real army.

<short pause> And the most famous moment of the whole war starts from Pennsylvania too. On Christmas night, seventeen seventy-six, Washington's freezing army climbs into boats on the Pennsylvania bank of the icy Delaware River, crosses in the dark, and surprises the enemy at Trenton.

## Part 3D

In seventeen eighty, Pennsylvania passes the first law in America to begin abolishing slavery. <sigh> Slowly. Too slowly. But first.

And in seventeen eighty-seven, delegates return to the same room in Philadelphia, and write the Constitution of the United States.

<short pause> The Declaration and the Constitution. Signed in the same building. In a city that exists because of an unpaid debt.

For ten years, Philadelphia is the capital of the United States.

<short pause> So far: a debt, a Quaker, a city of brotherly love, and the two most important documents in American history. All in one Pennsylvania building.

<breath> But the story of Pennsylvania is about to get darker. And dirtier. And richer.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

In the summer of seventeen ninety-three, yellow fever sweeps through Philadelphia.

<breath> Tens of thousands of people flee the city. The government flees. Doctors are overwhelmed.

Black Philadelphians, led by two ministers, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones, volunteer to nurse the sick and bury the dead. Many people wrongly believe Black people can't catch the fever. <long pause> They can. Some of the volunteers die too.

About five thousand people die. One in ten people in the city.

## Part 3F

A year later, Richard Allen, who was born enslaved and bought his own freedom, founds his own church in Philadelphia. Mother Bethel. <short pause> It grows into the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the first independent Black denomination in America. It now has millions of members around the world.

## Part 3G

In seventeen ninety-four, farmers in western Pennsylvania refuse to pay a new tax on whiskey. They tar and feather tax collectors. The Whiskey Rebellion.

<gasp> President Washington puts on his uniform and rides out at the head of thirteen thousand troops. It's the only time a sitting president has led an army in the field.

<chuckle> The rebels mostly go home before the army arrives.

## Part 4: Civil War

Pennsylvania is a free state on the border with slave states. Philadelphia becomes a key stop on the Underground Railroad. A free Black man named William Still helps hundreds of people escape. He keeps careful notes of every one, so families can find each other later.

<short pause> And in Congress, a fierce lawmaker from Lancaster, Thaddeus Stevens, becomes one of the loudest voices against slavery. After the war, he helps write the amendments that end slavery and promise equal protection of the law.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In July eighteen sixty-three, General Robert E. Lee's army marches north, into Pennsylvania.

The two armies meet near a small town called Gettysburg.

<breath> For three days, they fight in the fields, in the orchards, and on the hills. On the third day, more than twelve thousand Confederate soldiers march across an open field, straight at the Union guns. Pickett's Charge.

<long pause> By the end, more than fifty thousand soldiers are killed, wounded, captured or missing. It's the bloodiest battle in American history.

<short pause> Only one civilian in the town is killed. A twenty-year-old woman named Jennie Wade, baking bread in her kitchen, is hit by a stray bullet through the door.

<short pause> Four months later, President Lincoln comes to Gettysburg to dedicate a cemetery. He speaks for about two minutes. About two hundred and seventy words. That a nation "of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

## Part 5: Oil and Steel

In eighteen fifty-nine, in Titusville, a man named Edwin Drake drills down into the ground looking for oil. <chuckle> People call it Drake's Folly.

At sixty-nine feet, he hits it. <gasp> The first commercial oil well in America. The oil age begins in Pennsylvania.

<chuckle> Oil fever hits. In eighteen sixty-five, a town called Pithole appears out of nowhere around a gusher. In months, it has fifteen thousand people, fifty hotels, and one of the busiest post offices in the state. <short pause> Then the wells run dry. A year or two later, it's empty. Today, it's a grassy hill with signs where the streets used to be.

## Part 5B

And then Pittsburgh catches fire. <chuckle> Not literally. Well... almost.

The city sits where three rivers meet, on top of coal and near iron. A Scottish immigrant named Andrew Carnegie, who started as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill, builds giant steel mills. Pittsburgh steel builds bridges, railroads and skyscrapers across America.

<short pause> Carnegie becomes one of the richest men in history. And then, he gives it away. Almost all of it. He pays for more than two thousand five hundred public libraries around the world. <short pause> Mostly to atone, some say, for how he treated his workers.

<groan> The smoke is so thick that streetlights sometimes stay on during the day. A writer calls Pittsburgh "hell with the lid off".

<short pause> In eastern Pennsylvania, miners dig hard black coal called anthracite, which burns hot and clean. Coal heats much of the East.

<sigh> Some of the workers are boys as young as eight or nine. Breaker boys. They sit all day hunched over chutes, picking slate and rock out of the coal with bare hands, in clouds of black dust.

## Part 5C

And in Pittsburgh, an inventor named George Westinghouse bets on a new kind of electricity. Alternating current. It can travel long distances. <chuckle> Thomas Edison says it's dangerous. Westinghouse wins. Most of the world's electricity still works his way.

<gasp> And on November second, nineteen twenty, a Westinghouse station in Pittsburgh, KDKA, broadcasts the results of the presidential election. It's often called the first commercial radio station in America. People listening on homemade sets hear the news before the newspapers can print it.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In eighteen ninety-two, at Carnegie's steel works in Homestead, workers go on strike. The company brings in hundreds of armed Pinkerton guards, on barges, up the river.

<long pause> A gun battle breaks out. Workers and guards are killed. Then the state militia arrives, and the union is broken.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

Above the town of Johnstown, rich men from Pittsburgh, including Carnegie and his partner Henry Frick, own a private fishing lake behind an old earth dam. The dam is badly repaired.

<breath> In May eighteen eighty-nine, after days of heavy rain, it breaks.

<long pause> A wall of water rushes down the valley. More than two thousand two hundred people are killed.

## Part 6: Sweet Inventions

But Pennsylvania also invents things for fun.

In nineteen-oh-three, Milton Hershey starts building a whole town around a chocolate factory. Houses, a park, schools, a zoo. <chuckle> The streetlights are shaped like Hershey's Kisses.

In Pittsburgh, Henry Heinz sells ketchup and pickles with a slogan: fifty-seven varieties. <laugh> He already had more than sixty. He just liked the number fifty-seven.

<short pause> And during the Second World War, Hershey makes a special chocolate bar for soldiers. It's designed to taste... only a little better than a boiled potato. So soldiers won't eat it all at once. <laugh> It works. Soldiers call it Hitler's secret weapon.

## Part 6B

Crayola crayons come from Easton. Peeps from Bethlehem. Zippo lighters from Bradford. The banana split, from a drugstore in Latrobe.

<gasp> And in nineteen sixty-seven, a McDonald's owner in Uniontown, Pennsylvania, invents a bigger burger with two patties and a special sauce. The Big Mac.

The Slinky is invented by an engineer in Philadelphia in the nineteen forties, after he sees a spring fall off a shelf and keep... walking.

<chuckle> And every February second since eighteen eighty-seven, people in Punxsutawney have asked a groundhog called Phil about the weather.

<short pause> And since nineteen forty-seven, the best twelve-year-old baseball players in the world come every August to Williamsport, Pennsylvania, for the Little League World Series.

## Part 6C

Pennsylvania also changes science.

In nineteen forty-six, at the University of Pennsylvania, engineers switch on ENIAC. A room-sized machine with eighteen thousand vacuum tubes. <gasp> One of the first general-purpose electronic computers in the world.

And in nineteen fifty-five, at the University of Pittsburgh, a doctor named Jonas Salk announces a vaccine against polio. A disease that had paralysed tens of thousands of children every year. Church bells ring across America.

When asked who owns the patent, Salk answers: <short pause> could you patent the sun?

## Part 6D

In Pittsburgh, a gentle man in a cardigan sweater makes a TV show for children, where he talks to them about feelings, kindness and fear. Mister Rogers.

<short pause> And a shy kid from Pittsburgh named Andy Warhol grows up to paint soup cans, and becomes one of the most famous artists in the world.

## Part 7: Rust Belt

After the Second World War, the mills start to close. Cheaper steel comes from overseas. Pittsburgh loses jobs, and people.

<sigh> In a few years, tens of thousands of steelworkers are out of work. Pittsburgh loses about half its population from its peak. Bethlehem Steel, which made the steel for the Golden Gate Bridge and much of the New York skyline, eventually shuts down completely.

<sigh> In Centralia, a fire starts in an old coal mine in nineteen sixty-two. Nobody can put it out. Smoke comes up through cracks in the roads. Almost everyone moves away. <short pause> The fire is still burning today.

And in nineteen seventy-nine, at Three Mile Island near Harrisburg, a nuclear reactor partly melts down. America's worst commercial nuclear accident. <phew> Thankfully, no one is killed.

## Part 7B (quiet style)

On September eleventh, two thousand one, passengers on United Flight ninety-three learn that other hijacked planes have hit New York and Washington.

<breath> They fight back against the hijackers.

<long pause> The plane crashes into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. All forty passengers and crew are killed.

They are remembered as heroes.

## Part 7C

Today, Pittsburgh builds robots, runs giant hospitals and universities, and its rivers are clean enough for kayaks. Philadelphia is still one of America's great cities.

<chuckle> In the nineteen seventies, Pittsburgh's football and baseball teams win so many championships that it calls itself the City of Champions. Even as the mills close. <chuckle> And Rocky still runs up the museum steps.

<laugh> When the Philadelphia Eagles finally win the Super Bowl, in twenty eighteen, the city greases its lamp posts, to stop fans from climbing them. <chuckle> It doesn't work.

## Part 7D

So how does an unpaid bill end up giving birth to the United States?

<short pause> It starts with a king who owed a man sixteen thousand pounds.

Instead of money, a Quaker took land. He made it a place where anyone could worship and live freely. That freedom made Philadelphia big, rich and open. And when thirteen colonies needed a place to meet and argue about freedom... <short pause> they came to Philadelphia.

<long pause> King Charles never paid that debt.

<chuckle> But in a way, America has been paying it back ever since.
