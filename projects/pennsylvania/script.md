## Hook

Hershey's chocolate? <short pause> Pennsylvania. Crayola crayons? Pennsylvania. The Slinky, Peeps, and the groundhog who tells you if winter's over? All Pennsylvania.

There's a town in Pennsylvania where a fire has been burning under the ground since nineteen sixty-two.

<long pause> And the bloodiest battle ever fought in America happened in a small Pennsylvania farm town.

But the strangest thing? This whole state exists because a king couldn't pay a bill.

<short pause> So how does an unpaid bill end up giving birth to the United States?

<whispers> It starts with a king who owed a man sixteen thousand pounds.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Long before any king, this land of rivers and forests belonged to the Lenape, the Susquehannock, the Shawnee and other nations.

The Lenape live along the Delaware River, in villages of bark longhouses. They fish for shad in spring, plant corn, and trade up and down the river.

In the sixteen hundreds, Swedish and Dutch traders arrive to buy furs. Then the English take over. And England has a king named Charles the Second.

King Charles has a problem. He's broke.

## Part 2: Penn's Woods

One of the people the king owes money to is an admiral named Sir William Penn. Sixteen thousand pounds. A fortune.

When the admiral dies, the debt passes to his son. Also called William Penn.

And the son is... unusual. He's a Quaker. Quakers refuse to bow to anyone, even the king. They refuse to fight in wars. They believe men and women are equal before God. In England, they get arrested for it. Young William Penn has been in prison several times.

So Penn makes the king an offer. Forget the money. Give me land in America instead.

In sixteen eighty-one, the king agrees. He hands Penn about forty-five thousand square miles. One of the largest private land grants in history.

And the king names it after Penn's father. Penn's Woods. Pennsylvania. Penn is embarrassed. He's worried people will think he named it after himself. <chuckle> They do.

## Part 2B

Penn calls his colony a "holy experiment".

Anyone who believes in God can come and worship freely. No official church. Elected assemblies. And he buys land from the Lenape instead of just taking it.

He designs a city on a neat grid between two rivers, with green squares. He gives it a Greek name meaning "brotherly love". Philadelphia.

And people pour in. Quakers from England and Wales. German farmers, Mennonites and Amish, whose descendants are still called the Pennsylvania Dutch, from the word "Deutsch". Scots-Irish settlers heading for the frontier.

By the seventeen seventies, Philadelphia is one of the biggest and richest cities in British America.

And its most famous resident is a printer named Benjamin Franklin. He starts a library, a fire company, a hospital and a college. And flies a kite in a thunderstorm.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

But after Penn dies, his sons are less honest.

In seventeen thirty-seven, they produce an old deed for as much land as a man can walk in a day and a half. Then they hire the fastest runners they can find and clear a path in advance.

The Walking Purchase takes about twelve hundred square miles from the Lenape.

## Part 3: Revolution

In seventeen fifty-four, a young Virginia officer named George Washington fights the French in the woods of western Pennsylvania, near where Pittsburgh is today. It helps start a world war. The French and Indian War.

Britain wins. And to pay for it... taxes. You know how this goes.

In seventeen seventy-five, the colonies send delegates to Philadelphia. The Continental Congress.

And on July fourth, seventeen seventy-six, in the Pennsylvania State House, they approve the Declaration of Independence.

The building is now called Independence Hall. And the bell in its tower, with the words "Proclaim Liberty throughout all the Land"... the Liberty Bell. Its famous crack came later.

## Part 3B

The British capture Philadelphia. Congress runs away.

Washington's army spends the winter of seventeen seventy-seven at Valley Forge. Cold, hungry, many with no shoes. About two thousand soldiers die of disease.

But a Prussian officer, Baron von Steuben, drills them every day. In the spring, they march out a real army.

## Part 3C

In seventeen eighty, Pennsylvania passes the first law in America to begin abolishing slavery. Slowly. Too slowly. But first.

And in seventeen eighty-seven, delegates return to the same room in Philadelphia, and write the Constitution of the United States.

The Declaration and the Constitution. Signed in the same building. In a city that exists because of an unpaid debt.

## Part 4: Civil War

Pennsylvania is a free state on the border with slave states. Philadelphia becomes a key stop on the Underground Railroad.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In July eighteen sixty-three, General Robert E. Lee's army marches north, into Pennsylvania.

The two armies meet near a small town called Gettysburg.

For three days, they fight in the fields and on the hills. By the end, more than fifty thousand soldiers are killed, wounded, captured or missing.

It's the bloodiest battle in American history.

Four months later, President Lincoln comes to Gettysburg to dedicate a cemetery. He speaks for about two minutes. About two hundred and seventy words. That a nation "of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

## Part 4C

Meanwhile, in eighteen fifty-nine, in Titusville, a man named Edwin Drake drills down into the ground looking for oil. People call it Drake's Folly.

At sixty-nine feet, he hits it. The first commercial oil well in America. The oil age begins in Pennsylvania.

## Part 5: Oil and Steel

And then Pittsburgh catches fire. Not literally. Well... almost.

The city sits on rivers, coal and iron. A Scottish immigrant named Andrew Carnegie builds giant steel mills. Pittsburgh steel builds bridges, railroads and skyscrapers across America.

The smoke is so thick that streetlights sometimes stay on during the day.

Coal miners dig in the mountains. Many are immigrants. They fight long, sometimes bloody battles for better pay and safer mines.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In eighteen eighty-nine, an old dam above the town of Johnstown breaks after heavy rain.

A wall of water rushes down the valley.

More than two thousand two hundred people are killed.

## Part 5C

But Pennsylvania also invents things for fun.

In nineteen hundred and three, Milton Hershey starts building a whole town around a chocolate factory. The streetlights are shaped like Hershey's Kisses.

Crayola crayons come from Easton. Peeps from Bethlehem. Ketchup from Heinz in Pittsburgh.

The Slinky is invented by an engineer in Philadelphia in the nineteen forties, after he sees a spring fall off a shelf and "walk".

And every February second since eighteen eighty-seven, people in Punxsutawney have asked a groundhog called Phil about the weather.

## Part 6: Rust Belt

After the Second World War, the mills start to close. Cheaper steel comes from overseas. Pittsburgh loses jobs, and people.

In Centralia, a fire starts in an old coal mine in nineteen sixty-two. Nobody can put it out. Smoke comes up through the roads. Almost everyone moves away. The fire is still burning today.

And in nineteen seventy-nine, at Three Mile Island near Harrisburg, a nuclear reactor partly melts down. America's worst commercial nuclear accident. Thankfully, no one is killed.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

On September eleventh, two thousand one, passengers on United Flight ninety-three fight back against the hijackers.

The plane crashes into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. All forty passengers and crew are killed.

They are remembered as heroes.

## Part 6C

Today, Pittsburgh builds robots and runs giant hospitals and universities. Philadelphia is still one of America's great cities. And Rocky still runs up the museum steps.

So how does an unpaid bill end up giving birth to the United States?

It starts with a king who owed a man sixteen thousand pounds.

Instead of money, a Quaker took land. He made it a place where anyone could worship and live freely. That freedom made Philadelphia big, rich and open. And when thirteen colonies needed a place to meet and argue about freedom... they came to Philadelphia.

<short pause> King Charles never paid that debt.

<chuckle> But in a way, America has been paying it back ever since.
