## Hook

The shopping cart? <short pause> Invented in Oklahoma. The parking meter? Oklahoma. The yield sign? Also Oklahoma.

The word "Oklahoma" comes from two Choctaw words, meaning "red people".

In eighteen eighty-nine, fifty thousand people lined up, and at the sound of a gun, raced each other to grab free land. Whole cities appeared in a single afternoon.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-one, a white mob burned down one of the most successful Black neighbourhoods in America, in Tulsa. Up to three hundred people were killed.

<short pause> So how does land promised to Native nations forever end up given away in a race?

<whispers> It starts with a promise. "As long as the grass grows, and the water runs."

## Part 1: First Peoples

A thousand years ago, at a place called Spiro, people build great mounds and trade with half a continent. Copper from the Great Lakes. Shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Spiro is one of the most important towns in North America.

Later, the plains of Oklahoma are home to the Wichita, the Caddo, the Osage, the Kiowa, the Comanche, the Apache and many others.

## Part 2: Indian Territory

Now, the promise.

In the eighteen-thirties, the United States forces five Native nations out of the Southeast. The Cherokee, the Choctaw, the Chickasaw, the Muscogee Creek and the Seminole.

They are sent west, to land the government calls "Indian Territory". Today's Oklahoma.

The government tells them this new land will be theirs forever. In the words people remember: as long as the grass grows, and the water runs.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The journeys west kill thousands. Of cold, hunger and disease. Each nation has its own Trail of Tears.

## Part 2C

And then, in Indian Territory, they rebuild. From almost nothing.

They write constitutions. Elect governments. Build capitals, courts, schools and newspapers. The Cherokee publish in their own alphabet. The Choctaw send children to boarding academies.

For a few decades, Indian Territory is a land of self-governing Native nations.

## Part 2D

Then the Civil War comes. Some leaders of these nations, many of them slaveholders, side with the Confederacy. A Cherokee general, Stand Watie, is the last Confederate general to surrender.

After the war, the United States punishes all five nations. It forces them to give up the western half of their land.

The promise starts to crack.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

Some of that land is used to move even more nations into Indian Territory. Dozens of them, from all over the country. The Kiowa, the Comanche and the Cheyenne are forced onto reservations after years of war.

## Part 3: Land Runs

But in the middle of the territory, there's a big piece of land that hasn't been given to any nation. The "Unassigned Lands".

Settlers called "Boomers" demand it. Over and over, they sneak in and are thrown out.

In eighteen eighty-nine, the government gives in. It announces: on April twenty-second, at exactly noon, anyone can rush in and claim a free farm.

## Part 3B

Fifty thousand people line the border. On horses. In wagons. On bicycles. Even hanging off trains.

At noon, soldiers fire their guns. And they all race.

By nightfall, Oklahoma City has around ten thousand people. It had none that morning. Guthrie does the same.

<chuckle> But some people cheated. They sneaked in early and hid, so they could jump out and claim the best land first. They were called "Sooners".

Today, Oklahoma proudly calls itself the Sooner State.

## Part 3C

More land runs follow. In eighteen ninety-three, a hundred thousand people race for the Cherokee Outlet.

Then the government breaks up the nations' shared land into small private farms, with the leftover land sold to settlers. The nations' governments are dissolved.

In nineteen-oh-five, leaders of the Five Tribes propose their own state, run by Native people, called Sequoyah. Congress says no.

On November sixteenth, nineteen-oh-seven, Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory are joined together. Oklahoma becomes the forty-sixth state.

## Part 4: Oil

And then, under the land... oil.

Huge oil fields are found. Tulsa becomes "the Oil Capital of the World". Fortunes are made overnight.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

The Osage Nation had bought its land itself. And it kept the rights to the oil beneath it. The Osage become some of the richest people in the world, per person.

Then, in the nineteen-twenties, Osage people start dying. Shot. Poisoned. Blown up. Dozens of them. Killed by white men who want to inherit their oil money.

The young Bureau of Investigation, later called the FBI, finally catches some of the killers. Many deaths are never solved.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

And in Tulsa, there's a Black neighbourhood called Greenwood. Banks, hotels, theatres, doctors and shops. People call it "Black Wall Street".

On May thirty-first, nineteen twenty-one, after a young Black man is accused of assaulting a white woman in an elevator, a white mob attacks Greenwood.

Over two days, they burn it to the ground. More than thirty-five city blocks. Up to three hundred people are killed. Thousands are left homeless.

For decades, it's left out of the history books.

## Part 5: Dust Bowl

In the nineteen-thirties, a terrible drought hits the plains. The farmers have ploughed up the grass that held the soil down. And now the wind takes it.

On April fourteenth, nineteen thirty-five, Black Sunday, a giant wall of dust rolls across Oklahoma, turning day into night.

Hundreds of thousands of people leave the plains. Many head for California. People call them "Okies". John Steinbeck writes about them in The Grapes of Wrath. And an Oklahoma singer, Woody Guthrie, sings about them.

## Part 5B

But Oklahoma City is busy inventing.

In nineteen thirty-five, it installs the world's first parking meter.

And in nineteen thirty-seven, a grocery store owner named Sylvan Goldman notices that shoppers stop buying when their baskets get too heavy. So he puts two baskets on a folding chair with wheels.

The shopping cart.

<chuckle> At first, nobody wants to use it. Men think it's girly. Women think it looks like a baby pram. So he hires people to push carts around his store, pretending to shop.

## Part 6: Today

In nineteen ninety-five, Oklahoma City is hit by terrorism.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

On April nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five, a truck bomb destroys a federal building in Oklahoma City. One hundred and sixty-eight people are killed, including nineteen children.

## Part 6C

And then, in two thousand twenty, a Seminole man named Jimcy McGirt takes a case to the Supreme Court. The question: is the Muscogee Creek reservation, created all those years ago, still a reservation?

The court looks for any law where Congress clearly ended it. It can't find one.

So it rules: yes. For criminal law, much of eastern Oklahoma is still Native land.

<short pause> "Because Congress has not said otherwise," the court says, "we hold the government to its word."

## Part 6D

So how does land promised to Native nations forever end up given away in a race?

It starts with a promise. "As long as the grass grows, and the water runs."

The promise was broken again and again. By war. By land runs. By oil and greed. Piece by piece, the land was taken.

But the nations survived. Today, thirty-nine tribal nations have their headquarters in Oklahoma. And in two thousand twenty, the highest court in the land said part of that old promise still stands.

<short pause> The grass is still growing.

<chuckle> And somewhere in Oklahoma, somebody is pushing a shopping cart.
