# The Entire History of Oklahoma

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,600 spoken words, so roughly 14 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen twenty-one"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, ancient storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Serious and dignified, then proud. |
| Part 3 | Breathless race-day energy, wry and funny. |
| Part 4 | Booming oil-rush energy, then grave. |
| Part 5 | Dusty and weary, then cheeky and inventive. |
| Part 6 | Steady and thoughtful; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1830 · Indian Territory 1830 to 1866 · Outlaw Country 1866 to 1889 · Land Runs 1889 to 1907 · Oil 1907 to 1929 · Dust Bowl 1930 to 1940 · Sooner State 1940 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

The shopping cart? <short pause> Invented in Oklahoma. The parking meter? Oklahoma. The yield sign? Also Oklahoma.

<chuckle> The word "Oklahoma" comes from two Choctaw words, meaning "red people".

In eighteen eighty-nine, fifty thousand people lined up, and at the sound of a gun, raced each other to grab free land. Whole cities appeared in a single afternoon.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-one, a white mob burned down one of the most successful Black neighbourhoods in America, in Tulsa. Up to three hundred people were killed.

<short pause> So how does land promised to Native nations forever end up given away in a race?

<whispers> It starts with a promise. "As long as the grass grows, and the water runs."

## Part 1: First Peoples

A thousand years ago, at a place called Spiro, by the Arkansas River, people build great earth mounds, and trade with half a continent. Copper from the Great Lakes. Conch shells from the Gulf of Mexico. Obsidian from the Rocky Mountains.

<gasp> Inside one mound, leaders are buried with treasure. Carved shell cups, copper plates, pearls, and cloth woven from feathers.

<groan> In the nineteen-thirties, treasure hunters form a company, tunnel into the mound, and even use dynamite. They sell what they find to collectors all over the world. <sigh> People later called Spiro the "King Tut of the Midwest". But much of it was lost forever.

## Part 1B

Later, the plains of Oklahoma are home to the Wichita, the Caddo, the Osage, the Kiowa, the Comanche, the Apache and many others.

<short pause> When Spanish horses spread north, the Comanche become some of the greatest horse riders who have ever lived. They build an empire on horseback, stretching across the southern plains. Spain, Mexico and Texas all fear them.

## Part 2: Indian Territory

Now, the promise.

In the eighteen-thirties, the United States forces five Native nations out of the Southeast. The Cherokee, the Choctaw, the Chickasaw, the Muscogee Creek and the Seminole. <sigh> They have farms, towns, schools and written laws. It doesn't matter. Settlers want their land, and there's gold in Georgia.

They are sent west, to land the government calls "Indian Territory". Today's Oklahoma.

The government tells them this new land will be theirs forever. In the words people remember: as long as the grass grows, and the water runs.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The journeys west kill thousands. Of cold, hunger and disease. <breath> Families bury their children by the side of the road, and keep walking.

Each nation has its own Trail of Tears.

A Choctaw chief, asked about the journey, calls it a trail of tears and death. <long pause> The name stays.

## Part 2C

And then, in Indian Territory, they rebuild. From almost nothing.

They write constitutions. Elect governments. Build capitals, courts, schools and newspapers. <short pause> The Cherokee had already invented a way to write their own language. A man named Sequoyah, who couldn't read any language, invented a whole alphabet of eighty-five symbols, one for each sound. Within a few years, most Cherokee can read and write.

<gasp> Indian Territory has some of the best schools west of the Mississippi.

<short pause> In Tahlequah, the Cherokee build a capitol, a supreme court, and a school for girls, the Cherokee Female Seminary, teaching Latin, music and algebra. <chuckle> At a time when very few American girls get to go to high school at all.

## Part 2D

In eighteen forty-seven, just fourteen years after their own Trail of Tears, the Choctaw hear about a terrible famine in Ireland. People starving, far across the ocean.

<breath> The Choctaw know what that's like. So they collect what little money they have, about a hundred and seventy dollars, and send it to Ireland.

<short pause> The Irish never forget. In twenty seventeen, Ireland unveiled a giant sculpture of steel feathers to say thank you. And during the pandemic, Irish people raised money for Native nations in return.

## Part 2E

Then the Civil War comes. <sigh> Some leaders of these nations, many of them slaveholders, side with the Confederacy. A Cherokee general, Stand Watie, is the last Confederate general to surrender.

After the war, the United States punishes all five nations. It forces them to free the people they enslaved, and to give up the western half of their land.

<tsk> The promise starts to crack.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

Some of that land is used to move even more nations into Indian Territory. Dozens of them, from all over the country.

<breath> The Kiowa, the Comanche and the Cheyenne are forced onto reservations after years of war, and after the buffalo they live on are slaughtered.

<long pause> The great Apache leader Geronimo is brought to Fort Sill, Oklahoma, as a prisoner of war. He dies there, still a prisoner, in nineteen-oh-nine.

## Part 3: Outlaw Country

Indian Territory becomes a hiding place for outlaws. <chuckle> Tribal courts can't arrest outsiders. And federal marshals have to ride in from Arkansas.

One of those marshals is Bass Reeves. <gasp> Born into slavery, he escaped during the Civil War and hid in Indian Territory, learning to speak Muscogee, Seminole and Cherokee.

He becomes one of the first Black deputy U.S. marshals west of the Mississippi. A crack shot. A master of disguise. <short pause> Over his career, he's said to arrest more than three thousand people. He even arrests his own son, for murder.

<short pause> Some historians think the Lone Ranger, the masked cowboy hero of old radio and TV, may have been partly inspired by him.

## Part 3B

But in the middle of the territory, there's a big piece of land that hasn't been given to any nation. The "Unassigned Lands".

Settlers called "Boomers" demand it. <tsk> Over and over, they sneak in and are thrown out by the army.

In eighteen eighty-nine, the government gives in. It announces: on April twenty-second, at exactly noon, anyone can rush in and claim a free farm.

## Part 3C

<gasp> Fifty thousand people line the border. On horses. In wagons. On bicycles. On foot. Even hanging off the sides of trains.

At noon, soldiers fire their guns. <laugh> And they all race.

Horses gallop. Wagons crash. People leap off trains while they're still moving, and hammer stakes into the ground.

By nightfall, Oklahoma City has around ten thousand people. <short pause> It had none that morning. Guthrie does the same.

<chuckle> But some people cheated. They sneaked in early and hid in the bushes, so they could jump out and claim the best land first. They were called "Sooners".

<laugh> Today, Oklahoma proudly calls itself the Sooner State.

## Part 3D

More land runs follow. In eighteen ninety-three, a hundred thousand people race for the Cherokee Outlet. <groan> In the heat and dust, people and horses collapse.

<sigh> Then the government breaks up the nations' shared land into small private farms, with the leftover land sold to settlers. Within a few decades, much of it is lost, through trickery, debt and theft. The nations' governments are dissolved.

<short pause> In nineteen-oh-five, leaders of the Five Tribes propose their own state, run by Native people, called Sequoyah. They write a constitution. Congress says no.

On November sixteenth, nineteen-oh-seven, Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory are joined together. Oklahoma becomes the forty-sixth state.

<chuckle> At first, the capital is Guthrie. But in nineteen-ten, the state seal is taken to Oklahoma City, in the middle of the night, according to legend in a laundry bag. The capital moves with it.

## Part 4: Oil

And then, under the land... oil.

<gasp> Huge oil fields are found. Towns appear overnight. Tulsa becomes "the Oil Capital of the World". Fortunes are made in a week.

<short pause> In nineteen twenty-six, a Tulsa businessman named Cyrus Avery helps plan a brand-new highway, from Chicago all the way to Los Angeles. <chuckle> He picks the number himself. Route Sixty-Six.

It runs right through Tulsa and Oklahoma City. Oklahoma still has more miles of the old road you can drive on than any other state.

<chuckle> To this day, there's an oil well on the lawn of Oklahoma's State Capitol. It's said to be the only state capitol in America with one.

## Part 4B

Oklahoma's most famous son, in those years, is a Cherokee cowboy who can do amazing tricks with a rope. <short pause> Will Rogers.

He goes into show business, and becomes the most popular entertainer in America. Movies, radio, newspaper columns. <laugh> He jokes about politicians so gently that they love him for it. "I never met a man I didn't like," he says.

<short pause> And from the Sac and Fox Nation comes Jim Thorpe. In nineteen twelve, he wins two Olympic gold medals. The king of Sweden calls him the greatest athlete in the world. <tsk> Then his medals are taken away, because he'd once been paid a few dollars to play baseball. In twenty twenty-two, more than a century later, he's finally restored as the sole champion.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

The Osage Nation had bought its land itself. And it kept the rights to the oil beneath it. The Osage become some of the richest people in the world, per person.

<breath> Then, in the nineteen-twenties, Osage people start dying. Shot. Poisoned. Blown up in their homes. Dozens of them.

Killed by white men, some of them their own husbands and in-laws, who want to inherit their oil money.

<long pause> The young Bureau of Investigation, later called the FBI, finally catches some of the killers. Many deaths are never solved.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

And in Tulsa, there's a Black neighbourhood called Greenwood. Banks, hotels, theatres, churches, doctors and shops. People call it "Black Wall Street".

On May thirty-first, nineteen twenty-one, after a young Black man is accused of assaulting a white woman in an elevator, a white mob attacks Greenwood.

<breath> Over two days, they burn it to the ground. More than thirty-five city blocks. Planes fly overhead.

<long pause> Up to three hundred people are killed. Thousands are left homeless.

For decades, it's left out of the history books.

## Part 5: Dust Bowl

In the nineteen-thirties, a terrible drought hits the plains. <sigh> The farmers have ploughed up the deep-rooted grass that held the soil down. And now the wind takes it.

On April fourteenth, nineteen thirty-five, Black Sunday, a giant wall of dust rolls across Oklahoma, turning day into night. <groan> People stuff wet rags under their doors. It still gets in.

<short pause> To stop the wind, the government plants a giant wall of trees across the plains, from Canada to Texas. More than two hundred million trees. The Shelterbelt. Many of those windbreaks still stand.

Hundreds of thousands of people leave the plains. Many head for California. People call them "Okies". John Steinbeck writes about them in The Grapes of Wrath.

<short pause> And a singer from Okemah, Oklahoma, Woody Guthrie, travels with them, and writes a song you probably know. "This Land Is Your Land".

## Part 5B

But Oklahoma City is busy inventing.

<chuckle> In nineteen thirty-five, it installs the world's first parking meter. Some drivers are furious.

And in nineteen thirty-seven, a grocery store owner named Sylvan Goldman notices that shoppers stop buying when their baskets get too heavy. So he puts two baskets on a folding chair with wheels.

<laugh> The shopping cart.

At first, nobody wants to use it. Men think it's girly. Women think it looks like a baby pram. So he hires people to push carts around his store, pretending to shop.

<chuckle> And in nineteen forty-three, a Broadway musical called "Oklahoma!" becomes a giant hit. Its title song becomes the state song.

## Part 6: Today

In May nineteen ninety-nine, a tornado near Oklahoma City produces some of the fastest winds ever measured on Earth. <gasp> About three hundred miles an hour. Oklahoma sits in the heart of Tornado Alley.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

On April nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five, a man parks a truck full of explosives outside a federal building in Oklahoma City.

<long pause> The bomb destroys the building. One hundred and sixty-eight people are killed, including nineteen children, many of them in a daycare centre.

<breath> Today, a field of empty chairs stands where the building was. One for each person.

## Part 6C

And then, in two thousand twenty, a Seminole man named Jimcy McGirt takes a case to the Supreme Court. <short pause> The question: is the Muscogee Creek reservation, created all those years ago, still a reservation?

The court looks for any law where Congress clearly ended it. It can't find one.

So it rules: yes. For criminal law, much of eastern Oklahoma is still Native land.

"Because Congress has not said otherwise," the court says, "we hold the government to its word."

<sigh> Two years later, the court narrows the ruling, letting the state also prosecute non-Native people who commit crimes against Native people there. The arguments go on.

<breath> And in Tulsa, in two thousand one, a state commission finally publishes the full story of what happened to Greenwood. Today, a history centre stands on Greenwood Avenue. <short pause> Just over a century after the fire, the last known survivors were still telling the story.

## Part 6D

So how does land promised to Native nations forever end up given away in a race?

<whispers> It starts with a promise. "As long as the grass grows, and the water runs."

The promise was broken again and again. By war. By land runs. By oil and greed. Piece by piece, the land was taken.

<breath> But the nations survived. Today, thirty-nine tribal nations have their headquarters in Oklahoma. And in two thousand twenty, the highest court in the land said part of that old promise still stands.

<short pause> The grass is still growing.

<chuckle> And somewhere in Oklahoma, somebody is pushing a shopping cart.
