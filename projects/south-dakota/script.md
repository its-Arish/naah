# The Entire History of South Dakota

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen ninety"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, reverent storyteller of hills and prairie. |
| Part 2 | Serious and steady, then gold-rush rowdy, then grave. |
| Part 3 | Quiet and grieving throughout. |
| Part 4 | Grand and monumental, with a pointed edge. |
| Part 5 | Quirky roadside cheer, then firm and dignified; quiet and certain at the end. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1743 · The Treaty 1743 to 1874 · Gold Rush 1874 to 1889 · Wounded Knee 1889 to 1890 · Carved Mountains 1927 to 1973 · The Money 1980 to now.

## Hook

Mount Rushmore? <short pause> South Dakota.

<chuckle> Wall Drug gives away free ice water. And it puts up signs for it hundreds of miles away. There's a palace in South Dakota covered in corn. They redecorate it every year.

<long pause> In eighteen ninety, at Wounded Knee, U.S. soldiers killed around two hundred and fifty Lakota men, women and children.

And today, there are more than two billion dollars sitting in a government account, waiting for the Lakota people. They refuse to take it.

<short pause> So why would a nation turn down two billion dollars?

<whispers> It starts with a general who went looking for gold.

## Part 1: First Peoples

South Dakota is split in two by the Missouri River. East River: green farmland. West River: dry grasslands, badlands, and, rising out of the plains, a dark, pine-covered island of mountains.

<whispers> The Black Hills.

<gasp> In the Badlands, wind and rain carve the soft rock into spires and canyons, striped pink, yellow and grey. And out of those layers wash the bones of ancient animals. Sabre-toothed cats. Giant pig-like beasts. Tiny early horses.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-four, in the town of Hot Springs, a bulldozer driver levelling ground for houses uncovers a mammoth tusk. <gasp> It turns out to be an ancient sinkhole, where more than sixty mammoths fell in and couldn't climb out. The houses are never built. It's a museum now.

## Part 1B

Under the Black Hills, there's a cave that breathes. <chuckle> When the air pressure outside changes, wind rushes in or out of its tiny entrance, strong enough to blow off a hat. Wind Cave.

<short pause> In a Lakota story, it's the place where the people first came up from beneath the earth.

For centuries, the Arikara farm along the Missouri, in villages of earth lodges, growing corn, beans and squash.

<sigh> Then European diseases arrive, long before most Europeans do. Smallpox sweeps up the Missouri River again and again. The Arikara villages lose most of their people.

Then, in the seventeen hundreds, the Lakota arrive on horseback from the east. <short pause> The Black Hills become the centre of their world. They call them Paha Sapa. A sacred place.

<short pause> The highest point in the hills was named after a U.S. general who had attacked a Lakota camp. <sigh> In twenty sixteen, it's renamed Black Elk Peak, after a Lakota holy man who had a vision there as a boy.

## Part 1C

In seventeen forty-three, two French explorers, brothers, bury a small lead plate on a hill by the Missouri River, claiming the land for France.

<chuckle> In nineteen thirteen, a group of schoolkids, playing on that hill, dig it up.

<gasp> In eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark meet a band of Lakota near today's capital, Pierre. It's tense. Warriors grab the boat's rope. Clark draws his sword. Soldiers aim their guns. <phew> An old chief steps in, and calms everyone down.

## Part 2: The Treaty

In the eighteen sixties, the Lakota, led by Red Cloud, fight the U.S. Army to stop a road through their hunting lands. And they win.

<short pause> In eighteen sixty-eight, the United States signs the Treaty of Fort Laramie. It creates the Great Sioux Reservation, including all of the Black Hills, for the "absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota and their allies.

No white person, the treaty says, may settle there without their permission. <short pause> And no land can be taken unless three-quarters of the adult men agree.

## Part 2B

Now, the general.

In eighteen seventy-four, rumours say there's gold in the Black Hills. The army sends Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer on an expedition into the hills, with a thousand soldiers, scientists, a photographer, a brass band, and two gold miners.

<tsk> Inside the treaty land.

The miners find gold in French Creek. Custer sends the news out. <gasp> And newspapers across America shout it.

Gold in the Black Hills!

## Part 2C

Thousands of miners pour in. Illegally. The army barely tries to stop them.

Towns spring up overnight. Like Deadwood. <chuckle> Saloons, gunfights, gamblers, and a crowded Chinatown. Calamity Jane, a sharp-shooting frontierswoman who dresses like a man, rides into town.

In eighteen seventy-six, Wild Bill Hickok is shot from behind while playing poker in Deadwood. <short pause> His cards are said to be black aces and black eights. Ever since, that's been called the "dead man's hand".

<chuckle> Decades later, in nineteen twenty-nine, a tiny old prospector known as Potato Creek Johnny finds one of the biggest gold nuggets ever discovered in the Black Hills, in a creek. <short pause> It's about the size of his thumb. Well, of two thumbs.

## Part 2D

The government tries to buy the Black Hills. The Lakota refuse. <sigh> So war comes. In eighteen seventy-six, the Lakota and Cheyenne defeat Custer at the Little Bighorn.

But the army keeps coming. <tsk> And Congress passes a law that stops sending food rations to the Lakota until they give up the Black Hills. People call it "sell or starve".

In eighteen seventy-seven, Congress simply takes the Black Hills. It never gets the three-quarters of signatures the treaty demanded.

## Part 2E

The Homestake Mine opens in the Black Hills. <gasp> It becomes the biggest and deepest gold mine in North America. It runs for a hundred and twenty-five years, and goes down more than a mile and a half.

<chuckle> It's bought by a mining tycoon named George Hearst. Its gold helps pay for his son's newspaper empire. William Randolph Hearst.

<chuckle> After it closes, scientists move into its deepest tunnels, to catch tiny, ghostly particles from the Sun, called neutrinos. The rock above blocks out everything else. An experiment there helps win a Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Part 2F

In eighteen eighty-nine, South Dakota becomes a state. <laugh> On the same day as North Dakota. President Harrison shuffles the papers so nobody knows which one he signed first.

<short pause> Out on the eastern prairie, near the railroad town of De Smet, a girl named Laura Ingalls grows up in a claim shanty, through blizzards and grasshopper plagues. <chuckle> Years later, she writes about it. "Little Town on the Prairie".

<short pause> In the eighteen-eighties, a wave of settlers floods into Dakota. The Dakota Boom. Norwegians, Germans, Germans from Russia, Czechs, Dutch. <sigh> A Norwegian writer from South Dakota, Ole Rølvaag, later writes about how the empty prairie crushed some of them. Giants in the Earth.

## Part 3: Wounded Knee (quiet style)

By eighteen ninety, the buffalo are gone. The Lakota are on shrinking reservations. Hungry. <breath> Their rations have been cut.

Many begin to dance the Ghost Dance, a religious movement promising the return of the old world, and of the people they've lost.

The government panics. <long pause> In December, police come to arrest Sitting Bull. In the struggle, he is shot and killed.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

Two weeks later, on December twenty-ninth, eighteen ninety, soldiers surround a band of Lakota led by Spotted Elk, camped at Wounded Knee Creek. Spotted Elk is sick with pneumonia.

<breath> As the soldiers try to take their guns, a shot goes off.

The soldiers open fire, with rifles and with rapid-firing cannons on the hill.

<long pause> Around two hundred and fifty Lakota are killed, and maybe more. Many of them women and children.

<breath> Days later, after a blizzard, their bodies are buried in a single grave on the hill.

<breath> Twenty soldiers are awarded the Medal of Honor. For decades, Lakota families have asked for those medals to be taken back.

## Part 4: Carved Mountains

In the nineteen-twenties, South Dakota wants tourists. A historian has an idea. Carve giant figures into the Black Hills.

A sculptor named Gutzon Borglum picks a granite mountain the Lakota call the Six Grandfathers. <gasp> From nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen forty-one, about four hundred workers blast and carve the faces of four presidents into it. Washington. Jefferson. Theodore Roosevelt. Lincoln. Each face is about sixty feet tall.

<short pause> About ninety percent of the rock is removed with dynamite. Workers hang in harnesses from the cliff. <phew> Amazingly, nobody dies during the carving.

<chuckle> Jefferson was first carved on the other side of Washington. The rock cracked. So they blasted him off, and started again.

Mount Rushmore.

<short pause> Borglum dies in nineteen forty-one, just before it's finished. His son, Lincoln Borglum, completes the work. <chuckle> The plan was to carve the presidents down to their waists. The money ran out.

<breath> On land the treaty had promised to the Lakota.

## Part 4B

<whispers> Behind Lincoln's head, there's a hidden chamber, cut into the mountain. Borglum wanted it to be a Hall of Records. <short pause> In nineteen ninety-eight, they finally put a box inside, with panels telling the story of the carving, and sealed it.

## Part 4C

A few miles away, in nineteen forty-eight, a Lakota chief, Henry Standing Bear, invites another sculptor, Korczak Ziolkowski, to carve a mountain too. A giant figure of the Lakota leader Crazy Horse, on his horse. "We want the white man to know," the chief writes, "that the red man has great heroes, too."

<gasp> It's meant to be bigger than all four Rushmore faces put together. The face is finished in nineteen ninety-eight. <short pause> The rest is still being carved today.

<short pause> The project refuses government money. It's paid for by visitors and donations.

## Part 5: The Money

Meanwhile, the rest of South Dakota gets creative.

In eighteen ninety-two, the town of Mitchell builds a hall covered in corn, to show off its harvest. The Corn Palace. <chuckle> Every year, artists redesign its murals, using hundreds of thousands of ears of coloured corn. <laugh> And every year, the pigeons try to eat it.

In nineteen thirty-six, a drugstore in the tiny town of Wall is struggling. The owner's wife has an idea. Put up signs offering free ice water to hot, thirsty drivers.

<chuckle> It works. Today, Wall Drug signs appear hundreds of miles away. Even, they say, on other continents.

## Part 5B

<laugh> In nineteen thirty-eight, a few motorcycle riders hold a race in the little town of Sturgis. Today, the Sturgis Motorcycle Rally brings in hundreds of thousands of bikers every August.

<short pause> In nineteen-ninety, fossil hunters find the most complete Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton ever discovered. Her name is Sue.

<chuckle> In nineteen eighty, South Dakota scraps its limit on interest rates. Big banks move their credit card businesses to Sioux Falls. <short pause> There's a decent chance your credit card statement has been mailed from South Dakota.

<short pause> And beside the Missouri River, near Chamberlain, stands a fifty-foot steel statue of a Native woman, holding a star quilt of blue diamonds that glitter in the wind. She's called Dignity.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

On the night of June ninth, nineteen seventy-two, a stalled storm drops about fifteen inches of rain on the Black Hills.

<breath> A dam breaks above Rapid City. Rapid Creek turns into a torrent through the middle of town.

<long pause> Two hundred and thirty-eight people are killed.

## Part 5D

But the Lakota never stop fighting for the Black Hills.

<gasp> In nineteen sixty-four, an Oglala Lakota runner from the Pine Ridge Reservation, Billy Mills, wins Olympic gold in the ten-thousand-metre race in Tokyo. Almost nobody had heard of him. <short pause> It's one of the biggest upsets in Olympic history.

<tsk> In nineteen seventy-three, activists occupy Wounded Knee for seventy-one days.

<sigh> Today, Pine Ridge, where Wounded Knee lies, is still one of the poorest places in the United States.

And in nineteen eighty, the U.S. Supreme Court rules on the case. The Black Hills, it says, were taken illegally. <short pause> The government must pay. About a hundred and six million dollars.

## Part 5E

The Lakota nations say no.

<breath> The Black Hills, they say, are not for sale.

So the money sits in an account, earning interest. Year after year. <gasp> Today, it's worth more than two billion dollars.

And it's still sitting there.

## Part 5F

So why would a nation turn down two billion dollars?

<whispers> It starts with a general who went looking for gold.

Custer found the gold that broke the treaty. The miners came. The war came. And the hills were taken. <short pause> The Supreme Court agreed it was wrong. But it offered money, not land.

<breath> To the Lakota, taking the money would mean selling the Black Hills. And Paha Sapa, the heart of their world, was never for sale.

The gold has mostly been dug out. The money keeps growing. And the Lakota are still waiting for what they actually asked for.

<long pause> The land.
