## Hook

Mount Rushmore? <short pause> South Dakota.

Wall Drug gives away free ice water. And it puts up signs for it hundreds of miles away.

There's a palace in South Dakota covered in corn. They redecorate it every year.

<long pause> In eighteen ninety, at Wounded Knee, U.S. soldiers killed around two hundred and fifty Lakota men, women and children.

And today, there are more than two billion dollars sitting in a government account, waiting for the Lakota people. They refuse to take it.

<short pause> So why would a nation turn down two billion dollars?

<whispers> It starts with a general who went looking for gold.

## Part 1: First Peoples

South Dakota is split in two by the Missouri River. East River: green farmland. West River: dry grasslands, badlands, and, rising out of the plains, a dark, pine-covered island of mountains.

The Black Hills.

For centuries, the Arikara farm along the Missouri, in villages of earth lodges.

Then, in the seventeen hundreds, the Lakota arrive on horseback from the east. The Black Hills become the centre of their world. They call them Paha Sapa. A sacred place.

## Part 1B

In seventeen forty-three, two French explorers bury a small lead plate on a hill by the Missouri River, claiming the land for France.

<chuckle> In nineteen thirteen, a group of schoolkids, playing on that hill, dig it up.

## Part 2: The Treaty

In the eighteen sixties, the Lakota, led by Red Cloud, fight the U.S. Army to stop a road through their hunting lands. And they win.

In eighteen sixty-eight, the United States signs the Treaty of Fort Laramie. It creates the Great Sioux Reservation, including all of the Black Hills, for the "absolute and undisturbed use" of the Lakota and their allies.

No white person, the treaty says, may settle there without their permission. And no land can be taken unless three-quarters of the adult men agree.

## Part 2B

Now, the general.

In eighteen seventy-four, rumours say there's gold in the Black Hills. The army sends Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer on an expedition into the hills, with a thousand soldiers, scientists, and two gold miners.

Inside the treaty land.

The miners find gold in French Creek. Custer sends the news out. And newspapers across America shout it.

Gold in the Black Hills!

## Part 2C

Thousands of miners pour in. Illegally. The army barely tries to stop them.

Towns spring up overnight. Like Deadwood. Saloons, gunfights, and gamblers.

<chuckle> In eighteen seventy-six, Wild Bill Hickok is shot from behind while playing poker in Deadwood. His cards are said to be black aces and black eights. Ever since, that's been called the "dead man's hand".

## Part 2D

The government tries to buy the Black Hills. The Lakota refuse. So war comes. In eighteen seventy-six, the Lakota and Cheyenne defeat Custer at the Little Bighorn.

But the army keeps coming. And in eighteen seventy-seven, Congress simply passes a law taking the Black Hills. It never gets the three-quarters of signatures the treaty demanded.

## Part 2E

The Homestake Mine opens in the Black Hills. It becomes the biggest and deepest gold mine in North America. It runs for a hundred and twenty-five years.

In eighteen eighty-nine, South Dakota becomes a state. On the same day as North Dakota. President Harrison shuffles the papers so nobody knows which one he signed first.

## Part 3: Wounded Knee (quiet style)

By eighteen ninety, the buffalo are gone. The Lakota are on shrinking reservations. Hungry.

Many begin to dance the Ghost Dance, a religious movement promising the return of the old world.

The government panics. In December, police come to arrest Sitting Bull. In the struggle, he is shot and killed.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

Two weeks later, on December twenty-ninth, eighteen ninety, soldiers surround a band of Lakota led by Spotted Elk, camped at Wounded Knee Creek.

As the soldiers try to take their guns, a shot goes off.

The soldiers open fire, with rifles and with rapid-firing cannons on the hill.

Around two hundred and fifty Lakota are killed, and maybe more. Many of them women and children.

## Part 4: Carved Mountains

In the nineteen-twenties, South Dakota wants tourists. A historian has an idea. Carve giant figures into the Black Hills.

A sculptor named Gutzon Borglum picks a granite mountain the Lakota call the Six Grandfathers. From nineteen twenty-seven to nineteen forty-one, workers blast and carve the faces of four presidents into it. Washington. Jefferson. Theodore Roosevelt. Lincoln.

Mount Rushmore.

<short pause> On land the treaty had promised to the Lakota.

## Part 4B

A few miles away, in nineteen forty-eight, a Lakota chief invites another sculptor to carve a mountain too. A giant figure of the Lakota leader Crazy Horse, on his horse.

It's still being carved today.

## Part 5: The Money

Meanwhile, the rest of South Dakota gets creative.

In eighteen ninety-two, the town of Mitchell builds a hall covered in corn, to show off its harvest. The Corn Palace. Every year, artists redesign its murals, using thousands of ears of coloured corn.

In nineteen thirty-six, a drugstore in the tiny town of Wall is struggling. The owner's wife has an idea. Put up signs offering free ice water to hot, thirsty drivers.

<chuckle> It works. Today, Wall Drug signs appear hundreds of miles away. Even, they say, on other continents.

In nineteen-ninety, fossil hunters find the most complete Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton ever discovered. Her name is Sue.

## Part 5B

But the Lakota never stop fighting for the Black Hills.

In nineteen seventy-three, activists occupy Wounded Knee for seventy-one days.

And in nineteen eighty, the U.S. Supreme Court rules on the case. The Black Hills, it says, were taken illegally. The government must pay. About a hundred and six million dollars.

## Part 5C

The Lakota nations say no.

The Black Hills, they say, are not for sale.

So the money sits in an account, earning interest. Year after year. Today, it's worth more than two billion dollars.

And it's still sitting there.

## Part 5D

So why would a nation turn down two billion dollars?

It starts with a general who went looking for gold.

Custer found the gold that broke the treaty. The miners came. The war came. And the hills were taken. The Supreme Court agreed it was wrong. But it offered money, not land.

To the Lakota, taking the money would mean selling the Black Hills. And Paha Sapa, the heart of their world, was never for sale.

<short pause> The gold has mostly been dug out. The money keeps growing. And the Lakota are still waiting for what they actually asked for.

The land.
