# The Entire History of Maryland

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "The bloodiest single day"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, salty bay storyteller, with a wink. |
| Part 2 | Hopeful colonial voice, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Rising tension through the night, then triumphant at dawn. |
| Part 4 | Brisk and inventive, then brave and determined. |
| Part 5 | Tense and grave. |
| Part 6 | Warm Baltimore pride, a little quirky; smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1634 · Lord Baltimore 1634 to 1763 · Revolution 1763 to 1790 · Rockets' Red Glare 1812 to 1815 · Railroads and Freedom 1827 to 1860 · Civil War 1861 to 1865 · Today 1865 to now.

## Hook

The Ouija board? <short pause> Patented in Baltimore.

<chuckle> Washington, D.C., was carved out of land Maryland gave up. And until twenty twenty-one, Maryland's own state song called Abraham Lincoln a despot.

<long pause> The bloodiest single day in American history happened in Maryland. At Antietam, about twenty-three thousand soldiers were killed, wounded or went missing. In one day.

And America's national anthem was written by a man stuck on a ship, watching his own country get bombed. Set to the tune of a British drinking song.

<short pause> So how does one night's battle over one Maryland city give the whole country its anthem?

<whispers> It starts with a flag sewn so big, the enemy couldn't miss it.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Maryland wraps around the Chesapeake Bay, the biggest estuary in the United States. Fresh river water meets salt sea water.

<gasp> And the bay sits on top of a giant crater. Thirty-five million years ago, an asteroid or comet smashed into the ocean here. Millions of years later, as the sea level rose, the rivers flowed into the low ground it left behind.

The bay is full of fish, blue crabs and oysters. <chuckle> So many oysters that, in the sixteen-hundreds, their giant reefs were a danger to ships.

## Part 1B

For thousands of years, the Piscataway, the Nanticoke, the Susquehannock and other nations fish, farm and trade around the bay.

In sixteen-oh-eight, Captain John Smith of Jamestown explores the Chesapeake in a small open boat, and makes a map of it.

<groan> While spearing fish with his sword, he gets stung by a stingray. His arm swells up so badly that his men dig him a grave. <laugh> By evening, he's feeling better, and eats the stingray for supper. The place is still called Stingray Point.

<short pause> Smith also meets the Susquehannock. He describes them as giants, with deep voices that echo like a voice in a vault. <chuckle> He may have been exaggerating. He usually was.

## Part 2: Lord Baltimore

In England, Catholics are persecuted. So an English Catholic nobleman, George Calvert, Lord Baltimore, asks the king for a colony where Catholics can live in peace.

He dies before it's granted. <short pause> His son, Cecil, gets it. They name it after the queen, Henrietta Maria. Maryland.

In sixteen thirty-four, two little ships, the Ark and the Dove, land the first settlers at St. Mary's City. Catholics and Protestants together.

## Part 2B

<gasp> In sixteen forty-eight, a woman named Margaret Brent, who manages the governor's estate, walks into Maryland's assembly and asks for two votes. One for herself, as a landowner. One as the governor's lawyer.

<tsk> They say no. But she's thought to be the first woman in America to ask for the right to vote.

In sixteen forty-nine, Maryland passes the Toleration Act. Christians of different churches must not be harassed for their faith. It's one of the first laws of religious toleration in the American colonies.

<chuckle> A few decades later, Protestants take over the colony... and ban Catholics from holding office. In the colony founded for Catholics.

## Part 2C

Maryland grows rich the same way as Virginia. <sigh> Tobacco. Worked by indentured servants, then by tens of thousands of enslaved Africans.

<chuckle> Tobacco is so important that people use it as money. Taxes, fines, even a minister's salary, are paid in pounds of tobacco.

## Part 2D

And Maryland has a long fight with its neighbour Pennsylvania about where the border is. <tsk> For decades, settlers from both sides burn each other's farms.

So in the seventeen-sixties, two English surveyors, Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon, spend four years marking it out across forests and mountains, by the stars, with stone markers every mile.

The Mason-Dixon Line.

<chuckle> They were just settling a property dispute. But later, their line becomes the famous border between the free North and the slave South.

## Part 3: Revolution

In the Revolution, at the Battle of Brooklyn in seventeen seventy-six, a few hundred Maryland soldiers charge the British again and again, to give Washington's army time to escape. <sigh> Most of them are killed or captured. <short pause> Washington is said to have praised Maryland's troops of the line. That's why it's called the Old Line State.

In seventeen eighty-three, Congress meets in Annapolis, Maryland, for a while. There, George Washington hands back his commission as commander of the army, and goes home to his farm. <gasp> A victorious general giving up his power. The world is amazed.

In seventeen eighty-eight, Maryland becomes the seventh state.

## Part 3B

<gasp> Before the war, in October seventeen seventy-four, a ship called the Peggy Stewart sails into Annapolis carrying more than a ton of taxed British tea.

The town is furious. <short pause> Boston had dumped its tea into the harbour at night, in disguise. Annapolis does something bolder. The ship's owner is made to set fire to his own ship, in broad daylight. <laugh> With the tea still on board. The Peggy Stewart burns to the waterline.

## Part 3C

In seventeen ninety, Maryland and Virginia give up land along the Potomac River for a new capital city. Washington, D.C.

<short pause> Helping to survey the boundaries of the new city is Benjamin Banneker, a free Black man from Maryland. He taught himself astronomy. <chuckle> As a young man, he built a wooden clock, carving every gear by hand, after studying a borrowed pocket watch. It kept time for decades.

He writes a letter to Thomas Jefferson, asking how a man who wrote that all men are created equal can keep people enslaved.

## Part 3D

Now, the flag.

In the War of Eighteen-Twelve, Britain strikes back at America. In August eighteen fourteen, British troops march into Washington and burn the White House and the Capitol.

Next target: Baltimore. <chuckle> A rich port full of privateers, armed private ships that have captured hundreds of British merchant ships. The British call Baltimore "a nest of pirates".

Guarding Baltimore's harbour is a star-shaped fort. Fort McHenry.

Its commander, Major George Armistead, wants a flag so big that the British will have no trouble seeing it from far away.

<short pause> So a Baltimore flag maker, Mary Pickersgill, with her teenage daughter, two nieces and an indentured African American apprentice, sews one. Thirty feet by forty-two feet. Each star is about two feet across. <laugh> It's so big they have to lay it out on the floor of a brewery to finish it.

## Part 3E

On land, the British march toward Baltimore. <gasp> Two Maryland sharpshooters kill their commanding general, Robert Ross, at North Point. The army hesitates.

On September thirteenth, eighteen fourteen, British warships start bombarding Fort McHenry. Rockets. Giant exploding shells. For twenty-five hours.

A Maryland lawyer named Francis Scott Key is on a ship with the British fleet. He'd come to win the release of a doctor friend they'd captured. <tsk> Now the British won't let him leave until the attack is over.

<whispers> All night, he watches the rockets and bombs over the fort. In the dark and the rain, he can't tell who's winning.

At dawn, he looks across the water. <long pause> And the giant flag is still flying.

<phew> Baltimore has held.

## Part 3F

Key writes a poem, starting on the back of a letter. "O say can you see, by the dawn's early light..."

It's printed in Baltimore days later, and sung to the tune of a popular British song from a gentlemen's club in London. <chuckle> A drinking song.

It's catchy. And it's very hard to sing.

In nineteen thirty-one, Congress makes it the national anthem. "The Star-Spangled Banner".

<chuckle> And the flag? Armistead's family snipped off pieces of it over the years, as souvenirs, for friends and veterans. One star is missing. Nobody knows where it went.

## Part 4: Railroads and Freedom

In eighteen twenty-seven, Baltimore starts the Baltimore and Ohio. One of America's first railroads for passengers and freight. <short pause> The ninety-year-old Charles Carroll, the last living signer of the Declaration of Independence, lays the first stone.

In eighteen thirty, its little steam engine, Tom Thumb, races a horse-drawn car. The engine is winning... until a belt slips. <laugh> The horse wins.

<short pause> Baltimore's shipyards build fast, sleek sailing ships with tall, raked-back masts. Baltimore clippers. <chuckle> They're so fast that pirates, privateers and smugglers all want one.

<gasp> And in eighteen forty-four, along the railroad line, Samuel Morse strings up the first long-distance telegraph line, from Washington to Baltimore. His first message: "What hath God wrought."

## Part 4B

Maryland is a slave state. And two of the greatest freedom fighters in American history are born enslaved in Maryland.

As a boy in Baltimore, Frederick Douglass is forbidden to learn to read. <whispers> So he trades bread with poor white boys in the street, in exchange for reading lessons.

In eighteen thirty-eight, he borrows a free Black sailor's papers, dresses as a sailor, and boards a train north. <short pause> He becomes the most famous voice against slavery in America.

## Part 4C

In eighteen forty-nine, a young woman on the Eastern Shore escapes to Pennsylvania. Then she comes back. Again and again. About thirteen trips, guiding around seventy people to freedom. <short pause> She travels at night, by the North Star, and says she never lost a passenger.

Harriet Tubman.

## Part 5: Civil War

When the war comes, Maryland is split. And it surrounds Washington, D.C. on three sides. If Maryland joins the Confederacy, the capital is cut off.

<gasp> In April eighteen sixty-one, a pro-Southern mob in Baltimore attacks Union soldiers marching between train stations. Some of the first deaths of the war. Lincoln sends troops, and arrests suspected secessionists, to hold Maryland in the Union.

<tsk> Weeks earlier, on his way to his own inauguration, Lincoln had been warned of a plot to kill him in Baltimore. So he passed through the city secretly, in the middle of the night, in a soft hat and an overcoat. <short pause> Cartoonists mocked him for it for months.

<tsk> A Maryland poet writes "Maryland, My Maryland", calling Lincoln a despot and urging Maryland to fight him. Decades later, it becomes the state song. And stays the state song... until twenty twenty-one.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

On September seventeenth, eighteen sixty-two, near the little town of Sharpsburg, along Antietam Creek, the Union and Confederate armies collide.

<breath> In a cornfield. On a sunken farm road. On a narrow stone bridge.

<long pause> In about twelve hours, roughly twenty-three thousand soldiers are killed, wounded or missing. It's the bloodiest single day in American history.

A nurse named Clara Barton works on the battlefield, under fire. A bullet passes through her sleeve and kills the man she's treating.

<breath> Lee's army retreats. Five days later, Lincoln announces the Emancipation Proclamation.

## Part 5C

In eighteen sixty-four, Maryland ends slavery with its own new constitution.

<short pause> In eighteen seventy-six, a Baltimore merchant named Johns Hopkins leaves his fortune, then the biggest gift of its kind in American history, to build a university and a hospital. <chuckle> Yes, his first name really is Johns. With an S.

## Part 6: Today

In eighteen ninety-one, a Baltimore company patents a "talking board". <chuckle> The Ouija board. According to the story, they asked the board itself what to call it.

Baltimore also gives the world Babe Ruth, who learns baseball at a school for troubled boys. <short pause> And Thurgood Marshall. He's not allowed into the University of Maryland's law school because he's Black. <gasp> So, as a young lawyer, he sues it, and wins, so the next Black student can get in. Later, he becomes the first Black justice on the Supreme Court.

And Edgar Allan Poe dies in Baltimore, mysteriously, in eighteen forty-nine. <whispers> Found delirious, in someone else's clothes. Nobody knows what happened. Baltimore's football team is named after his most famous poem. The Ravens.

<chuckle> And Baltimore's baseball shortstop, Cal Ripken Junior, plays in two thousand six hundred and thirty-two games in a row, without missing one. For more than sixteen years.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In nineteen fifty-one, a young Black mother named Henrietta Lacks is treated for cancer at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore.

<breath> Doctors take cells from her tumour, without asking her. <long pause> Her cells become the first human cells to keep growing in a lab. They help create the polio vaccine, and countless medical discoveries.

She dies that year, at thirty-one. For decades, her family doesn't know.

## Part 6C

<chuckle> Marylanders put a spice mix called Old Bay on just about everything. Especially steamed blue crabs, cracked open with wooden mallets on newspaper-covered tables.

<sigh> The bay's oysters, once so thick they threatened ships, have been fished out and hit by disease and pollution. Today there's only a tiny fraction left. <short pause> Marylanders are now building new oyster reefs, to bring them back.

<laugh> And Maryland's official state sport? Jousting. Riders still gallop at full speed, spearing small rings with a lance.

<short pause> Maryland is also home to the U.S. Naval Academy, in Annapolis. And to Camp David, the president's secret mountain retreat, in the Catoctin Mountains.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

Early on March twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-four, a giant cargo ship loses power and hits the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore's harbour.

<breath> The bridge collapses into the water.

<long pause> Six road workers on the bridge are killed.

## Part 6E

So how does one night's battle over one Maryland city give the whole country its anthem?

<whispers> It starts with a flag sewn so big, the enemy couldn't miss it.

Major Armistead wanted the British to see it. Mary Pickersgill made it huge. <short pause> So when Francis Scott Key looked across the water at dawn, after a whole night of bombs, he could still see it. And he wrote about what he saw.

<breath> That flag still exists. It's in the Smithsonian in Washington. On land that used to be Maryland.

<chuckle> And every time a crowd tries to hit the high note on "land of the free"... you can thank Baltimore.
