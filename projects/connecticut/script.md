# The Entire History of Connecticut

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,250 spoken words, so roughly 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In sixteen thirty-seven"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm river storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Stern Puritan voice with a wink, then grave. |
| Part 3 | Brave and dramatic, then clever. |
| Part 4 | Fast-talking Yankee peddler, cheeky. |
| Part 5 | Calm, trustworthy, a little proud; quirky in the inventions. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1614 · Puritan River Towns 1614 to 1700 · Revolution 1700 to 1788 · Yankee Peddlers 1788 to 1860 · Insurance City 1794 to 1900 · Today 1900 to now.

## Hook

The Frisbee? <short pause> Named after a Connecticut pie company.

<chuckle> A little lunch counter in New Haven says it served the first hamburger sandwich, in nineteen hundred. It still won't give you ketchup.

America's first phone book was printed in Connecticut. Fifty names. No numbers.

<long pause> In sixteen thirty-seven, English soldiers surrounded a Pequot village at Mystic, and set it on fire. Hundreds of people inside were killed.

And Hartford, Connecticut, calls itself the insurance capital of the world.

<short pause> So how does one of the smallest states end up insuring the world?

<whispers> It starts with ships that never came home.

## Part 1: First Peoples

<gasp> Two hundred million years ago, dinosaurs walked along the muddy shore of a lake in what's now Connecticut. In nineteen sixty-six, a bulldozer driver building a government office in Rocky Hill turns over a slab of rock... and finds their footprints. Hundreds of them.

<chuckle> They cancel the building. Today it's Dinosaur State Park.

## Part 1B

Connecticut is named after its great river. In the Algonquian language of the people who lived there, "Quinetucket" means "the long tidal river".

The Mohegan, the Pequot, the Niantic, the Quinnipiac and others fish its rivers and coast, and farm corn. <short pause> They make wampum, tiny beads carved from white whelk shells and purple quahog clam shells, strung into long belts. Wampum is used for trade, for treaties, and to record important agreements.

## Part 2: Puritan River Towns

In sixteen fourteen, a Dutch explorer named Adriaen Block sails up the river. Dutch traders build a little fort where Hartford is today.

Then the English arrive. <short pause> In sixteen thirty-six, a Puritan minister named Thomas Hooker leads about a hundred people from Massachusetts, driving their cattle through the woods for two weeks, to the Connecticut River. His wife is so ill that she's carried on a litter. They found Hartford.

## Part 2B

Hooker preaches that the power to choose leaders belongs to the people. <short pause> In sixteen thirty-nine, the river towns write down how they'll govern themselves. The Fundamental Orders.

It's one of the first written constitutions in history. The power comes from the people, not from a king.

<chuckle> That's why Connecticut is called the Constitution State.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

But the English and their allies are at war with the powerful Pequot nation.

<breath> In May sixteen thirty-seven, English soldiers, with Mohegan and Narragansett allies, surround a fortified Pequot village near the Mystic River before dawn. They set it on fire. People who try to escape are shot.

<long pause> Somewhere between four hundred and seven hundred Pequot are killed, most of them women, children and elders.

After the war, captives are sold into slavery. The English even try to wipe out the Pequot name. <breath> But the Pequot survive. Today, the Mashantucket Pequot Nation runs one of the largest casinos in the world, and a museum telling their story.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

In sixteen forty-seven, years before the famous trials in Salem, a woman named Alse Young is hanged in Hartford for witchcraft.

<breath> She's the first person executed as a witch in the American colonies.

<long pause> Connecticut goes on to accuse dozens more.

## Part 2E

In sixteen eighty-seven, a royal governor arrives to take Connecticut's charter away. <whispers> During the meeting, late in the evening, the candles suddenly go out. When they're relit, the charter is gone.

Legend says it was hidden in a giant hollow oak tree. The Charter Oak. <short pause> When the old tree finally blows down in a storm, in eighteen fifty-six, bells toll across Hartford. <chuckle> Its wood is turned into chairs, cups, and so many souvenirs that people joke the tree must have been the size of a forest. It's on Connecticut's state quarter.

## Part 2F

In seventeen-oh-one, a little college is founded. A few years later, a rich merchant named Elihu Yale sends it a gift. Some books, a portrait of the king, and a bundle of goods to sell. <chuckle> The college names itself after him. Yale.

And in seventeen sixty-four, the Hartford Courant starts printing. It's the oldest newspaper in America that's still published today.

## Part 3: Revolution

In seventeen seventy-six, a young Connecticut schoolteacher named Nathan Hale volunteers to spy on the British in New York. <sigh> He's caught and hanged. His famous last words, at least as people remembered them: "I only regret that I have but one life to lose for my country."

<gasp> The same year, a Yale student named David Bushnell builds a one-man wooden submarine, shaped like two turtle shells stuck together. The Turtle. A soldier inside pedals it under a British warship in New York Harbour, to screw a bomb to its hull. <groan> The screw won't go in. But it's the first submarine ever used in war.

<short pause> At Bunker Hill, a tough old Connecticut farmer-turned-general named Israel Putnam is said to tell his men to save their powder. "Don't fire until you see the whites of their eyes."

## Part 3B

Connecticut sends so much food, cattle and gunpowder to Washington's army that he calls it the Provision State.

<tsk> But Connecticut also produces America's most famous traitor. Benedict Arnold, from Norwich. In seventeen eighty-one, fighting for the British, he burns New London, a town just a few miles from where he grew up.

## Part 3C

In seventeen eighty-seven, at the Constitutional Convention, big states and small states are deadlocked. Should states get votes by population, or equal votes?

Connecticut's delegates, Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth, propose both. One house by population. One house where every state gets two senators.

<short pause> The Connecticut Compromise. It's still how Congress works.

<chuckle> Roger Sherman is also the only man to sign all four of America's great founding papers. Including the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution.

## Part 3D

In West Hartford, a schoolteacher named Noah Webster is fed up with British spelling. <chuckle> Why "colour" with a U? Why "centre" and not "center"?

So in eighteen twenty-eight, he publishes his American Dictionary of the English Language. Seventy thousand words. It takes him more than twenty years. <short pause> It's why Americans spell things differently from the British.

<tsk> In the winter of eighteen fourteen, during the War of Eighteen-Twelve, angry New England politicians meet in secret in Hartford. Some even talk about leaving the United States. <chuckle> By the time their complaints reach Washington, the war is over, and they look ridiculous. The Hartford Convention ruins their party.

## Part 4: Yankee Peddlers

Connecticut has rocky soil. So its people turn to trade, and to making things.

Connecticut peddlers travel the country with carts and packs, selling clocks, tin pots, buttons and pins. <laugh> They're so clever that legends say some even sold fake nutmegs, carved from wood.

That's why Connecticut is called the Nutmeg State.

<short pause> A clockmaker named Eli Terry figures out how to make clocks from identical, machine-cut wooden parts. Suddenly, ordinary families can afford a clock.

In New Haven, Eli Whitney builds muskets with interchangeable parts. In Hartford, Samuel Colt builds revolvers in a giant factory, with a blue onion dome on the roof.

<chuckle> And a stubborn inventor named Charles Goodyear spends years trying to make rubber that doesn't melt in summer or crack in winter. He ends up in debtors' prison more than once. <gasp> Then, around eighteen thirty-nine, he accidentally drops a mix of rubber and sulphur onto a hot stove. It turns tough and stretchy. Vulcanised rubber. <short pause> He later makes rubber goods in Connecticut. But he dies deep in debt.

## Part 4B

In eighteen thirty-three, in the village of Canterbury, a teacher named Prudence Crandall admits a Black girl to her school for young ladies. White parents pull their daughters out. <short pause> So Crandall turns it into a school for Black girls.

<tsk> The town is furious. The state passes a law to stop her. She's arrested. Mobs throw rocks and rotten eggs, and poison the school's well. <sigh> Finally, a mob breaks in at night and smashes the windows. She closes the school.

<short pause> A hundred and fifty years later, Connecticut makes her its state heroine.

## Part 4C

In eighteen thirty-nine, Africans kidnapped into slavery take over a Spanish ship called the Amistad. They try to sail home to Africa. <tsk> But the Spanish crew they kept alive to navigate steers north at night. The ship ends up off Long Island, and the Africans are jailed in Connecticut.

Their case goes all the way to the Supreme Court. Former president John Quincy Adams argues for them. <gasp> And they win their freedom.

## Part 4D

In New London and Mystic, sailors go whaling. <short pause> Voyages last three or four years, chasing whales across the Pacific, for the oil that lights America's lamps.

<chuckle> One of those whaling ships still floats today, at Mystic Seaport. The Charles W. Morgan. Built in eighteen forty-one, it made thirty-seven voyages. It's the last wooden whaling ship left in the world.

## Part 5: Insurance City

Now, the ships.

In the seventeen hundreds, Connecticut merchants send ships to the Caribbean, carrying timber, horses and salted meat. <groan> Storms sink some. Pirates take others. A merchant could lose everything with one ship.

So merchants start meeting in coffee houses and taverns to share the risk. Each one promises to pay a small part if a ship is lost. They sign their names underneath the list of cargo.

<short pause> They're called underwriters. And it's the start of the insurance business in Hartford.

## Part 5B

In seventeen ninety-four, Hartford's first fire insurance company is founded. Then more.

<gasp> In December eighteen thirty-five, a giant fire burns much of New York City. Many insurance companies go bust and can't pay.

But the president of a Hartford insurance company, Eliphalet Terry, travels to New York through the snow, by sleigh, and promises to pay every claim in full. Even if it means risking his own money.

<phew> Overnight, Hartford's insurance is famous for keeping its promises. Customers pour in.

<short pause> In eighteen forty-two, Hartford opens the Wadsworth Atheneum. The oldest public art museum in America.

## Part 5C

In eighteen sixty-four, a Hartford company called Travelers sells America's first accident insurance policy. <chuckle> The price: two cents. For a man walking home from the post office.

Later, Travelers writes some of the first car insurance. And Aetna, Travelers, and dozens of others make Hartford the insurance capital of the world.

<gasp> After the Civil War, thanks to insurance, guns and factories, Hartford is said to be the richest city in America, for its size.

## Part 5D

Hartford is also home to Mark Twain, who writes Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn in his big, fancy house there. His neighbour is Harriet Beecher Stowe, who wrote "Uncle Tom's Cabin".

<groan> Twain pours a fortune into a typesetting machine invented in Hartford, with eighteen thousand parts. It breaks down all the time. He goes bankrupt.

## Part 5E

<chuckle> In eighteen seventy-eight, New Haven prints the first telephone directory. It has fifty names, and no numbers. You just asked the operator.

Yale students throw empty pie tins from the Frisbie Pie Company to each other. Later, a toy company calls a plastic flying disc the Frisbee.

<short pause> Connecticut also gives the world Pez candy dispensers, made in the town of Orange. And Subway sandwiches, started by a seventeen-year-old in Bridgeport, to pay for college.

<laugh> Bridgeport's most famous mayor is the greatest showman of the age, P.T. Barnum, from nearby Bethel. He tours the world with circuses, giants and a tiny man called General Tom Thumb, also from Bridgeport.

<chuckle> And the actor who made Sherlock Holmes famous on stage, William Gillette, builds himself a stone castle on a hill above the Connecticut River, with hidden mirrors so he can spy on his guests, and a little railway around the garden.

<laugh> And in nineteen seventy-nine, in the little town of Bristol, a cable channel starts showing sports all day. ESPN.

<short pause> In nineteen thirty-nine, in Stratford, a Russian immigrant named Igor Sikorsky lifts off in a strange machine with one big rotor on top. <chuckle> He wears a business suit and a fedora to fly it. It's the first practical helicopter.

<sigh> And in the nineteen-seventies, in the little town of Lyme, doctors puzzle over a cluster of children with swollen joints. They trace it to tick bites. The illness is named after the town. Lyme disease.

## Part 5F

<gasp> In nineteen fifty-four, in Groton, the Navy launches the world's first nuclear-powered submarine. The Nautilus. <short pause> It can stay underwater for weeks. In nineteen fifty-eight, it sails under the ice of the North Pole.

Connecticut's shipyard in Groton still builds America's submarines.

## Part 5G (quiet style)

On July sixth, nineteen forty-four, during an afternoon show in Hartford, the big top of the Ringling Brothers circus catches fire.

<breath> The canvas has been waterproofed with wax and gasoline. It burns in minutes.

<long pause> One hundred and sixty-seven people are killed. Many of them children.

## Part 5H (quiet style)

On December fourteenth, twenty twelve, a gunman attacks Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut.

<long pause> Twenty children and six adults are killed.

## Part 5I

So how does one of the smallest states end up insuring the world?

<whispers> It starts with ships that never came home.

Connecticut's merchants couldn't afford to lose a ship. So they shared the risk. <short pause> That habit grew into fire insurance, then a reputation for paying when others wouldn't. And Hartford became the city that promises to pay when things go wrong.

<breath> The Constitution State built its fortune on keeping its word.

<chuckle> And on the occasional wooden nutmeg.
