# The Entire History of Louisiana

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,400 spoken words, so roughly 18 to 19 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Warm, playful and quick for the first four facts; slow and serious from "But New Orleans"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller, like describing a living river. |
| Part 2 | Brisk and wry: a colony sold with lies and passed between kings. |

## Hook

Jazz? <short pause> Born in New Orleans.

Tabasco sauce? Made on one little salt-dome island in Louisiana since eighteen sixty-eight.

And the Louisiana Purchase? <chuckle> It wasn't really Louisiana. It was a chunk of fifteen future states, bought from Napoleon for about three cents an acre.

In Louisiana, there are no counties. They're called parishes. And the law still has a French accent.

<long pause> But New Orleans was also the biggest slave market in America. And today, about half of the city sits below sea level.

<short pause> So how does a city that probably shouldn't exist, built on mud, become the place that gave America its music?

<whispers> It starts with a river that refuses to stay still.

## Part 1: First Louisianans

Louisiana is made by a river.

For thousands of years, the Mississippi has carried mud from half a continent. From the Rockies. From Minnesota. From Pennsylvania. And it drops that mud where it meets the sea.

<short pause> Layer by layer, it builds land.

Then, every thousand years or so, the river gets bored. <chuckle> It jumps to a new, shorter path to the sea, and starts building somewhere else. The whole south of Louisiana is made of old deltas the river built and abandoned. Like a hand, laying down fingers of land, one after another.

<breath> Remember that. The river moves. That's going to matter a lot.

<short pause> And it's a big river. The Mississippi gathers water from thirty-one states and two Canadian provinces. The Ojibwe called it Misi-ziibi. <short pause> The great river. Every drop of rain that falls between the Rocky Mountains and the Appalachians has a good chance of ending up... right here.

## Part 1B

The people who lived here built too.

In north Louisiana, at a place called Watson Brake, people built a ring of earth mounds around five and a half thousand years ago. <gasp> Older than the pyramids of Egypt. It's among the oldest known mound complexes in North America.

And at Poverty Point, around three and a half thousand years ago, people built giant curved ridges of earth, the size of a small town. They traded for stone from as far away as the Great Lakes. Today it's a World Heritage Site.

Later came the nations Europeans would meet: the Chitimacha, the Houma, the Natchez, the Caddo, the Choctaw, the Tunica and many more.

## Part 2: New France

In sixteen eighty-two, a French explorer, René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle, paddles all the way down the Mississippi to the Gulf. He plants a cross, and claims everything the river drains for France.

<chuckle> That's about a third of what's now the United States. Claimed by one man with a cross.

He names it for his king. Louis the Fourteenth.

<short pause> La Louisiane.

Seventeen years later, two French brothers, Iberville and Bienville, sail into the mouth of the Mississippi to build the colony.

<chuckle> They camp, for a night, on a little spot of land on the river. It happens to be the day of a French carnival holiday. So they name the place Point du Mardi Gras.

<short pause> Fat Tuesday has been part of Louisiana since its very first week.

## Part 2B

In seventeen eighteen, the French found a city on a curve of the river, a few miles from the Gulf. The ground is soggy. The place floods. There are snakes, alligators, and mosquitoes everywhere.

<tsk> Even the engineers say it's a terrible spot.

But it controls the river. Everything going in or out of the middle of North America has to pass by.

They name it after a French duke. La Nouvelle-Orléans. New Orleans.

The city is so shaped by the river's curve that people still call it the Crescent City.

## Part 2C

Now, nobody in France wants to move to a swamp. So a Scottish banker named John Law, who runs France's finances, launches a giant advertising campaign.

Louisiana, his posters say, is a paradise. Mountains of gold and silver. Friendly people. Perfect weather.

<laugh> None of it is true.

Investors rush to buy shares in his Mississippi Company. The price goes up, and up, and up. In seventeen twenty, it all collapses. Thousands of people in France are ruined. <short pause> It's one of the first great stock market bubbles in history.

So France starts sending people who don't get a choice. Prisoners. Debtors. And later, young women from orphanages and convents, sent to marry the colonists. Each one carries her belongings in a small chest. <chuckle> New Orleans still calls them the casket girls.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

The French bring enslaved Africans to build the colony. Thousands of them, many from the rice-growing coast of West Africa.

<breath> In seventeen twenty-four, France passes a set of rules for slavery called the Code Noir. The Black Code.

And yet, on Sunday afternoons in New Orleans, enslaved people gather in a field at the edge of town to drum, sing and dance. It becomes known as Congo Square.

<long pause> Remember that place. It matters later.

## Part 2E

Then Louisiana gets passed around like a parcel.

In seventeen sixty-two, France secretly gives it to Spain. <short pause> The Spanish rule for almost forty years.

A Spanish governor, Bernardo de Gálvez, even helps the American Revolution. He sends money, guns and gunpowder up the Mississippi to the rebels, and attacks British forts along the Gulf. <chuckle> Galveston, Texas, is named after him.

Then, in seventeen eighty-eight and seventeen ninety-four, two great fires burn most of New Orleans. So the famous French Quarter, with its iron balconies and courtyards? <short pause> Most of its oldest buildings were actually built by the Spanish.

## Part 2F

Meanwhile, French-speaking families from Acadia, in what's now eastern Canada, are thrown out of their homes by the British. Their villages are burned. Families are split onto different ships.

<breath> Over many years, thousands of them make the long journey to Louisiana's bayous and prairies.

They keep their language, their music, their fiddles and their food. Acadian slowly becomes... <short pause> Cajun.

In eighteen hundred, Spain secretly gives Louisiana back to France. To Napoleon.

<short pause> And Napoleon has a problem.

## Part 3: The Purchase

Napoleon's richest colony is Saint-Domingue, in the Caribbean. It produces a huge share of the world's sugar and coffee, with the labour of hundreds of thousands of enslaved people.

And there, the enslaved have risen up. And they're winning. They'll become the free nation of Haiti.

<gasp> Napoleon sends a giant army to crush them. It's destroyed by war and yellow fever.

Without that colony, Louisiana is far less useful to him. And he needs money for a war with Britain.

## Part 3B

Meanwhile, President Thomas Jefferson only wants one thing. New Orleans. The port at the bottom of the river. He sends diplomats to Paris with permission to pay up to ten million dollars for the city.

And Napoleon's minister says: <short pause> how about... all of it?

<laugh> The American diplomats are stunned. They don't even have permission. They say yes anyway.

In eighteen-oh-three, for about fifteen million dollars, the United States doubles in size. Land that will become all or part of fifteen states. <gasp> About three cents an acre.

One of the Americans says it's the noblest work of their whole lives.

<short pause> Some historians say America owes that bargain partly to the people of Haiti.

In eighteen twelve, the southern piece becomes the state of Louisiana. The eighteenth state. And it keeps its French-style civil law. And its parishes.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

In January eighteen eleven, just upriver from New Orleans, enslaved people rise up.

<breath> Led by a man named Charles Deslondes, they march toward the city, along the river road. Hundreds join them.

It's the largest slave revolt in American history.

<long pause> Militia and soldiers crush it within days. Dozens are killed. Their heads are put on poles along the river road, as a warning.

## Part 3D

And in January eighteen fifteen, the British attack New Orleans.

Andrew Jackson defends the city with an unlikely army: soldiers, free men of colour, Choctaw warriors, Tennessee and Kentucky frontiersmen... <chuckle> and pirates, led by the smuggler Jean Lafitte.

The British march across open sugar-cane fields, straight at Jackson's line. <gasp> In about half an hour, they lose two thousand men. The Americans lose a few dozen.

<laugh> One small problem. The war was already over. The peace treaty had been signed two weeks earlier, in Europe. Nobody here knew yet.

<short pause> So far: a river that moves, a city in a swamp, four flags, the biggest land deal in history, and a battle won after the war was over.

<breath> But the river is about to make this city very rich. And very cruel.

## Part 4: Boom

In eighteen eleven, the first steamboat on the Mississippi, named the New Orleans, chugs down the river from Pittsburgh. <gasp> On the way, the most powerful earthquakes in eastern American history hit, near New Madrid. The river boils. Banks collapse. The boat keeps going.

<short pause> Soon, hundreds of steamboats are on the river. The Mississippi becomes a highway.

Cotton and sugar pour down to New Orleans. By eighteen forty, it's the third-biggest city in America and one of the busiest ports in the world.

<chuckle> Its sugar comes from a Louisiana discovery. In seventeen ninety-five, a planter named Étienne de Boré figures out how to turn Louisiana cane juice into crystal sugar. People crowd around his kettle to watch the sugar form.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

That wealth is built on slavery.

Planters in Louisiana grow sugar and cotton, and they need ever more enslaved workers. Since importing people from Africa is now banned, they're bought from other states. Forced to walk south, in chains, or shipped down the coast.

<long pause> New Orleans becomes the largest slave market in the United States. More than one hundred and thirty-five thousand people are bought and sold here.

Families are separated forever.

## Part 4C

At the same time, New Orleans has the largest community of free people of colour in the South. Musicians. Builders. Doctors. Business owners. Poets.

<short pause> Both things are true at once.

<short pause> In eighteen forty-five, free Creole poets in New Orleans publish a book of their poems, in French. Les Cenelles. It's the first collection of poetry by African American writers ever published in the United States.

## Part 4D

And in the same years, the most famous woman in New Orleans is a free Creole hairdresser named Marie Laveau.

<whispers> They call her the Voodoo Queen.

Voodoo, or Vodou, came with enslaved Africans and with refugees from Haiti. Marie Laveau leads ceremonies at Congo Square and on the shore of Lake Pontchartrain. She nurses the sick in yellow fever epidemics. Rich white families come to her for advice. And charms.

<chuckle> People still leave gifts on her tomb. Even though the cemetery has asked them, very politely, to stop.

## Part 4E (quiet style)

And every summer, New Orleans faces another killer. Yellow fever.

<breath> In eighteen fifty-three, it kills around eight thousand people in New Orleans alone. Carts go street to street, collecting the dead.

Nobody yet knows it's spread by mosquitoes.

## Part 4F

In eighteen fifty-seven, a group of New Orleans men form a secret society, the Mistick Krewe of Comus, and hold a night parade on Mardi Gras with floats and torches.

<chuckle> Krewes have been parading ever since. Throwing beads. Coconuts. Shoes.

## Part 5: War and after

In eighteen sixty-one, Louisiana leaves the Union. But the Union navy captures New Orleans in April eighteen sixty-two, early in the war. <short pause> The South's biggest city falls with barely a fight.

After the war, for a few years, Black Louisianans vote, hold office, and help write a new constitution. In eighteen seventy-two, P. B. S. Pinchback becomes the first Black governor of any American state.

<sigh> Then white rule returns, often through violence.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

<breath> In July eighteen sixty-six, a convention meets in New Orleans to give Black men the vote. A white mob, joined by police, attacks the building and the Black marchers outside. Dozens are killed.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-three, at Colfax, a white militia attacks Black men defending a courthouse. Many are killed after they surrender. It's one of the worst massacres of the whole Reconstruction era.

## Part 5C

And in eighteen ninety-two, a New Orleans shoemaker named Homer Plessy boards a whites-only train car on purpose, to challenge the law. A group of Creole activists planned it carefully.

The case goes to the Supreme Court. Plessy versus Ferguson.

<long pause> In eighteen ninety-six, the Court rules against him. And makes "separate but equal" the law of the land for almost sixty years.

## Part 5D

But out of all of this... comes music.

<short pause> Remember Congo Square? African rhythms. Add brass bands, from parades and funerals. Church music. Blues from the cotton fields. Caribbean beats. And the music of Creole musicians trained in classical music.

Around nineteen hundred, in the dance halls of New Orleans, it all melts together.

<gasp> Jazz.

A cornet player named Buddy Bolden plays it louder than anyone. They say you could hear him across the river.

Jelly Roll Morton plays it on piano, and claims he invented it. <chuckle> He didn't. But he was very good.

## Part 5E

A lot of early jazz is played in the dance halls and saloons of a district called Storyville. It's the one part of New Orleans where certain illegal things are allowed. <tsk> In nineteen seventeen, the navy forces the city to close it.

The musicians scatter. And they take jazz with them.

<short pause> The same year, a band from New Orleans makes the first jazz record ever sold. It sells over a million copies. <sigh> But it's a white band, copying music Black musicians had been playing for years.

## Part 5F

And in nineteen-oh-one, a boy is born in a poor neighbourhood of New Orleans. Louis Armstrong.

He learns to play the cornet in a home for boys, after he's arrested for firing a pistol into the air on New Year's Eve.

<short pause> He'll take jazz to the whole world.

Jazz rides the steamboats up the Mississippi, to Memphis, St. Louis and Chicago. The river that carried the cotton... now carries the music.

<short pause> And it doesn't stop at jazz. In the nineteen fifties, in a tiny recording studio on Rampart Street, Fats Domino records hit after hit. A young singer named Little Richard walks in one day in nineteen fifty-five, and screams out a song called Tutti Frutti. <laugh> Rock and roll has some of its very first records... made in New Orleans.

<short pause> Up in Shreveport, a radio show called the Louisiana Hayride gives a nervous young singer from Memphis one of his first big stages in nineteen fifty-four. Elvis Presley. <chuckle> The crowds won't stop screaming for more after his shows. So the announcer has to tell them: Elvis has left the building.

## Part 6: The Kingfish

In nineteen twenty-eight, Louisiana elects a governor unlike any other. Huey Long. The Kingfish.

He promises "Every man a king". He builds roads, bridges, free schoolbooks, hospitals. He takes on the oil companies. <tsk> He also runs the state almost like a dictator.

He builds the tallest state capitol in America, in Baton Rouge. And he plans to run for president.

<long pause> In nineteen thirty-five, he's shot... inside that capitol. He dies two days later.

## Part 6B

And the river?

In nineteen twenty-seven, the Mississippi floods like never before. To save New Orleans, officials dynamite a levee downstream, and flood the homes of poorer parishes instead. <sigh> Many of those families are never properly paid back.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

And the Cajuns face their own loss.

<breath> In nineteen twenty-one, Louisiana's new constitution makes English the only language of its schools. Cajun children who speak French in class are punished. Some are made to write "I will not speak French on the school grounds" over and over.

Many grow up ashamed of their own language, and don't teach it to their children.

<short pause> In nineteen sixty-eight, Louisiana finally creates an agency to bring French back. Today, children can learn in French again, in immersion schools across Acadiana.

## Part 6D

Then engineers notice something terrifying.

<short pause> The Mississippi wants to move again. Just like it always has. There's a shorter, steeper path to the sea, down a smaller river called the Atchafalaya. And every year, more of the Mississippi's water is sliding into it.

If the river jumps, New Orleans and Baton Rouge are left on a stagnant, salty dead end. Their ports, their water supply, their factories... gone.

So in nineteen sixty-three, the Army Corps of Engineers finishes a giant set of gates and walls at a place called Old River.

<short pause> Its job? To hold the Mississippi River where it is. Forever.



In nineteen seventy-three, a flood nearly tears the structure apart. <phew> It holds. Barely. Engineers build more.

So far... it's working.

## Part 6E

Louisiana also finds oil. On land, then out in the Gulf. Offshore platforms rise on the horizon. Pipelines and refineries line the river between Baton Rouge and New Orleans.

The canals dug for oil and shipping slice through the marshes. And salt water creeps in.

## Part 6F (quiet style)

On August twenty-ninth, two thousand five, Hurricane Katrina hits.

<long pause> The levees around New Orleans fail.

About eighty percent of the city floods.

More than one thousand eight hundred people die across the region. Most of them in Louisiana.

<breath> Thousands wait for days on rooftops, and at the Superdome, for help that comes far too slowly.

## Part 6G (quiet style)

Five years later, in April twenty ten, the Deepwater Horizon oil rig explodes in the Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers are killed.

For eighty-seven days, oil pours into the sea, and washes into Louisiana's marshes.

## Part 7: Today

New Orleans came back. Not all of it, and not everyone. But it came back.

<gasp> In twenty ten, the New Orleans Saints won the Super Bowl. People danced in the streets for days.

The music never stopped. Brass bands still lead funerals and second-line parades. Mardi Gras parades still roll every year. Cajun and Creole cooking, gumbo, jambalaya, crawfish, spread across the world.

<chuckle> Louisiana grows about nine out of every ten crawfish eaten in America. In spring, people boil them by the sackful, with corn, potatoes and spice, and dump them onto newspaper-covered tables.

And on Avery Island, the McIlhenny family still makes Tabasco, on top of a giant dome of salt.

## Part 7B

But Louisiana is still losing land.

The levees keep the river from flooding. But they also keep it from spreading its mud. <short pause> Without new mud, the delta the river built is sinking and washing away.

<gasp> Roughly a football field of Louisiana disappears every couple of hours. Some towns are already gone. In two thousand sixteen, the people of Isle de Jean Charles became some of the first in America to get government money to move their whole community away from rising water.

So engineers have drawn up plans to cut openings in the levees on purpose. To let the river's mud flow back into the marsh. <short pause> To let the river build land again. <sigh> It's a fierce argument. Fishermen worry the fresh water will ruin their oyster beds. The biggest project has been put on hold.

## Part 7C

So how does a city that probably shouldn't exist, built on mud, become the place that gave America its music?

<short pause> It starts with a river that refuses to stay still.

The river built the land. The river made New Orleans rich, and brought slavery with that money. The river brought the people, from Africa, France, Spain, Acadia and Haiti. Their music mixed at the bottom of it, and the river carried jazz north to the world.

<long pause> And now people are holding that river in place, with gates and walls, because if it moved, they'd lose everything.

Louisiana has always lived on the edge between the water and the land. <short pause> And somehow, it always finds a way to dance.

<chuckle> Pass the Tabasco.
