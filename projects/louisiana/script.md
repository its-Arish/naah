## Hook

Jazz? Born in New Orleans.

Tabasco sauce? Made on one little salt-dome island in Louisiana since eighteen sixty-eight.

And the Louisiana Purchase? <short pause> It wasn't really Louisiana. It was a chunk of fifteen future states, bought from Napoleon for about three cents an acre.

In Louisiana, there are no counties. They're called parishes. And the law still has a French accent.

<long pause> But New Orleans was also the biggest slave market in America. And today, about half of the city sits below sea level.

<short pause> So how does a city that probably shouldn't exist, built on mud, become the place that gave America its music?

<whispers> It starts with a river that refuses to stay still.

## Part 1: First Louisianans

Louisiana is made by a river.

For thousands of years, the Mississippi has carried mud from half a continent and dropped it at the sea. Layer by layer, it built the land. Then it would swing to a new path, and build somewhere else. Every thousand years or so, the river moves.

The people who lived here built too. In north Louisiana, at a place called Watson Brake, people built a ring of earth mounds around five and a half thousand years ago. Older than the pyramids of Egypt. It's among the oldest known mound complexes in North America.

And at Poverty Point, around three and a half thousand years ago, people built giant curved ridges of earth, the size of a small town. Today it's a World Heritage Site.

Later came the nations Europeans would meet: the Chitimacha, the Houma, the Natchez, the Caddo, the Choctaw and many more.

## Part 2: New France

In sixteen eighty-two, a French explorer, René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle, paddles all the way down the Mississippi to the Gulf. He plants a cross, and claims everything the river drains for France.

He names it for his king. Louis the Fourteenth.

La Louisiane.

In seventeen eighteen, the French found a city on a curve of the river. The ground is soggy, the place floods, and there are mosquitoes everywhere. But it controls the river.

They name it after a French duke. La Nouvelle-Orléans. New Orleans.

The city is so shaped by the river's curve that people still call it the Crescent City.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The French bring enslaved Africans to build the colony. Thousands of them, many from West Africa.

In seventeen twenty-four, France passes a set of rules for slavery called the Code Noir. The Black Code.

And yet, on Sunday afternoons in New Orleans, enslaved people gather in a field at the edge of town to drum, sing and dance. It becomes known as Congo Square.

Remember that place. It matters later.

## Part 2C

Then Louisiana gets passed around like a parcel.

In seventeen sixty-two, France secretly gives it to Spain. The Spanish rule for almost forty years. The French Quarter of New Orleans? Most of its oldest buildings were actually built by the Spanish, after two great fires.

Meanwhile, French-speaking families from Acadia, in what's now Canada, are expelled by the British. Many of them make the long journey to Louisiana's swamps and prairies. Acadian becomes "Cajun".

In eighteen hundred, Spain secretly gives Louisiana back to France. To Napoleon.

And Napoleon has a problem.

## Part 3: The Purchase

Napoleon's richest colony is Saint-Domingue, in the Caribbean. And there, enslaved people have risen up and are winning. They'll become the free nation of Haiti.

Napoleon's army there is destroyed by war and yellow fever. Without that colony, Louisiana is far less useful to him. And he needs money for a war with Britain.

The Americans only want to buy New Orleans.

Napoleon says: how about... all of it?

In eighteen oh three, for about fifteen million dollars, the United States doubles in size. Land that will become all or part of fifteen states. About three cents an acre.

Some historians say America owes that bargain partly to the people of Haiti.

In eighteen twelve, the southern piece becomes the state of Louisiana. And it keeps its French-style civil law. And its parishes.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In January eighteen eleven, just upriver from New Orleans, enslaved people rise up.

Led by a man named Charles Deslondes, they march toward the city. Hundreds join them.

It's the largest slave revolt in American history.

Militia and soldiers crush it within days. Dozens are killed. Their heads are put on poles along the river road, as a warning.

## Part 3C

And in January eighteen fifteen, the British attack New Orleans.

Andrew Jackson defends the city with an unlikely army: soldiers, free men of colour, Choctaw warriors, frontiersmen... and pirates, led by the smuggler Jean Lafitte.

They crush the British.

<chuckle> One small problem. The war was already over. The peace treaty had been signed two weeks earlier in Europe. Nobody here knew yet.

## Part 4: Boom

Steamboats arrive. And the river becomes a highway.

Cotton and sugar pour down the Mississippi. By eighteen forty, New Orleans is the third-biggest city in America and one of the busiest ports in the world.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

That wealth is built on slavery.

Planters in Louisiana grow sugar and cotton, and they need ever more enslaved workers. Since importing people from Africa is now banned, they're bought from other states.

New Orleans becomes the largest slave market in the United States. More than one hundred and thirty-five thousand people are bought and sold here.

Families are separated forever.

At the same time, New Orleans has the largest community of free people of colour in the South. Musicians. Builders. Business owners.

Both things are true at once.

## Part 5: War and after

In eighteen sixty-one, Louisiana leaves the Union. But the Union navy captures New Orleans in April eighteen sixty-two, early in the war.

After the war, for a few years, Black Louisianans vote, hold office, and help write a new constitution. In eighteen seventy-two, P. B. S. Pinchback becomes the first Black governor of any American state.

Then white rule returns. And in eighteen ninety-two, a man named Homer Plessy boards a whites-only train car in New Orleans on purpose, to challenge the law.

The case goes to the Supreme Court. Plessy versus Ferguson. And in eighteen ninety-six, the Court rules against him, and makes "separate but equal" the law of the land for almost sixty years.

## Part 5B: Jazz

But out of all of this... comes music.

Remember Congo Square? African rhythms. Add brass bands from Europe. Church music. Blues from the cotton fields. Caribbean beats. And the music of Creole musicians trained in classical music.

Around nineteen hundred, in the dance halls of New Orleans, it all melts together.

Jazz.

A cornet player named Buddy Bolden plays it louder than anyone. And in nineteen oh one, a boy is born in a poor neighbourhood of New Orleans. Louis Armstrong.

He'll take jazz to the whole world.

## Part 6: The Kingfish

In nineteen twenty-eight, Louisiana elects a governor unlike any other. Huey Long. The Kingfish.

He builds roads, bridges, free schoolbooks, hospitals. He fights the oil companies. He also runs the state almost like a dictator.

He builds the tallest state capitol in America, in Baton Rouge.

And in nineteen thirty-five, he's shot... inside that capitol.

## Part 6B

And the river?

In nineteen twenty-seven, the Mississippi floods like never before. To save New Orleans, officials dynamite a levee downstream, and flood the homes of poorer parishes instead.

Then engineers notice something terrifying. The Mississippi wants to move again. Into a shorter path, called the Atchafalaya. If it does, New Orleans and Baton Rouge are left on a dying river.

So in nineteen sixty-three, the Army Corps of Engineers finishes a giant set of gates at a place called Old River.

Its job? To hold the Mississippi River where it is. Forever.

So far... it's working.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

On August twenty-ninth, two thousand five, Hurricane Katrina hits.

The levees around New Orleans fail.

About eighty percent of the city floods.

More than one thousand eight hundred people die across the region. Most of them in Louisiana.

Thousands wait for days on rooftops, and at the Superdome, for help that comes far too slowly.

## Part 7: Today

New Orleans came back. Not all of it, and not everyone. But it came back.

In twenty ten, the New Orleans Saints won the Super Bowl. People danced in the streets for days.

The music never stopped. Mardi Gras parades still roll every year. Cajun and Creole cooking, gumbo, jambalaya, crawfish, spread across the world.

But Louisiana is still losing land. Without the river's mud to rebuild it, the coast is sinking and washing away. Roughly a football field of land disappears every couple of hours.

## Part 7B

So how does a city that probably shouldn't exist, built on mud, become the place that gave America its music?

It starts with a river that refuses to stay still. The river built the land. The river made New Orleans rich, and brought slavery with that money. The river brought the music up from the Gulf, and carried jazz north to the world.

And now people are holding that river in place, with gates and walls, because if it moved, they'd lose everything.

<short pause> Louisiana has always lived on the edge between the water and the land. And somehow, it always finds a way to dance.

<chuckle> Pass the Tabasco.
