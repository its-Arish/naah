## Hook

The keyboard you're probably looking at right now? <short pause> Its QWERTY layout was invented in Wisconsin.

Harley-Davidson? Wisconsin. The first kindergarten in America? Also Wisconsin.

Wisconsin makes about a quarter of all the cheese in America. And its football fans wear foam cheese on their heads.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-one, on the same night as the Great Chicago Fire, a much deadlier fire in Wisconsin killed well over a thousand people. Almost nobody remembers it.

<short pause> So how does a state of wheat farmers become America's Dairyland?

<whispers> It starts with a tiny bug.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Wisconsin sits between two giant lakes, Lake Michigan and Lake Superior, with forests, rivers and thousands of smaller lakes.

A thousand years ago, people here shape the earth into giant animals. Birds with wings spread wide. Bears. Panthers. Some longer than a football field. Effigy mounds. Wisconsin has more of them than anywhere else.

Later, the land belongs to the Ho-Chunk, the Menominee, the Ojibwe, the Potawatomi and others. The Menominee have lived here so long that their stories say they began here.

## Part 1B

In sixteen thirty-four, a French explorer named Jean Nicolet paddles into Green Bay. He's hoping he's found a route to China.

<chuckle> So he puts on a fancy Chinese silk robe to meet the people there. And fires his pistols in the air.

The people he meets are Ho-Chunk. Not Chinese.

## Part 1C

French fur traders follow. Then British. Then Americans.

In the eighteen-twenties, miners rush to southwest Wisconsin for lead. Some are in such a hurry, they don't build houses. They dig holes in the hillsides and live in them, like badgers.

That's why Wisconsin is the Badger State.

## Part 1D (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-two, the Sauk leader Black Hawk leads his people back across the Mississippi to their old homeland. The army chases them.

At the Bad Axe River, as they try to cross back, soldiers and a gunboat fire on them. Hundreds of men, women and children are killed.

## Part 2: Wheat

In eighteen forty-eight, Wisconsin becomes the thirtieth state.

Immigrants pour in. Germans, Norwegians, Poles, Swiss, Irish. In Milwaukee, Germans open breweries. Pabst. Schlitz. Miller. Milwaukee becomes a beer capital.

And on the farms, everyone grows the same thing. Wheat.

By the eighteen-sixties, Wisconsin is one of the biggest wheat states in America.

## Part 2B

Now, the bug.

Growing wheat year after year wears out the soil. And then the chinch bugs arrive. Tiny insects, smaller than a grain of rice. Millions of them. They suck the juice out of wheat plants until they wither.

Harvests crash. Farmers are in trouble.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

On October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, after a long drought, small fires in the forests of northeast Wisconsin are whipped up by a powerful wind.

They join into a firestorm. It races through the lumber town of Peshtigo.

People run into the river to escape. The flames sweep over the water.

Somewhere between twelve hundred and twenty-five hundred people are killed. It's the deadliest wildfire in American history.

That same night, Chicago burns too. And the newspapers mostly write about Chicago.

## Part 3: Dairyland

So Wisconsin's farmers need a new plan.

A newspaper editor named William Dempster Hoard has one. Stop growing wheat. Raise cows. Treat them like members of the family. Grow hay and corn to feed them. Let them put the goodness back into the soil.

The Swiss, German and Scandinavian immigrants already know how to make cheese and butter. Wisconsin's cool, wet climate grows great grass.

## Part 3B

But there's a problem. Farmers cheat. Some water down their milk before selling it.

In eighteen ninety, a professor at the University of Wisconsin, Stephen Babcock, invents a quick, cheap test to measure how much butterfat is in milk. Now buyers can pay for quality.

And Babcock refuses to patent it. He gives it to the world for free.

## Part 3C

Cheese factories pop up at country crossroads all over the state. Thousands of them.

By the early nineteen-hundreds, Wisconsin is the top dairy state in America. America's Dairyland. It's even on the licence plates.

## Part 4: Progressives

Wisconsin is full of new ideas.

In eighteen fifty-four, in a little schoolhouse in Ripon, Wisconsin, people meet to start a new anti-slavery party. It's one of the places that claims to be the birthplace of the Republican Party.

In eighteen fifty-six, in Watertown, a German immigrant named Margarethe Schurz opens America's first kindergarten.

In eighteen sixty-eight, in Milwaukee, a printer named Christopher Sholes invents a practical typewriter. He arranges the keys in a strange order. Q, W, E, R, T, Y. It's still on your keyboard.

## Part 4B

In nineteen-oh-three, two young men in a little wooden shed in Milwaukee build a motorcycle. William Harley and Arthur Davidson.

Wisconsin also passes America's first unemployment insurance law, in nineteen thirty-two. And a Wisconsin senator, Gaylord Nelson, founds Earth Day in nineteen seventy.

And the magician Harry Houdini grows up in Appleton, Wisconsin.

## Part 5: Today

In nineteen nineteen, in the little city of Green Bay, a meatpacking company pays for football uniforms. The team is named after it. The Packers.

Today, the Green Bay Packers are owned by their fans. Hundreds of thousands of them. The only community-owned team in the NFL.

And in nineteen eighty-seven, a fan cuts a hat out of a foam couch cushion, shaped like a wedge of cheese.

The Cheesehead.

## Part 5B

So how does a state of wheat farmers become America's Dairyland?

It starts with a tiny bug.

The chinch bugs, and the worn-out soil, wrecked Wisconsin's wheat. Farmers had to find something else. With the help of immigrants who knew cheese, a newspaper editor who loved cows, and a professor who gave his invention away, they built the biggest dairy state in America.

<short pause> A bug smaller than a grain of rice changed the whole state.

<chuckle> And it's the reason Wisconsin's football fans have cheese on their heads.
