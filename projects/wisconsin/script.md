# The Entire History of Wisconsin

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen seventy-one"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, woodsy storyteller, with a wink. |
| Part 2 | Bustling immigrant energy, then brave, then worried. |
| Part 3 | Folksy, practical farm voice, proud. |
| Part 4 | Clever, reforming, inventive. |
| Part 5 | Cheerful, cheesy and proud; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1634 · Fur and Lead 1634 to 1848 · Wheat 1848 to 1880 · Dairyland 1880 to 1920 · Progressives 1900 to 1960 · Today 1960 to now.

## Hook

The keyboard you're probably looking at right now? <short pause> Its QWERTY layout was invented in Wisconsin.

Harley-Davidson? Wisconsin. The first kindergarten in America? Also Wisconsin.

<chuckle> Wisconsin makes about a quarter of all the cheese in America. And its football fans wear foam cheese on their heads.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-one, on the same night as the Great Chicago Fire, a much deadlier fire in Wisconsin killed well over a thousand people. Almost nobody remembers it.

<short pause> So how does a state of wheat farmers become America's Dairyland?

<whispers> It starts with a tiny bug.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Wisconsin sits between two giant lakes, Lake Michigan and Lake Superior, with forests, rivers and thousands of smaller lakes, scooped out by the glaciers.

<gasp> But the glaciers somehow missed one corner. The southwest. It's called the Driftless Area. Steep hills, deep valleys and rock towers, that look nothing like the rest of the Midwest.

<short pause> On the east, a thumb of land sticks out into Lake Michigan. Door County. Between its tip and the islands beyond is a strait of wild currents, where hundreds of ships have sunk. <whispers> The French called it the Door of Death. The name stuck.

## Part 1B

A thousand years ago, people here shape the earth into giant animals. Birds with wings spread wide. Bears. Panthers. Some longer than a football field. <short pause> Effigy mounds. Wisconsin once had thousands of them. More than anywhere else on Earth.

At a place called Aztalan, people from the great city of Cahokia build a town with a tall wooden wall and flat-topped pyramids of earth.

Later, the land belongs to the Ho-Chunk, the Menominee, the Ojibwe, the Potawatomi and others. The Menominee have lived here so long that their stories say they began here, at the mouth of the Menominee River.

## Part 1C

In sixteen thirty-four, a French explorer named Jean Nicolet paddles into Green Bay. He's hoping he's found a route to China.

<chuckle> So he puts on a fancy Chinese silk robe, embroidered with flowers and birds, to meet the people there. And fires his pistols in the air.

<laugh> The people he meets are Ho-Chunk. Not Chinese.

<short pause> In sixteen seventy-three, Marquette and Jolliet paddle up the Fox River, carry their canoes over a short stretch of land, and float down the Wisconsin River... into the Mississippi. A water road from the Great Lakes to the heart of the continent.

## Part 1D

French fur traders follow. Then British. Then Americans.

In the eighteen-twenties, miners rush to southwest Wisconsin for lead. <chuckle> Some are in such a hurry, they don't build houses. They dig holes in the hillsides and live in them, like badgers.

That's why Wisconsin is the Badger State.

## Part 1E (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-two, the Sauk leader Black Hawk leads his people back across the Mississippi to their old homeland. The army chases them across Wisconsin.

<breath> At the Bad Axe River, as they try to cross back, soldiers and a steamboat with a cannon fire on them.

<long pause> Hundreds of men, women and children are killed.

## Part 1F (quiet style)

<breath> The government also orders the Ho-Chunk out of Wisconsin. It removes them again and again, to Iowa, Minnesota, South Dakota, Nebraska.

<long pause> And again and again, many of them walk back home.

<short pause> In the end, the government gives up, and lets them stay. The Ho-Chunk Nation is still in Wisconsin today.

## Part 2: Wheat

In eighteen forty-eight, Wisconsin becomes the thirtieth state.

<gasp> Immigrants pour in. Germans, Norwegians, Poles, Swiss, Irish, Dutch, Czechs. In some towns, nobody speaks English at all.

In Milwaukee, Germans open breweries. Pabst. Schlitz. Miller. Blatz. <chuckle> Milwaukee becomes the beer capital of America. One brewery even advertises itself as "the beer that made Milwaukee famous".

And on the farms, everyone grows the same thing. Wheat.

By the eighteen-sixties, Wisconsin is one of the biggest wheat states in America.

<short pause> And in the north, lumberjacks cut down the great pine forests. Logs float down every river, so many that men can walk across them like a floor.

<laugh> In eighteen ninety-three, in the lumber town of Rhinelander, a prankster claims he's captured a terrifying beast. Horns, fangs, spikes down its back, and a terrible smell. The Hodag. <chuckle> He shows it off at the county fair, hidden in a dark tent, and moves it with wires. It's carved from wood and covered in ox hide. Rhinelander still loves it. The Hodag is the high school's mascot.

## Part 2B

In eighteen fifty-four, a man who had escaped slavery, Joshua Glover, is living and working near Racine. Slave catchers find him, beat him, and lock him in the Milwaukee jail, to send him back to Missouri.

<gasp> Thousands of people from Milwaukee surround the jail. They smash the door down with a wooden beam, and free him. <phew> He escapes to Canada.

## Part 2C

That same year, in a little schoolhouse in Ripon, Wisconsin, people meet to start a new anti-slavery party. It's one of the places that claims to be the birthplace of the Republican Party.

<chuckle> In the Civil War, one Wisconsin regiment carries a live bald eagle into battle, on a perch. Old Abe. He screams and flaps his wings through the gunfire. <short pause> Confederate soldiers try to shoot him. He survives the whole war, and becomes a celebrity.

<short pause> Wisconsin men also fill the Iron Brigade, famous for their tall black hats, and for standing their ground. <sigh> At Gettysburg, on the first day, they lose about two-thirds of their men, holding back the Confederates.

## Part 2D

Now, the bug.

Growing wheat year after year wears out the soil. <groan> And then the chinch bugs arrive. Tiny insects, smaller than a grain of rice. Millions of them. They suck the juice out of wheat plants until they wither.

Harvests crash. Farmers are in trouble.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

On October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, after a long drought, small fires in the forests of northeast Wisconsin are whipped up by a powerful wind.

<breath> They join into a firestorm. It races through the lumber town of Peshtigo.

People run into the river to escape. The flames sweep over the water.

<long pause> Somewhere between twelve hundred and twenty-five hundred people are killed. It's the deadliest wildfire in American history.

That same night, Chicago burns too. And the newspapers mostly write about Chicago.

## Part 3: Dairyland

So Wisconsin's farmers need a new plan.

A newspaper editor named William Dempster Hoard has one. Stop growing wheat. Raise cows. <chuckle> Treat them like members of the family. "Speak to a cow as you would a lady," he says. Grow hay and corn to feed them. Let them put the goodness back into the soil.

The Swiss, German and Scandinavian immigrants already know how to make cheese and butter. Wisconsin's cool, wet climate grows great grass.

## Part 3B

But there's a problem. <tsk> Farmers cheat. Some water down their milk before selling it. Some skim off the cream.

In eighteen ninety, a professor at the University of Wisconsin, Stephen Babcock, invents a quick, cheap test to measure how much butterfat is in milk. Now buyers can pay for quality.

<short pause> And Babcock refuses to patent it. He gives it to the world for free.

## Part 3C

Cheese factories pop up at country crossroads all over the state. Thousands of them. <chuckle> In eighteen eighty-five, in the town of Colby, a cheesemaker invents a new, mild cheese. He names it after the town. Colby.

By the early nineteen-hundreds, Wisconsin is the top dairy state in America. America's Dairyland. It's even on the licence plates.

<laugh> Wisconsin takes butter so seriously that, for decades, it bans coloured margarine. Until nineteen sixty-seven. People drive across the border to Illinois, and smuggle margarine home in their cars.

<chuckle> Fresh cheese curds, a day old, squeak against your teeth when you bite them. Wisconsin is also one of the only places outside Europe with an official "Master Cheesemaker" programme. It takes about ten years.

## Part 4: Progressives

Wisconsin is full of new ideas.

In eighteen fifty-six, in Watertown, a German immigrant named Margarethe Schurz opens America's first kindergarten. <short pause> In German. For her own daughter and some cousins.

In eighteen sixty-eight, in Milwaukee, a printer named Christopher Sholes invents a practical typewriter. He arranges the keys in a strange order. Q, W, E, R, T, Y.

One popular story says he did it to slow typists down, so the keys wouldn't jam. <short pause> He sells the design to a gun maker, Remington, which starts building typewriters. <chuckle> And that strange order is still on your keyboard.

<chuckle> And in Baraboo, five brothers named Ringling start a little travelling show, in eighteen eighty-four, with a few acts and a trained horse. <gasp> It grows into the biggest circus in the world. Ringling Brothers. For decades, it spends every winter in Baraboo.

## Part 4B

At the start of the nineteen-hundreds, a fiery governor named Robert La Follette, "Fighting Bob", takes on the railroads and big businesses that control the state.

<short pause> He brings university professors in to help write laws. It's called the Wisconsin Idea. The university's work should reach every home in the state.

Wisconsin lets voters pick party candidates in primary elections. It creates one of the first workers' compensation systems. And in nineteen thirty-two, America's first unemployment insurance law.

<chuckle> Milwaukee even elects Socialist mayors, for decades. People call them "sewer socialists", because what they care about most is clean water, sewers, parks and honest government.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In May eighteen eighty-six, thousands of workers in Milwaukee go on strike, demanding an eight-hour work day.

<breath> At the Bay View steel mill, the state militia is ordered to fire on a crowd of marching workers.

<long pause> Seven people are killed, including a schoolboy.

## Part 4D

In nineteen-oh-three, two young men in a little wooden shed in Milwaukee build a motorcycle. William Harley and Arthur Davidson. <laugh> The shed is about ten feet by fifteen. The words "Harley-Davidson Motor Company" are painted on the door.

<short pause> The magician Harry Houdini grows up in Appleton, Wisconsin. The architect Frank Lloyd Wright is born in Richland Center, and builds his famous home, Taliesin, in the Wisconsin hills. Orson Welles is born in Kenosha.

And a little girl born in a log cabin near Pepin, Wisconsin, later writes about it, in "Little House in the Big Woods". Laura Ingalls Wilder.

<chuckle> In eighteen eighty-three, an Austrian immigrant in Sheboygan, John Kohler, takes an iron horse trough... coats it in white enamel, puts four legs on it, and sells it as a bathtub. <laugh> "A horse trough or hog scalder, when finished," his catalogue says, "a bathtub." Kohler is still making bathrooms today.

## Part 4E

<tsk> In the nineteen-fifties, a Wisconsin senator, Joseph McCarthy, claims that communists have infiltrated the U.S. government. He accuses hundreds of people, often with little or no evidence. <sigh> Careers are ruined. In nineteen fifty-four, the Senate censures him.

<short pause> And another Wisconsin senator, Gaylord Nelson, does something very different. In nineteen seventy, he founds Earth Day. Twenty million Americans take part.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

In August nineteen seventy, during protests against the Vietnam War, four young men set off a huge truck bomb outside a building at the University of Wisconsin in Madison, where the army funds research.

<breath> They think the building is empty at night.

<long pause> It isn't. A young physics researcher, a father of three, is killed.

## Part 5: Today

In nineteen nineteen, in the little city of Green Bay, a meatpacking company pays for football uniforms. The team is named after it. The Packers.

<gasp> Today, the Green Bay Packers are owned by their fans. Hundreds of thousands of them. The only community-owned team in the NFL. <chuckle> Green Bay is by far the smallest city with a team in the league.

<groan> In nineteen sixty-seven, the Packers play for the championship in Green Bay at thirteen degrees below zero. The referees' whistles freeze to their lips. The Ice Bowl.

<chuckle> Milwaukee gets baseball fever, too. In nineteen fifty-seven, the Milwaukee Braves, with a young slugger named Hank Aaron, win the World Series.

## Part 5B

<laugh> And in nineteen eighty-seven, a fan cuts a hat out of a foam couch cushion, shaped like a wedge of cheese.

The Cheesehead.

<chuckle> Wisconsin grows more cranberries than any other state. The Wisconsin Dells calls itself the waterpark capital of the world. And every summer, in Oshkosh, more than ten thousand airplanes fly in for one of the biggest air shows on Earth.

<laugh> On Friday nights, all over Wisconsin, people head to the fish fry. Fried fish, potato pancakes, coleslaw. Often at an old-fashioned supper club, with a brandy old fashioned. <short pause> It's said that Wisconsin drinks more brandy than almost anywhere on Earth.

<short pause> And in a cold winter, on Lake Superior, the frozen waves and dripping cliffs of the Apostle Islands turn into ice caves, full of giant icicles. When the ice is thick enough, thousands of people walk out across the frozen lake to see them.

## Part 5C

So how does a state of wheat farmers become America's Dairyland?

<whispers> It starts with a tiny bug.

The chinch bugs, and the worn-out soil, wrecked Wisconsin's wheat. Farmers had to find something else. <short pause> With the help of immigrants who knew cheese, a newspaper editor who loved cows, and a professor who gave his invention away, they built the biggest dairy state in America.

<breath> A bug smaller than a grain of rice changed the whole state.

<chuckle> And it's the reason Wisconsin's football fans have cheese on their heads.
