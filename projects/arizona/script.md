## Hook

London Bridge? <short pause> It's in Arizona. A man bought it in nineteen sixty-eight and rebuilt it in the desert.

The gunfight at the O.K. Corral? It didn't happen at the O.K. Corral.

Arizona doesn't change its clocks for daylight saving. Except the Navajo Nation does. Except the Hopi land in the middle of the Navajo Nation doesn't.

And the deepest rocks in the Grand Canyon are almost two billion years old.

<long pause> Phoenix gets about eight inches of rain a year. And it's the fifth biggest city in America.

<short pause> So how does a giant city grow in one of the driest places in the country?

<whispers> It starts with canals nobody had used for four hundred years.

## Part 1: First Arizonans

Arizona is a land written in stone.

The Colorado River has spent millions of years cutting down through layer after layer of rock. The result is the Grand Canyon. A mile deep. Its oldest rocks at the bottom are almost two billion years old. Nearly half the age of the Earth.

People have lived in Arizona for at least twelve thousand years.

In the north, the ancestors of the Hopi and Pueblo peoples build stone towns on mesas and in cliffs. The Hopi village of Old Oraibi has been lived in since around eleven hundred. It's one of the oldest continuously inhabited towns in the United States.

And in the valley where Phoenix is today, lived a people we call the Hohokam.

The Hohokam do something astonishing. With stone tools and digging sticks, they dig canals. Lots of them. More than a hundred miles of main canals, carrying river water across the desert to fields of corn, beans, squash and cotton.

It's one of the largest irrigation systems built anywhere in the ancient Americas.

And then, around fourteen fifty... the Hohokam towns are abandoned. Drought? Floods? War? Nobody knows for sure.

The canals fill with sand. And they wait.

## Part 2: Gold Seekers

In fifteen forty, a Spanish expedition led by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado marches north from Mexico.

They're looking for the Seven Cities of Gold. A legend says there are cities up here with streets paved in gold.

They find adobe villages. No gold.

But a small group of Coronado's men are led by Hopi guides to the edge of something no European had ever seen. The Grand Canyon.

They try to climb down to the river. It looks like a little stream from the top. They give up. <chuckle> The "little stream" was the Colorado River.

The Spanish build missions in the south. San Xavier del Bac, near Tucson, the White Dove of the Desert, still stands. And in seventeen seventy-five, they build a fort that becomes the city of Tucson.

After eighteen twenty-one, it all belongs to Mexico.

Then, after the Mexican-American War, most of Arizona becomes American in eighteen forty-eight. And in eighteen fifty-three, the United States buys a strip of land along the south, including Tucson, from Mexico. Why? It's the best flat route for a railroad. The Gadsden Purchase.

## Part 3: Territory

In eighteen sixty-three, during the Civil War, Arizona becomes its own territory.

But these are the homelands of the Apache, the Navajo, the Tohono O'odham, the Akimel O'odham, the Yavapai, the Hualapai and many more.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-four, the U.S. Army forces thousands of Navajo people to walk more than three hundred miles to a camp in New Mexico.

It's called the Long Walk. Hundreds die on the way.

Four years later, the Navajo sign a treaty, and they are allowed to walk home, to a part of their old homeland.

Today, the Navajo Nation is the largest reservation in the United States.

## Part 3C

The Apache fight for decades. Leaders like Cochise and Geronimo become famous across America.

In eighteen eighty-six, Geronimo surrenders in Skeleton Canyon. It's considered the end of the major Indian Wars.

## Part 4: Wild West

Meanwhile, the canals are waiting.

In eighteen sixty-seven, a Civil War veteran and adventurer named Jack Swilling rides through the Salt River Valley. He notices long, low mounds of earth running across the desert.

Old canals. Hohokam canals.

He realises: if they could carry water once, they can carry it again. He and a crew start digging them out.

Farms appear. Pumpkins grow so big that people want to call the town Pumpkinville.

But an English settler named Darrell Duppa has a better idea. A new city, rising from the ruins of an old one.

Like the bird of legend that rises from its own ashes.

Phoenix.

## Part 4B

And then, silver. In eighteen seventy-seven, a prospector strikes silver in the south. People told him all he'd find out there was his tombstone. So that's what he names it.

Tombstone.

In October eighteen eighty-one, the Earp brothers and Doc Holliday face the Clanton and McLaury cowboys in a gunfight that lasts about thirty seconds.

It happened in a narrow lot near the back of the O.K. Corral. But the O.K. Corral made a better headline.

Then copper. Huge copper mines open at Bisbee, Jerome and Morenci. Copper wires carry electricity across America, and much of that copper comes from Arizona.

Arizona's economy becomes the five Cs. Copper, cattle, cotton, citrus... and climate.

## Part 5: Statehood

By nineteen ten, Arizona wants to be a state. It writes a constitution. It's very progressive for its time, and it lets voters remove judges they don't like.

President Taft hates that. He vetoes Arizona's statehood.

So Arizona takes the judge part out. Taft signs. On February fourteenth, nineteen twelve, Valentine's Day, Arizona becomes the forty-eighth state. The last of the lower forty-eight.

And then, a few months later, Arizona voters put the judge part right back in. <chuckle>

## Part 5B

Water is still the question.

In nineteen eleven, the Roosevelt Dam is finished on the Salt River. Phoenix finally has steady water. Later, Hoover Dam on the Colorado River. Then the Central Arizona Project, a canal more than three hundred miles long, bringing Colorado River water across the desert to Phoenix and Tucson.

Modern canals, following some of the very same paths as the Hohokam.

## Part 5C

In the Second World War, the Pacific war needs an unbreakable code.

Young Navajo men become Marines and invent one, based on their own language. The Japanese never crack it.

The Navajo Code Talkers.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

During the same war, the government forces Japanese American families from the West Coast into camps.

Two of the biggest are in Arizona, on tribal land. Poston and Gila River.

For a while, the Gila River camp is one of the biggest towns in the whole state. Behind barbed wire.

## Part 6: Sun Belt

After the war, two things change Arizona forever.

Air conditioning. And retirement.

Families and retirees pour in from the cold North. Phoenix grows from about a hundred thousand people in nineteen fifty to more than a million and a half today.

In nineteen sixty-eight, a businessman named Robert McCulloch buys the old London Bridge, ships it across the ocean in numbered blocks, and rebuilds it over a channel in the desert. Today it's one of the most visited sights in the state.

In nineteen eighty-one, Arizona judge Sandra Day O'Connor becomes the first woman on the United States Supreme Court.

## Part 6B

Today, Phoenix is the fifth biggest city in America. Arizona builds computer chips, rockets and electric cars. Millions visit the Grand Canyon every year.

But the Colorado River is shrinking. Years of drought have cut the water Arizona can take from it. The old question is back.

## Part 6C

So how does a giant city grow in one of the driest places in the country?

It starts with canals nobody had used for four hundred years. The Hohokam dug them. They waited under the sand. Jack Swilling dug them out again. And a city was named after a bird that rises from ashes.

<short pause> Arizona has always been a place where people bet everything on water. The Hohokam made that bet a thousand years ago.

Now it's Arizona's turn.

<chuckle> And if you visit London Bridge... remember, it's falling down a lot less in Arizona than it was in London.
