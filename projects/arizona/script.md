# The Entire History of Arizona

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,150 spoken words, so roughly 17 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused, playful for the first four facts; slower and serious from "Phoenix gets"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller in a canyon. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, a little wry. |
| Part 3 | Serious and steady, then dusty frontier energy. |
| Part 4 | Wild West energy, then wry, then grave. |
| Part 5 | Brisk, building, cheeky, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Bright and confident, then calm and certain, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Arizonans 11,000 BC to 1450 · Gold Seekers 1539 to 1821 · Mexico 1821 to 1853 · Territory 1853 to 1886 · Wild West 1867 to 1912 · Statehood 1912 · Water and War 1911 to 1945 · Sun Belt 1950 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

London Bridge? <short pause> It's in Arizona. A man bought it in nineteen sixty-eight and rebuilt it in the desert.

The gunfight at the O.K. Corral? <chuckle> It didn't happen at the O.K. Corral.

Arizona doesn't change its clocks for daylight saving. Except the Navajo Nation does. Except the Hopi land in the middle of the Navajo Nation doesn't.

And the deepest rocks in the Grand Canyon are almost two billion years old.

<long pause> Phoenix gets about eight inches of rain a year. And it's the fifth biggest city in America.

<short pause> So how does a giant city grow in one of the driest places in the country?

<whispers> It starts with canals nobody had used for four hundred years.

## Part 1: First Arizonans

Arizona is a land written in stone.

The Colorado River has spent millions of years cutting down through layer after layer of rock. <short pause> The result is the Grand Canyon. A mile deep. Up to eighteen miles wide. Its oldest rocks at the bottom are almost two billion years old. <gasp> Nearly half the age of the Earth.

And Arizona has scars from space. About fifty thousand years ago, a chunk of iron the size of a house slammed into the desert near Flagstaff. It left a crater almost a mile wide. <chuckle> It's so well preserved that astronauts trained there before going to the Moon.

<short pause> And in the east, there are fallen trees, more than two hundred million years old, that have turned to stone. Swirls of red, purple and yellow quartz, still in the shape of logs. <tsk> By nineteen-oh-six, visitors were carting off so much of it that the government had to protect it. The Petrified Forest.

## Part 1B

People have lived in Arizona for at least twelve thousand years.

In the San Pedro Valley, archaeologists have found the bones of mammoths, with stone spear points still among them. Ice Age hunters. <short pause> Thirteen thousand years ago.

Later, people learn to farm. Corn. Beans. Squash. In the north, the ancestors of the Hopi and the Pueblo peoples build stone towns on mesas and in cliffs.

The Hopi village of Old Oraibi has been lived in since around eleven hundred. It's one of the oldest continuously inhabited towns in the United States.

<chuckle> And in the Verde Valley, the Sinagua people build a five-storey apartment in a cliff. Settlers later name it Montezuma Castle, thinking the Aztecs built it. <tsk> The Aztecs were never here. And it's not a castle.

## Part 1C

And in the valley where Phoenix is today, there lived a people archaeologists call the Hohokam.

The Hohokam do something astonishing. <short pause> With stone tools, digging sticks and baskets, they dig canals. Lots of them.

Some are ten feet deep and dozens of feet wide. Together, the canals stretch for hundreds of miles, carrying water from the Salt and Gila rivers across the desert, to fields of corn, beans, squash and cotton.

It's one of the largest irrigation systems built anywhere in the ancient Americas.

<short pause> They build ball courts for games. They trade for shells from the Gulf of California and macaw feathers from Mexico. And at Casa Grande, they build a four-storey "Big House" of earth, with windows that line up with the sun and the moon.

<long pause> And then, around fourteen fifty... the Hohokam towns are abandoned. Drought? Floods? War? Nobody knows for sure. Their descendants, many believe, are the Akimel O'odham and Tohono O'odham peoples who still live here.

<breath> The canals fill with sand. And they wait.

## Part 2: Gold Seekers

In fifteen thirty-nine, a Spanish friar and an African explorer named Esteban march north from Mexico. Esteban, born in Morocco and enslaved, had already crossed half of North America on foot. <short pause> He goes ahead as a scout. He never comes back.

The friar returns to Mexico with a story. Somewhere up north, he says, are the Seven Cities of Gold.

<gasp> So in fifteen forty, a huge expedition sets out, led by Francisco Vázquez de Coronado. Hundreds of soldiers. More than a thousand Native allies. Horses, cattle, sheep.

They find adobe villages. <chuckle> No gold.

## Part 2B

But a small group of Coronado's men are led by Hopi guides to the edge of something no European had ever seen. <short pause> The Grand Canyon.

They try to climb down to the river. From the top, it looks like a little stream, maybe six feet across. After three days, they give up.

<laugh> The "little stream" was the Colorado River.

## Part 2C

A hundred and fifty years later, an Italian Jesuit priest named Eusebio Kino rides into southern Arizona. He rides thousands of miles on horseback. He brings wheat, fruit trees, cattle and horses to the O'odham villages. And he founds missions.

<chuckle> He also draws maps. And on his travels, he proves something mapmakers in Europe had wrong for years. California is NOT an island.

One of them, San Xavier del Bac, near Tucson, is rebuilt in white stone in the seventeen hundreds. People call it the White Dove of the Desert. <short pause> It still stands. Mass is still said there.

## Part 2D

In seventeen fifty-two, after an O'odham uprising, the Spanish build a fort at Tubac. It's the first European town in Arizona.

And in seventeen seventy-five, an Irish officer serving the King of Spain, Hugo O'Conor, picks a new spot for a fort on the Santa Cruz River. <chuckle> An Irishman, founding a Spanish fort, beside an O'odham village.

It becomes the city of Tucson.

## Part 2E

After eighteen twenty-one, it all belongs to Mexico.

Then, in the Mexican-American War, an American army battalion marches through southern Arizona, building a wagon road. <laugh> Its only real battle in the whole war is against a herd of angry wild bulls, which charge the column and gore the mules.

In eighteen forty-eight, most of Arizona becomes American. And in eighteen fifty-three, the United States buys a strip of land along the south, including Tucson, from Mexico, for ten million dollars. Why? It's the best flat route for a railroad to California. The Gadsden Purchase.

## Part 3: Territory

The army needs to get supplies across the desert. So in the eighteen fifties, someone has a brilliant idea.

<short pause> Camels.

The army imports camels from the Middle East, with a Syrian camel driver the soldiers nickname "Hi Jolly". The camels carry heavy loads, go days without water, and cross the desert easily.

<chuckle> The soldiers hate them. They spit. They bite. They scare the horses. When the Civil War starts, the camel corps is forgotten. Hi Jolly stays in Arizona. His tomb, in the little town of Quartzsite, has a camel on top.

## Part 3B

In the Civil War, Confederate soldiers ride into Arizona from Texas. In April eighteen sixty-two, at Picacho Pass, they skirmish with Union cavalry. It's the westernmost battle of the whole war.

And in eighteen sixty-three, Arizona becomes its own territory.

<chuckle> Then it can't decide where its capital should be. First Prescott. Then Tucson. Then back to Prescott. Finally, in eighteen eighty-nine, Phoenix. People call it the capital on wheels.

But these are the homelands of the Apache, the Navajo, the Tohono O'odham, the Akimel O'odham, the Yavapai, the Hualapai, the Havasupai and many more.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-four, the U.S. Army forces thousands of Navajo people to walk more than three hundred miles to a camp in New Mexico.

<breath> It's called the Long Walk. Hundreds die on the way.

Four years later, the Navajo sign a treaty, and they are allowed to walk home, to a part of their old homeland.

Today, the Navajo Nation is the largest reservation in the United States.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

In eighteen seventy-one, at Camp Grant, near Tucson, a group of Apache families have settled under the army's protection.

<breath> At dawn, a mob from Tucson, with O'odham allies, attacks the sleeping camp.

<long pause> More than a hundred people are killed. Almost all of them women and children. The attackers are put on trial. The jury takes less than twenty minutes to find them not guilty.

## Part 3E

The Apache fight for decades. Leaders like Cochise and Geronimo become famous across America. Small bands of Apache warriors run rings around thousands of soldiers in the mountains.

The army only catches up when it hires Apache scouts, who know the land as well as Geronimo does.

In eighteen eighty-six, Geronimo surrenders in Skeleton Canyon. <short pause> It's considered the end of the major Indian Wars. He's sent to prison in Florida, and never allowed to return to Arizona.

## Part 4: Wild West

<short pause> Meanwhile, the canals are waiting.

In eighteen sixty-seven, a Civil War veteran and adventurer named Jack Swilling rides through the Salt River Valley. He notices long, low mounds of earth running in straight lines across the desert.

<gasp> Old canals. Hohokam canals.

He realises: if they could carry water once, they can carry it again. He and a crew start digging them out.

Farms appear. Wheat. Barley. <chuckle> Pumpkins grow so big that people want to call the town Pumpkinville.

But an English settler named Darrell Duppa has a better idea. A new city, rising from the ruins of an old one.

<short pause> Like the bird of legend that rises from its own ashes.

<whispers> Phoenix.

## Part 4B

And then, silver. In eighteen seventy-seven, a prospector named Ed Schieffelin heads out into Apache country in the south. People tell him all he'll find out there is his tombstone.

<chuckle> He finds silver. So that's what he names the town.

Tombstone.

Within a few years, it's a boomtown of saloons, gambling halls, an opera house... and a lot of guns.

## Part 4C

In October eighteen eighty-one, the town marshal, Virgil Earp, his brothers Wyatt and Morgan, and their friend Doc Holliday walk down the street to disarm a group of cowboys.

<short pause> The gunfight lasts about thirty seconds. Three cowboys are killed.

It happened in a narrow lot near the back of the O.K. Corral. <laugh> But the O.K. Corral made a better headline. And a better movie.

<short pause> Wyatt Earp lives until nineteen twenty-nine. At the end of his life, he's in Los Angeles, advising Hollywood filmmakers on how to make Westerns. <chuckle> The Wild West, teaching Hollywood how to play the Wild West.

## Part 4D

Then copper. Huge copper mines open at Bisbee, Jerome and Morenci. Jerome is built on a mountainside so steep that buildings sometimes slide downhill. <chuckle> The town jail once slid more than two hundred feet across the street.

Copper wires carry electricity and telephone calls across America. And much of that copper comes from Arizona.

Arizona's economy becomes the five Cs. Copper, cattle, cotton, citrus... and climate.

<short pause> And the railroads bring a new kind of visitor. Tourists.

In nineteen-oh-three, President Theodore Roosevelt stands at the edge of the Grand Canyon and tells Arizonans: leave it as it is. You cannot improve on it. <short pause> In nineteen nineteen, it becomes a national park. Later, Route sixty-six brings millions of drivers through northern Arizona, with neon motels and roadside dinosaurs.

## Part 4E (quiet style)

In July nineteen seventeen, copper miners in Bisbee go on strike.

<breath> At dawn, armed men deputised by the sheriff round up more than a thousand strikers and their supporters. They march them to the railroad, load them into cattle cars, and dump them in the New Mexico desert, with little food or water.

<long pause> It's called the Bisbee Deportation. Nobody is ever punished for it.

## Part 5: Statehood

By nineteen ten, Arizona wants to be a state. It writes a constitution. It's very progressive for its time. And it lets voters recall judges they don't like.

President Taft hates that. He vetoes Arizona's statehood.

<chuckle> So Arizona takes the judge part out. Taft signs. On February fourteenth, nineteen twelve, Valentine's Day, Arizona becomes the forty-eighth state. The last of the lower forty-eight.

And then, that November, Arizona voters put the judge part right back in. <laugh> And in the same election, they give women the vote.

## Part 5B

Water is still the question.

In nineteen eleven, the Roosevelt Dam is finished on the Salt River. <gasp> At the time, it's the tallest masonry dam in the world. Phoenix finally has steady water, all year.

And Arizona gets very protective of its water.

In nineteen thirty-four, California starts building a dam on the Colorado River, to send water to Los Angeles. Arizona's governor is so angry, he sends the National Guard to the river to stop it.

<laugh> The Guard borrows two old ferry boats. Newspapers call them the Arizona Navy. The boats get tangled in cables, and have to be rescued by the Californians.

<short pause> Two years later, Hoover Dam is finished on Arizona's border with Nevada. And Arizona spends decades fighting California, in Congress and in the Supreme Court, for its share of the Colorado River. <short pause> It finally wins in nineteen sixty-three.

## Part 5C

In the Second World War, the Pacific war needs an unbreakable code.

Young Navajo men become Marines and invent one, based on their own language. <short pause> The Japanese never crack it.

The Navajo Code Talkers.

<short pause> It starts with twenty-nine young men. By the end of the war, around four hundred. The code is kept secret for more than twenty years after the war. <sigh> For decades, the Code Talkers can't even tell their own families what they did.

And a young Pima Marine from Arizona, Ira Hayes, is one of the men who raise the American flag on Iwo Jima, in the most famous photograph of the war.

## Part 5D

<chuckle> Arizona also has German prisoners of war, in a camp at Papago Park, near Phoenix.

In December nineteen forty-four, twenty-five of them dig a tunnel and escape. Some of them have a plan. They've seen the Gila River on a map. They've built a folding boat. <short pause> They'll float down the river to Mexico.

<laugh> They get to the river. It's dry. Arizona has been using the water.

They're all caught.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

During the same war, the government forces Japanese American families from the West Coast into camps.

<breath> Two of the biggest are in Arizona, on tribal land. Poston and Gila River.

<long pause> For a while, the Gila River camp is one of the biggest towns in the whole state. Behind barbed wire.

## Part 6: Sun Belt

After the war, two things change Arizona forever.

<short pause> Air conditioning. And retirement.

In nineteen sixty, a builder named Del Webb opens Sun City, near Phoenix. A whole town just for retirees. Golf, swimming pools, sunshine. <chuckle> On opening weekend, tens of thousands of curious visitors jam the roads for miles.

Families and retirees pour in from the cold North. Phoenix grows from about a hundred thousand people in nineteen fifty to more than a million and a half today.

## Part 6B

In nineteen sixty-three, police in Phoenix arrest a man named Ernesto Miranda. He confesses. Nobody tells him he has a right to stay silent, or to have a lawyer.

<short pause> His case goes to the Supreme Court. And in nineteen sixty-six, it rules that police must warn people of their rights.

<chuckle> You've heard it in a thousand movies. "You have the right to remain silent." Those are the Miranda rights. From Phoenix.

## Part 6C

And a boy born on a farm near Yuma, Arizona, grows up picking crops with his family. Cesar Chavez.

He becomes the leader of America's farmworkers' union. In nineteen seventy-two, he comes back to Arizona and fasts for twenty-four days, in Phoenix, to protest a new law against farmworker strikes.

<short pause> When people tell him it can't be done, his colleague Dolores Huerta answers in Spanish. Sí, se puede. Yes, it can be done.

<gasp> That slogan, born in Phoenix, will one day be shouted at rallies all over America.

## Part 6D

In nineteen sixty-eight, a businessman named Robert McCulloch buys the old London Bridge, which is sinking into the River Thames. He has it taken apart, ships it across the ocean in numbered blocks, and rebuilds it over a channel dug in the desert, at Lake Havasu City.

<laugh> There's a story that he thought he was buying the fancier Tower Bridge. He always said no.

Today, it's one of the most visited sights in the state.

And in nineteen eighty-one, Arizona judge Sandra Day O'Connor becomes the first woman on the United States Supreme Court.

<chuckle> And in nineteen ninety-one, eight people walk into a giant glass greenhouse in the desert north of Tucson, and seal the door. Biosphere two. Rainforest, ocean, desert and farm, all under glass. They're supposed to live off it for two years.

<groan> They do make it. But oxygen runs low, crops fail, and the crew gets very hungry. And very grumpy with each other. <short pause> Today, it's a science lab, run by the University of Arizona.

## Part 6E (quiet style)

In June twenty thirteen, a wildfire near the town of Yarnell suddenly changes direction in a storm.

<long pause> Nineteen firefighters of the Granite Mountain Hotshots are killed. It's the deadliest day for American wildland firefighters in eighty years.

## Part 6F

Today, Phoenix is the fifth biggest city in America. Arizona builds computer chips, cars and spacecraft parts. Millions of people visit the Grand Canyon every year.

<chuckle> Every spring, half of Major League Baseball comes to train in the Phoenix sun. And every winter, the snowbirds fly in from Canada and the Midwest, with their golf clubs.

But it's getting hotter. In the summer of twenty twenty-three, Phoenix hits a hundred and ten degrees or more for thirty-one days in a row.

<sigh> And the Colorado River is shrinking. Years of drought have cut the water Arizona can take from it. The old question is back.

## Part 6G

So how does a giant city grow in one of the driest places in the country?

<short pause> It starts with canals nobody had used for four hundred years.

The Hohokam dug them. They waited under the sand. Jack Swilling dug them out again. Then came the dams, and a canal across the desert, more than three hundred miles long, bringing Colorado River water to Phoenix and Tucson. <short pause> Modern canals, following some of the very same paths as the Hohokam.

And a city was named after a bird that rises from its ashes.

<long pause> Arizona has always been a place where people bet everything on water. The Hohokam made that bet a thousand years ago.

Now it's Arizona's turn.

<chuckle> And if you visit London Bridge... <short pause> remember, it's falling down a lot less in Arizona than it was in London.
