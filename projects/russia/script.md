# Russia: The Country With Everything Except People

## Cold Open {nocard}
Look at Russia on a map.
It barely looks real.
It covers more than 17 million square kilometres.
That's over a tenth of all the land on Earth.
It stretches across 11 time zones.
When people in Kaliningrad sit down to dinner, people in Kamchatka are already waking up tomorrow.
[Note: a map of Russia overlaid on the other continents, then the time zones sweeping across it.]
But here is the strange part.
Most Russians live in only a small corner of it.
Roughly three-quarters of the population is packed into the European west.
The rest of the country is an ocean of forest, mountains, and frozen river, with very few people per square kilometre.
Russia has more land than any country on Earth.
And it has never had enough people to fill it.
That one problem explains a surprising amount of Russian history.
It explains why Russia kept expanding.
Why its rulers chained farmers to the land.
And why a country with all that space has always felt so afraid.
But it points to something darker, too.
Again and again, Russia's rulers have solved the problem of holding all that land by spending people to do it.
Serfs. Famine victims. Prisoners. Soldiers.
Tens of millions of them.
And now that bill is coming due, in the one place no tank can reach.
The birth rate.
To see how a small forest settlement ended up here, we have to go back more than a thousand years.
To a country with no walls.

## Chapter 1: A Country With No Walls
Stand in Germany and look east.
You won't find a mountain range or a desert.
You'll find the North European Plain, a vast flat stretch that runs, with barely a hill in the way, all the way into the heart of Russia.
Armies can cross it.
Traders can cross it.
For a thousand years, invaders did.
In the 800s, the people who came were Vikings.
Instead of sailing west to England, many travelled east up Russia's rivers, chasing silver and the rich markets of Constantinople.
They became known as the Rus.
According to an old chronicle, local tribes who were tired of fighting each other sent them an invitation.
Our land is great and rich, but there is no order in it. Come and rule over us.
Historians debate how much of that story is true.
But the name stuck.
Rurik's descendants built a state around Kyiv, called Kievan Rus.
Russians, Ukrainians and Belarusians all claim it as part of their history.
In 988, its prince, Vladimir, chose Orthodox Christianity from Constantinople.
That tied Russia's religion, art, and politics to Byzantium for centuries.
Then, in the winter of 1237, the Mongols arrived.
They came in winter on purpose.
Frozen rivers became highways for their cavalry.
Russian cities fell one by one.
In December 1240, they took Kyiv.
A European traveller passing through years later described fields still covered in bones and skulls.
The Mongols didn't just destroy.
They ruled.
Russian princes could keep their thrones, but only if they travelled to the Mongol court, kneeled, and paid tribute.
And one small town learned to play that game better than anyone else.

## Chapter 2: The Moneybag and the Tsars
Moscow first shows up in the records in 1147.
For a long time it was nothing special.
A wooden town in the forest.
But its princes were clever.
One of them, Ivan I, won the Mongols' trust by collecting tribute for them from other Russian lands.
And he pocketed a share.
Russians nicknamed him Ivan Kalita.
Ivan the Moneybag.
He used the money to buy land, build influence, and bring the head of the Orthodox Church to Moscow.
Moscow didn't rise by defying the Mongols.
It rose by working for them.
Then, in 1480, a later ruler, Ivan III, simply stopped paying.
The two armies faced each other across the Ugra River for weeks.
Neither attacked.
Eventually, the Mongols withdrew.
Over two centuries of rule ended without a decisive battle.
Ivan III absorbed rival cities and took the double-headed eagle as his symbol.
After Constantinople fell to the Ottomans in 1453, he began presenting Moscow as the new centre of Orthodox Christianity.
Some Russian thinkers called it the Third Rome.
His grandson, Ivan IV, was crowned in 1547 as the first Tsar of all Russia.
The title came from Caesar.
History remembers him as Ivan the Terrible.
A better translation of the Russian word is fearsome.
And he earned both.
He crushed his nobles.
He launched waves of terror against his own people.
And by tradition, he killed his own son in a rage.
But Ivan also understood something about a country without walls.
If you can't build a barrier, you can build distance.
So Russia went east.
In 1552, it took Kazan.
In 1582, a Cossack leader named Yermak crossed the Ural Mountains into Siberia.
A land of forests, rivers, and one very valuable animal.
The sable.
Its fur was so prized that people called it soft gold.
[Note: animated map, with the border creeping east year by year.]
Traders and soldiers pushed on.
They built forts, demanded tribute, and moved again.
By 1639, Russians had reached the Pacific.
In about 60 years, Russia had crossed a continent.
But there was a catch.
It was gaining land far faster than it was gaining people.
And soon, its rulers would decide how to fix that.

## Chapter 3: The Chains
But first, Russia nearly vanished.
In 1600, a volcano erupted in Peru.
It threw ash and sulphur into the sky and cooled the climate around the world.
In Russia, harvests failed.
Famine spread for years, and millions died.
The ruling dynasty had also died out.
Rival nobles fought for the throne.
Pretenders appeared.
Foreign armies saw their chance.
In 1610, Polish troops occupied the Kremlin.
A volunteer army led by a merchant and a prince drove them out in 1612.
The next year, a national assembly chose a 16-year-old, Michael Romanov, as Tsar.
His family would rule for more than 300 years.
Now the new dynasty faced a problem.
Russia had huge land and little labour.
And a peasant who'd had enough could simply walk away into the forest.
Landowners needed workers.
The state needed taxes.
So in 1649, a new legal code locked the peasants in place.
Over time, they became bound to their landlords' estates.
And so did their children.
This was serfdom, and it lasted for more than two centuries.
[Note: a peasant village, a landlord's estate, a ledger listing human beings as property.]
Imagine being born into it.
Your farm isn't yours.
Your labour isn't yours.
Your children inherit your chains.
And the reason isn't cruelty for its own sake.
It's arithmetic.
Russia was too big and too empty, and the state's answer was to make sure nobody left.
Then came Peter the Great.
An enormous man, around six feet eight, with an enormous appetite for change.
In 1697, he toured Europe incognito and worked in a Dutch shipyard to learn shipbuilding himself.
He came home to rebuild the army and navy.
He forced Russia's elite to adopt Western ways, even ordering nobles to shave their beards.
He also wanted a window onto the sea.
In 1703, he began building a new capital on the Baltic coast.
On a swamp.
Tens of thousands of conscripted labourers worked on it, and many died of disease and exhaustion.
The city that rose was St. Petersburg.
It was Russia's pattern in miniature.
A glittering result, paid for with human lives.

## Chapter 4: The Empire That Won and Fell Behind
In 1762, a German-born princess seized the throne from her husband.
She became Catherine the Great.
Under her, Russia pushed west and south.
It joined Prussia and Austria in carving up Poland.
In 1783, it annexed Crimea, giving it a hold on the Black Sea.
Russian settlers had also crossed the Pacific into Alaska.
But it was too far and too expensive to defend.
So in 1867, Russia sold it to the United States for $7.2 million.
Roughly two cents an acre.
Then, in June 1812, Napoleon invaded with one of the largest armies Europe had ever seen.
He wanted one decisive battle.
Russia refused to give him one.
Instead, its armies retreated, burning crops and supplies as they went.
Napoleon marched deeper into a land that offered him nothing.
He reached Moscow in September to find it nearly empty.
Then burning.
He waited for a surrender that never came, and eventually turned back.
The winter hurt, but it wasn't the main killer.
Distance, hunger, disease, and Russian attacks had already shredded his army.
Only a small fraction of it made it home.
Two years later, Russian troops marched into Paris.
But here's the cruel twist.
While Russia was winning wars, the rest of Europe was building factories and railways.
Russia still relied on millions of serfs.
Its defeat in the Crimean War in the 1850s exposed how far behind it had slipped.
In 1861, Tsar Alexander II freed about 23 million serfs.
But freedom didn't mean prosperity.
Many freed peasants ended up in debt and poverty.
And in 1881, revolutionaries assassinated the Tsar with a bomb.
Still, the empire pushed on.
In 1891, it began the Trans-Siberian Railway.
Over 9,000 kilometres of track, from Moscow to the Pacific.
A steel thread stitched across the country Russia had conquered.
But the railway couldn't save the Romanovs.

## Chapter 5: Collapse, and the Man of Steel
In 1904, Russia went to war with Japan, expecting an easy win.
It suffered a humiliating defeat.
The first time a modern European power had been beaten decisively by an Asian one.
Then came World War I.
Millions were sent to the front without enough weapons, food, or supplies.
By early 1917, protests and bread riots swept the capital.
Soldiers sent to crush them joined the crowds.
Nicholas II abdicated, ending the Romanov dynasty.
[Note: Russia then used the Julian calendar, so these events are dated to February and October. By the modern calendar they fall in March and November.]
The Bolsheviks, under Lenin, seized power.
In July 1918, Nicholas II, his wife, and their five children were shot in Yekaterinburg.
A brutal civil war followed, killing millions through fighting, famine, and disease.
In 1922, the Soviet Union was born.
After Lenin died in 1924, a man named Joseph Stalin won the struggle to replace him.
He wanted to turn a peasant country into an industrial power, fast.
And he was willing to pay for it in lives.
He forced millions of peasants onto collective farms and seized their grain.
In the early 1930s, famine killed millions of people in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and southern Russia.
Then, in 1937 and 1938, came the Great Terror.
The secret police arrested people accused of being enemies of the state.
Hundreds of thousands were shot.
Millions more passed through the Gulag labour camps.
[Note: still photos only. No music.]
Here is the contradiction at the heart of this story.
For centuries, Russia's central problem was too few people for too much land.
And in the 1930s, its own government destroyed millions of them.
But Stalin wasn't thinking about the future of the birth rate.
He was thinking about the next invasion.
And in June 1941, it came.

## Chapter 6: The War That Spent 27 Million
On June 22, 1941, Nazi Germany launched Operation Barbarossa.
More than three million soldiers poured across the border.
The Red Army, weakened by Stalin's purges and caught unprepared, was overwhelmed.
Whole armies were encircled.
Millions of Soviet soldiers were captured.
By December, German troops were within 20 or 25 kilometres of Moscow.
In the north, they surrounded Leningrad.
The siege lasted 872 days.
Food ran out.
People ate wallpaper paste and leather, and died in the streets.
Among them was a girl named Tanya Savicheva.
She was eleven years old.
In a small notebook, she wrote down, page by page, the deaths of her family.
Her sister.
Her grandmother.
Her brothers.
Her uncles.
Her mother.
The last entries are only a few words.
Tanya herself died not long after.
[Note: the real notebook pages, one at a time. Narration slows down.]
The siege killed hundreds of thousands of civilians.
Perhaps around a million.
But Russia's size also saved it.
As the Germans advanced, the Soviet state dismantled thousands of factories and loaded them onto trains.
They were shipped beyond the Urals.
Workers went with them.
Production resumed in the east, far from the front, turning out the tanks and shells that would win the war.
Then came Stalingrad.
For months, soldiers fought house by house through ruins.
In February 1943, the German Sixth Army, surrounded, surrendered.
The tide had turned.
In May 1945, the Red Army took Berlin.
The cost was staggering.
Around 27 million Soviet citizens died.
More than any other nation.
Stalin drew a lesson from it.
Never again would Russia's western border be exposed.

## Chapter 7: A Wall Made of Other Countries
After the war, the Soviet Union took control of Eastern Europe.
Poland. East Germany. Hungary. Romania. Bulgaria.
These countries formed a buffer between Moscow and the West.
For Stalin, it was security.
For the people who lived there, it meant repression and lost independence.
The buffer worked, in its way.
The Soviet Union became one of two superpowers.
It launched Sputnik in 1957.
It sent Yuri Gagarin into orbit in 1961, exactly a century after freeing its serfs.
It also tested the Tsar Bomba, the largest nuclear weapon ever detonated.
But behind the achievements was an economy that struggled.
A huge military budget.
And a costly war in Afghanistan, starting in 1979.
In 1986, the Chernobyl reactor exploded.
The government's first attempt to hide it deepened public distrust.
Mikhail Gorbachev tried to reform the system.
And the reforms helped bring it down.
On December 25, 1991, the red flag came down over the Kremlin.
Fifteen new countries emerged.
Russia was the largest.
But it had lost territory, population, and the buffer Stalin had built.
About 25 million ethnic Russians suddenly lived outside its borders.
They hadn't moved.
The borders had.
The 1990s were brutal.
The economy collapsed.
Savings vanished.
Life expectancy for Russian men fell sharply.
Births dropped hard.
And that drop is important.
It created a gap in the population that echoes through Russia today.
On the last day of 1999, Boris Yeltsin resigned.
He handed power to a former intelligence officer named Vladimir Putin.

## Chapter 8: The Old Fear Returns
Putin promised stability.
And in the early 2000s, helped by high energy prices, he delivered it.
The economy recovered.
The state centralised.
Russia regained influence abroad.
Putin also called the collapse of the Soviet Union a major geopolitical catastrophe.
And he was deeply troubled by what was happening on Russia's western edge.
Countries that had once been Moscow's buffer were joining NATO.
From Moscow's view, the wall was disappearing.
From the view of those countries, joining the West was a choice about their own security and independence.
Ukraine became the flashpoint.
In 2014, after a revolution toppled Ukraine's pro-Moscow president, Russia seized and annexed Crimea.
Most of the world refused to recognise it.
On February 24, 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine.
Moscow pointed to NATO expansion and security fears.
Ukraine and most other countries called it an unprovoked war of aggression.
The UN General Assembly condemned it as a violation of the UN Charter.
It became the largest war in Europe since 1945.
Cities were flattened.
Millions were displaced.
Russia's own losses were enormous.
Estimates run from hundreds of thousands to more than a million killed and wounded.
And Moscow publishes no full count.
In 2023, the Wagner mercenary group staged an armed revolt and marched toward Moscow before turning back.
In 2024, Ukrainian troops crossed into Russia's Kursk region and held parts of it.
The first time a foreign army had held Russian ground since World War II.
As of late October 2026, the war is still going on, with talks starting and stalling.
The old fear had returned.
But the real danger was happening somewhere no one could shoot at.

## Chapter 9: The Bill Comes Due
Remember the problem from the beginning.
Too much land.
Too few people.
For centuries, Russia's answer was to expand, to put distance between itself and its enemies.
When that wasn't enough, it spent people.
Serfs. Peasants. Prisoners. Soldiers.
Now it is spending the one thing it can't easily make more of.
And the war is doing it again.
Russia's population peaked at about 148.7 million in 1991.
Today it is around 146 million, and falling.
In 2024, Russia recorded about 1.22 million births.
The fewest since 1999.
Early 2025 was even worse.
Russian demographers described some months as the lowest in two centuries.
The country's fertility rate is around 1.4 children per woman.
Far below the 2.1 needed to keep a population steady.
The gap left by the 1990s makes it worse.
There are simply fewer women of childbearing age now.
The war removes young men, by death and by emigration.
Official forecasts suggest the population could fall to somewhere between 130 and 139 million by 2046.
About what it was in the Russian Empire of 1897.
The Kremlin has noticed.
It has promoted traditional family values, restricted abortion access, and offered payments to larger families.
So far, the numbers keep dropping.
And reportedly, in 2025, Russia's statistics agency quietly stopped publishing some of its monthly demographic figures.
[Note: a line chart of births from 1999 to 2025, with a pause at the 1990s dip and the latest data point.]
The emptiness is most visible in the east.
Russia spent four centuries pushing across Siberia to the Pacific.
Today the Far East, rich in resources and thin on people, keeps losing residents to cities in the west.
There is one way out that the Kremlin has little appetite for.
Mass immigration.

## Conclusion
According to the old chronicle, the tribes told the Vikings: our land is great and rich, but there is no order in it.
Twelve centuries later, the first half still holds.
The land is great and rich.
Russia is still the largest country on Earth.
It survived the Mongols.
Drove out the Poles.
Defeated Napoleon.
And outlasted Hitler.
But through all of it, the problem never went away.
Russia never had enough people for its land.
And too often, its leaders treated people as the cheapest resource it had.
No Napoleon is marching toward Moscow now.
No Mongol cavalry rides across the ice.
The threat is quieter.
Borders can be moved.
Territory can be taken.
Empires can be rebuilt.
But people take generations to replace.
Russia was never going to run out of land.
The question is who will be left to live on it.
