## Hook

Most of America's lobsters come from Maine.

Maine is the only state that touches just one other state.

Earmuffs? Invented by a fifteen-year-old in Maine whose ears got cold.

And for more than a hundred and fifty years, Maine wasn't even its own colony. It was part of Massachusetts.

<long pause> In nineteen twelve, Maine evicted an entire mixed-race island community, dug up their graves, and sent some of them to an institution.

And lobster? In colonial times, it was so common that it was food for prisoners.

<short pause> So how does prison food become a luxury... and keep a whole coast alive?

<whispers> It starts with lobsters piled two feet high on the beach.

## Part 1: Dawnland

Maine is the most eastern state. The sun hits it first. The people who've lived here for thousands of years call it the Dawnland.

They are the Wabanaki. The Penobscot, the Passamaquoddy, the Maliseet, the Mi'kmaq and the Abenaki.

They fish the rocky coast, hunt in the endless pine forests, and travel the rivers in birchbark canoes.

## Part 1B

Now, the lobsters.

When Europeans arrive, the coast is crawling with them. Early colonists write of lobsters washing up after storms, in piles two feet high. Children catch them by hand in tide pools. Farmers use them as fertiliser. And as fish bait.

<chuckle> Lobster is so cheap and so common that it's fed to prisoners. And servants. It's poor people's food.

## Part 2: Popham and the Frontier

In sixteen-oh-seven, the same year as Jamestown, English colonists build a settlement at the mouth of the Kennebec River. The Popham Colony.

It lasts about a year. The winter is brutal. They give up and sail home.

But before they leave, they build a ship. The Virginia. The first ocean-going ship built by the English in the Americas.

## Part 2B

English fishermen and farmers settle the coast anyway. In the sixteen-fifties, Massachusetts takes over Maine. It stays part of Massachusetts for more than a hundred and fifty years.

For decades, Maine is a war zone. The English fight the French and the Wabanaki. Settlements are burned. And rebuilt. And burned again.

## Part 2C

In the Revolution, in October seventeen seventy-five, the British navy bombards and burns the town of Falmouth. Today it's called Portland.

## Part 3: District of Maine

After the war, Maine's people get tired of taking orders from Boston. Especially when Massachusetts fails to protect them from the British in the War of eighteen twelve.

So Maine asks to become its own state.

And the timing gets tangled up with slavery. Missouri wants to join as a slave state. So Congress makes a deal. Maine joins as a free state. Missouri as a slave state. Balance kept.

On March fifteenth, eighteen twenty, Maine becomes the twenty-third state.

## Part 3B

In the eighteen-thirties, Maine and the British colony of New Brunswick argue about the border. Both sides send soldiers and lumberjacks. People call it the Aroostook War.

<chuckle> No battles are ever fought. In eighteen forty-two, a treaty settles it. The biggest casualty is everyone's pride.

## Part 3C

Maine's forests and coast make it rich. Bangor ships so much lumber it's called the lumber capital of the world. In Bath, shipyards build wooden sailing ships by the hundreds. Mainers even cut ice from frozen rivers and ship it around the world.

In Brunswick, Maine, a professor's wife named Harriet Beecher Stowe writes Uncle Tom's Cabin.

And in eighteen fifty-one, Maine becomes the first state to ban alcohol.

## Part 3D

In the Civil War, at Gettysburg, a college professor from Maine, Colonel Joshua Chamberlain, holds the far left end of the Union line on a hill called Little Round Top. When his men run out of bullets, he orders a bayonet charge, downhill. It works.

## Part 4: Vacationland

After the war, things change for lobsters.

Canneries start packing lobster meat into tins and shipping it across the country. Railroads carry rich city people to Maine's coast for the summer. They try fresh lobster. They love it. Restaurants in Boston and New York start serving it.

By the end of the eighteen hundreds, lobster isn't poor people's food any more. It's a delicacy.

## Part 4B

And Maine's lobstermen notice something. Lobsters are getting smaller. And fewer.

So, starting in the eighteen-seventies, Maine passes some of the first lobster conservation laws. Don't keep lobsters that are too small. Later, don't keep ones that are too big, either, because the big ones make the most babies. And if you catch a female carrying eggs, cut a small V into her tail, and throw her back. Nobody can ever keep her.

Lobstermen guard these rules fiercely. Even on each other.

## Part 4C

In eighteen seventy-three, a fifteen-year-old in Farmington, Maine, named Chester Greenwood, is tired of freezing ears while ice skating. He bends some wire and asks his grandmother to sew on fur.

Earmuffs.

In nineteen-twelve, a Maine hunter named Leon Leonwood Bean sews leather tops onto rubber boots, and starts selling them by mail. L.L. Bean.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

Also in nineteen-twelve, the state of Maine forces about forty-five people off Malaga Island, a poor community where Black and white families had lived together for generations.

Their homes are torn down. Their graves are dug up. Some are sent to a state institution for the "feeble-minded", where they spend the rest of their lives.

In two thousand ten, Maine's governor visits the island and expresses the state's regret.

## Part 5: Today

In nineteen fifty, a senator from Maine, Margaret Chase Smith, the first woman to serve in both the House and the Senate, stands up in the Senate to speak against the fear and accusations of the McCarthy era. Her speech is called the "Declaration of Conscience".

And a writer from Bangor, Stephen King, makes little Maine towns the scariest places in fiction.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In October twenty twenty-three, a gunman opens fire at a bowling alley and a bar in Lewiston, Maine. Eighteen people are killed.

## Part 5C

Today, Maine's lobstermen land around a hundred million pounds of lobster in a good year. The Gulf of Maine is warming fast, and the lobsters are slowly moving north. But Maine's conservation rules are a big reason the lobster boom has lasted so long.

## Part 5D

So how does prison food become a luxury... and keep a whole coast alive?

It starts with lobsters piled two feet high on the beach.

They were so plentiful nobody valued them. Then trains and tourists turned them into a treat. And when they started to run out, Maine's lobstermen made rules to protect them, and kept them. That's why Maine still has lobsters when other places ran short.

<short pause> Maine learned that what's common today can be precious tomorrow.

<chuckle> The prisoners of sixteen-fifty would be very confused by today's lobster prices.
