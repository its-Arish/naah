# The Entire History of Maine

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,150 spoken words, so roughly 11 to 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen twelve"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, misty, dawn-on-the-coast storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Hardy, weathered frontier voice, then grim. |
| Part 3 | Stubborn, practical Yankee voice with a dry wit. |
| Part 4 | Warm, salty, seaside storyteller, a little cheeky. |
| Part 5 | Plain-spoken and proud; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Dawnland 10,000 BC to 1607 · Popham and the Frontier 1607 to 1775 · District of Maine 1775 to 1820 · Ships and Timber 1820 to 1865 · Vacationland 1865 to 1945 · Today 1945 to now.

## Hook

Most of America's lobsters come from Maine.

<chuckle> Maine is the only state that touches just one other state. And earmuffs? Invented by a fifteen-year-old in Maine whose ears got cold.

For more than a hundred and fifty years, Maine wasn't even its own colony. It was part of Massachusetts.

<long pause> In nineteen twelve, Maine evicted an entire mixed-race island community, dug up their graves, and sent some of them to an institution.

And lobster? In colonial times, it was so common that it was food for prisoners.

<short pause> So how does prison food become a luxury... and keep a whole coast alive?

<whispers> It starts with lobsters piled two feet high on the beach.

## Part 1: Dawnland

Maine is the most eastern state. For part of the year, the very first sunrise in the United States lights up the top of Cadillac Mountain, on the coast. <short pause> The people who've lived here for thousands of years call it the Dawnland.

<gasp> The glaciers that covered Maine scraped and sank the land, drowning old river valleys. That's why Maine's coast is so ragged. It's only about two hundred and thirty miles in a straight line. But follow every cove, bay and island, and it's more than three thousand miles long.

<chuckle> Inland, Maine is nine-tenths forest. It has more moose than any other state south of Canada. Drivers learn to watch out. Hitting a moose is like hitting a horse on stilts.

## Part 1B

The people of the Dawnland are the Wabanaki. The Penobscot, the Passamaquoddy, the Maliseet, the Mi'kmaq and the Abenaki.

They fish the rocky coast, hunt moose in the endless pine forests, and travel the rivers in birchbark canoes. <short pause> Thousands of years ago, people here bury their dead with beautiful stone tools, sprinkled with bright red ochre. Archaeologists once called them the Red Paint People.

<whispers> And in a pile of old shells and bones, at a Native site on the coast, an amateur archaeologist finds a tiny silver coin. It's Norwegian. About nine hundred years old. <short pause> Most experts think it travelled down the coast through Native trade, from the Vikings' visits much further north.

## Part 1C

Now, the lobsters.

When Europeans arrive, the coast is crawling with them. Early colonists write of lobsters washing up after storms, in piles two feet high. <chuckle> Children catch them by hand in tide pools. Farmers spread them on their fields as fertiliser. And use them as fish bait.

<laugh> Lobster is so cheap and so common that it's fed to prisoners. And servants. It's poor people's food.

## Part 2: Popham and the Frontier

In sixteen-oh-seven, the same year as Jamestown, English colonists build a settlement at the mouth of the Kennebec River. The Popham Colony.

<groan> It lasts about a year. The winter is brutal. Their leader dies. They give up and sail home.

<short pause> But before they leave, they build a ship. The Virginia. The first ocean-going ship built by the English in the Americas. <chuckle> Maine has been building ships ever since.

## Part 2B

English fishermen and farmers settle the coast anyway. In the sixteen-fifties, Massachusetts takes over Maine. It stays part of Massachusetts for more than a hundred and fifty years.

<sigh> For decades, Maine is a war zone. The English fight the French and the Wabanaki. Settlements are burned. And rebuilt. And burned again.

<chuckle> The little town of Castine changes hands so often that it flies the flags of four different nations over the years. French, Dutch, British and American.

## Part 2C

In the Revolution, in October seventeen seventy-five, the British navy bombards and burns the town of Falmouth. Today it's called Portland.

<groan> In seventeen seventy-nine, Massachusetts sends a big fleet to drive the British out of Castine. More than forty ships. It's a disaster. The commanders argue for weeks. When British warships arrive, the Americans run their own ships aground and burn them. <short pause> It's the worst American naval defeat until Pearl Harbor. One of the officers court-martialled afterwards is Paul Revere.

## Part 3: District of Maine

After the war, Maine's people get tired of taking orders from Boston. <tsk> Especially when Massachusetts fails to protect them from the British in the War of Eighteen-Twelve.

So Maine asks to become its own state.

And the timing gets tangled up with slavery. Missouri wants to join as a slave state. So Congress makes a deal. Maine joins as a free state. Missouri as a slave state. Balance kept.

On March fifteenth, eighteen twenty, Maine becomes the twenty-third state.

<short pause> Even before that, in seventeen ninety-one, President George Washington's new government lights a lighthouse on a rocky point near Portland. Portland Head Light. <chuckle> It's still shining. It may be the most photographed lighthouse in America.

## Part 3B

In the eighteen-thirties, Maine and the British colony of New Brunswick argue about the border. Both sides send soldiers and lumberjacks into the disputed forest. People call it the Aroostook War.

<chuckle> No battles are ever fought. In eighteen forty-two, a treaty settles it. The biggest casualty is everyone's pride.

## Part 3C

Maine's forests and coast make it rich. <gasp> Bangor ships so much lumber that it's called the lumber capital of the world. Its riverfront is so crowded with ships that you could walk across the river on their decks. <chuckle> Bangor also claims to be the birthplace of the giant lumberjack, Paul Bunyan. It has a thirty-one-foot statue of him.

In Bath, shipyards build wooden sailing ships by the hundreds. <short pause> And Mainers cut blocks of ice from frozen rivers in winter, pack them in sawdust, and ship them around the world. Kennebec ice cools drinks in Southern cities and the Caribbean.

<short pause> Bath still builds ships today. Bath Iron Works builds destroyers for the U.S. Navy.

## Part 3D

In Brunswick, Maine, a professor's wife named Harriet Beecher Stowe writes Uncle Tom's Cabin.

<tsk> And in eighteen fifty-one, Maine becomes the first state to ban alcohol. Portland's mayor, Neal Dow, is the law's biggest champion. <sigh> In eighteen fifty-five, a crowd riots outside a city building where liquor is stored. Dow orders the militia to fire. One man is killed. People call it the Portland Rum Riot.

## Part 3E

When the Civil War comes, Abraham Lincoln's first vice president is Hannibal Hamlin, from Maine.

<gasp> At Gettysburg, a college professor from Maine, Colonel Joshua Chamberlain, holds the far left end of the Union line on a hill called Little Round Top. When his men run out of bullets, he orders a bayonet charge, downhill. <phew> It works.

<short pause> At the end of the war, Chamberlain is chosen to receive the formal surrender of the Confederate infantry at Appomattox. <breath> As the beaten soldiers march past, he orders his men to salute them. The Confederates salute back.

## Part 4: Vacationland

After the war, things change for lobsters.

Canneries start packing lobster meat into tins and shipping it across the country. <short pause> Railroads carry rich city people to Maine's coast for the summer. They try fresh lobster. They love it. Restaurants in Boston and New York start serving it.

<gasp> By the end of the eighteen hundreds, lobster isn't poor people's food any more. It's a delicacy.

Rich families build summer mansions on Mount Desert Island. <short pause> In nineteen sixteen, some of them help protect the land that becomes the first national park east of the Mississippi. Acadia.

<chuckle> Up in the woods, at a spring in the town of Poland, a family starts selling bottled water in eighteen forty-five, claiming it cures diseases. Poland Spring.

<short pause> And Maine's highest mountain, Katahdin, becomes the northern end of the Appalachian Trail. Hikers who walk the whole two thousand miles from Georgia finish on its rocky summit.

## Part 4B

And Maine's lobstermen notice something. Lobsters are getting smaller. And fewer.

<tsk> So, starting in the eighteen-seventies, Maine passes some of the first lobster conservation laws. Don't keep lobsters that are too small. Later, don't keep ones that are too big, either, because the big ones make the most babies. <short pause> And if you catch a female carrying eggs, cut a small V into her tail, and throw her back. Nobody can ever keep her.

<chuckle> Lobstermen guard these rules fiercely. Even on each other. They even have their own unwritten territories. Drop your traps in someone else's waters, and you might find your lines cut.

## Part 4C

In eighteen seventy-three, a fifteen-year-old in Farmington, Maine, named Chester Greenwood, is tired of freezing ears while ice skating. He bends some wire, and asks his grandmother to sew on fur.

<laugh> Earmuffs. Farmington still holds a Chester Greenwood Day every December, with a parade.

## Part 4D

In nineteen-twelve, a Maine hunter named Leon Leonwood Bean is tired of wet feet. So he sews leather tops onto rubber boots, and starts selling them by mail.

<groan> Of the first hundred pairs, ninety come back. The stitching leaks. <short pause> He gives every customer their money back, fixes the boots, and keeps going. L.L. Bean.

<chuckle> Maine also has its own soda, Moxie. It tastes like bitter roots. People either love it or hate it. It's the official state soft drink.

<chuckle> For more than a century, Maine holds its elections in September, two months before everyone else. People say: "As Maine goes, so goes the nation." <laugh> In nineteen thirty-six, Maine and Vermont are the only two states to vote against Franklin Roosevelt. Someone jokes: "As Maine goes, so goes Vermont."

## Part 4E (quiet style)

Also in nineteen-twelve, the state of Maine forces about forty-five people off Malaga Island, a poor community where Black and white families had lived together for generations.

<breath> Their homes are torn down. Their graves are dug up.

<long pause> Some are sent to a state institution for the "feeble-minded", where they spend the rest of their lives.

In two thousand ten, Maine's governor visits the island and expresses the state's regret.

## Part 5: Today

<whispers> In November nineteen forty-four, on a snowy night, a German submarine surfaces off Hancock Point, Maine. Two spies paddle ashore in a rubber boat, and walk off through the snow, in city clothes.

<chuckle> A teenage boy notices them, because nobody walks around there in a snowstorm wearing city shoes. The FBI catches them in New York a few weeks later.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In October nineteen forty-seven, after a long drought, fires break out across Maine's forests.

<breath> In Bar Harbor, a wildfire roars through town, destroying dozens of the grand summer mansions, and hundreds of homes. Thousands flee by boat. <long pause> Across the state, sixteen people die. People call it "the year Maine burned".

## Part 5C

In nineteen fifty, a senator from Maine, Margaret Chase Smith, the first woman to serve in both the House and the Senate, stands up in the Senate to speak against the fear and accusations of the McCarthy era. <short pause> Her speech is called the "Declaration of Conscience".

The poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow grows up in Portland. The painter Andrew Wyeth paints a Maine farm field, with a woman reaching toward a house. And a writer from Bangor, Stephen King, makes little Maine towns the scariest places in fiction.

<chuckle> Maine also grows potatoes in Aroostook County, and almost all of America's wild blueberries.

<laugh> Its official state treat is the whoopie pie. Two chocolate cakes, with a big blob of fluffy cream in the middle.

<short pause> In nineteen eighty, after years in court, the Penobscot and the Passamaquoddy win a land-claims settlement worth more than eighty million dollars, to buy back some of their land.

<short pause> And in twenty eighteen, Maine becomes the first state to use ranked-choice voting in a statewide election, where voters rank the candidates in order.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In October twenty twenty-three, a gunman opens fire at a bowling alley and a bar in Lewiston, Maine.

<long pause> Eighteen people are killed.

## Part 5E

Today, Maine's lobstermen land around a hundred million pounds of lobster in a good year. <sigh> The Gulf of Maine is warming faster than almost any ocean on Earth, and the lobsters are slowly moving north, into cooler water. Lobstermen are also arguing with the government over new rules to protect endangered right whales from their trap lines.

<short pause> But Maine's conservation rules are a big reason the lobster boom has lasted so long.

## Part 5F

So how does prison food become a luxury... and keep a whole coast alive?

<whispers> It starts with lobsters piled two feet high on the beach.

They were so plentiful nobody valued them. Then trains and tourists turned them into a treat. <short pause> And when they started to run out, Maine's lobstermen made rules to protect them, and kept them. That's why Maine still has lobsters when other places ran short.

<breath> Maine learned that what's common today can be precious tomorrow.

<chuckle> The prisoners of sixteen-fifty would be very confused by today's lobster prices.
