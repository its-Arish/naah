# The Entire History of Colorado

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen sixty-four"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller among cliffs. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Greedy gold-rush energy, then serious. |
| Part 4 | Glittering and fast, then crashing. |
| Part 5 | Tough, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Fresh, outdoorsy, upbeat; calm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Cliff Dwellers 10,000 BC to 1300 · Plains and Mountains 1300 to 1806 · Explorers 1806 to 1858 · Gold Rush 1858 to 1876 · Silver 1876 to 1893 · Hard Times 1893 to 1941 · Ski Country 1941 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Chipotle? <short pause> Started in Denver. And a man in Denver once trademarked the word "cheeseburger".

<chuckle> Colorado looks like a perfect rectangle. It isn't. It has almost seven hundred sides.

The song "America the Beautiful" was written after a trip to the top of a Colorado mountain.

<long pause> In eighteen sixty-four, soldiers attacked a peaceful camp at Sand Creek. Most of the dead were women, children and elders.

And Colorado's famous gold rush? It was named after a mountain that didn't have any gold.

<short pause> So how does a gold rush named after the wrong mountain build one of the fastest-growing states in America?

<whispers> It starts with a mountain an explorer swore no one would ever climb.

## Part 1: Cliff Dwellers

Long before people, Colorado belonged to dinosaurs. <short pause> In eighteen seventy-seven, a schoolteacher near the town of Morrison finds giant bones in the hills. Among them, a dinosaur nobody has ever seen, with a small head, big plates down its back, and spikes on its tail. <gasp> The Stegosaurus. Today, it's Colorado's state fossil. And on a ridge nearby, you can still put your hand in real dinosaur footprints.

<short pause> Much later, people arrive.

About eight hundred years ago, in the canyons of southwest Colorado, people build entire villages inside the cliffs.

At Mesa Verde, the Ancestral Puebloans fit stone houses, towers and round ceremonial rooms called kivas under huge overhanging rock. One of them, Cliff Palace, has around a hundred and fifty rooms.

<gasp> They climb in and out on tiny toeholds cut into the sheer rock. With babies on their backs. And water jars.

<short pause> And then, around thirteen hundred, they leave. Probably because of long droughts, and conflict. They move south, and their descendants live among the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico and Arizona today.

## Part 1B

For six hundred years, the cliff houses sit empty.

<chuckle> Then, one snowy day in eighteen eighty-eight, two cowboys searching for stray cattle look across a canyon... and see a whole stone city tucked into the cliff. They name it Cliff Palace.

<short pause> Visitors soon start carting away pots, baskets and even mummies. So in nineteen-oh-six, Mesa Verde becomes the first national park created to protect the works of people, not just nature.

## Part 1C

The mountains become the home of the Ute, who know every valley and hot spring. Out on the eastern plains, the Cheyenne, the Arapaho, the Comanche and the Kiowa follow the buffalo.

<short pause> In the sixteen hundreds, they get something that changes everything. Horses, escaped and traded from Spanish New Mexico. Within a few generations, they're some of the greatest riders on Earth.

<chuckle> And in the San Luis Valley, wind piles sand against the mountains into the tallest dunes in North America. Seven hundred feet high. With snowy peaks behind them.

## Part 2: Explorers

In eighteen-oh-three, the United States buys a huge chunk of the West from France. The Louisiana Purchase. The eastern half of Colorado is part of it.

In eighteen-oh-six, an army explorer named Zebulon Pike sees a giant mountain on the horizon. He tries to climb it. <short pause> In November. With his men in summer uniforms.

<chuckle> He doesn't make it. He writes that no human being could ever reach the top.

The mountain is named after him anyway. <short pause> Pikes Peak.

<laugh> Later that winter, Pike wanders into Spanish territory and is arrested by Spanish soldiers. They're very polite about it.

## Part 2B

In eighteen-twenty, another army explorer crosses the plains and labels them on his map as "the Great American Desert". Not a place anyone would want to live.

<short pause> But traders come anyway. In the eighteen-thirties, the Bent brothers build a giant adobe trading fort on the Arkansas River. Bent's Fort. Trappers, Cheyenne families, Mexican traders and travellers on the Santa Fe Trail all meet there. It has a billiard table. <chuckle> In the middle of the plains.

<short pause> William Bent marries a Cheyenne woman, Owl Woman, the daughter of a holy man. Their children grow up between two worlds. That will matter, terribly, later.

## Part 3: Gold Rush

Then, in eighteen fifty-eight, a small party finds gold where Cherry Creek meets the South Platte River.

The news spreads east. And because nobody back home has heard of Cherry Creek, the newspapers name the rush after the one landmark everyone knows. Pikes Peak. <gasp> About eighty miles away.

<laugh> The Pikes Peak Gold Rush. Not at Pikes Peak.

In eighteen fifty-nine, around a hundred thousand people head west, many with "Pikes Peak or Bust!" painted on their wagons.

<chuckle> Lots of them find very little gold. Some go home with a new slogan painted on their wagons. "Busted, by God."

<short pause> The ones who stay find gold in the mountains at Central City, in a gulch people call the richest square mile on Earth.

## Part 3B

The camp at Cherry Creek becomes a town. Its founders name it after the governor of Kansas Territory, James Denver, hoping he'll help them.

<laugh> He'd already quit. They keep the name anyway.

<short pause> Denver.

The real gold turns up in the mountains west of town, at places like Central City. In eighteen sixty-one, Colorado becomes a territory. Its border is drawn as a neat rectangle on paper.

<chuckle> But surveyors in the eighteen hundreds make lots of small mistakes on the ground. So the real border zigzags. Today it has six hundred and ninety-seven sides.

## Part 3C

In the Civil War, a Confederate army marches north from Texas into New Mexico, aiming for Colorado's gold mines and California beyond.

In March eighteen sixty-two, at Glorieta Pass, in New Mexico, volunteer soldiers from Colorado, many of them miners, sneak around the Confederate army and burn its whole supply train. <gasp> Without food or ammunition, the Confederates have to retreat all the way back to Texas.

<short pause> Some call it the Gettysburg of the West.

## Part 3D

And then, a disaster. The first railroad across America skips Denver. It goes through Wyoming instead, a hundred miles north. <tsk> People say Denver is too close to the mountains to bother with.

So Denver builds its own railroad, north to meet it. In eighteen seventy, the trains arrive. Denver survives.

<chuckle> A few years later, in the town of Golden, a German immigrant named Adolph Coors starts a brewery. It's still there.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

The gold rush brings miners onto land promised to the Cheyenne and Arapaho.

In November eighteen sixty-four, a Cheyenne and Arapaho camp at Sand Creek is flying an American flag and a white flag. Their leader, Black Kettle, believes they are under the army's protection.

<breath> At dawn, Colonel John Chivington's soldiers attack.

<long pause> Around two hundred and thirty people are killed. Most of them women, children and elders.

Some officers refuse to fire. One of them, Captain Silas Soule, testifies about what he saw. Months later, he is shot dead in the streets of Denver. Congress condemns the massacre.

## Part 3F (quiet style)

Over the next years, the Cheyenne and Arapaho are pushed out of Colorado.

<breath> The Ute leader Ouray tries for years to keep peace, and keep his people's land, through treaty after treaty. Each treaty gives away more. In eighteen eighty-one, most of the Ute are forced off their mountain lands, to reservations in Utah and the far southwest corner of Colorado.

## Part 4: Silver

In eighteen seventy-six, a hundred years after the Declaration of Independence, Colorado becomes a state. The Centennial State.

<short pause> And then... silver.

In Leadville, two miles high in the mountains, miners strike silver. A shopkeeper named Horace Tabor helps two prospectors with supplies, in exchange for a share of what they find. <gasp> They find a fortune.

Tabor becomes one of the richest men in America. He builds opera houses. He scandalises everyone by divorcing his wife and marrying a young divorcée named Baby Doe.

<chuckle> Leadville is more than ten thousand feet up. The highest city in America. In eighteen eighty-two, the Irish writer Oscar Wilde comes to give a lecture on art to the miners. He goes down a mine, and drinks with them. <laugh> In a saloon, he sees a sign over the piano: Please do not shoot the pianist. He is doing his best.

Leadville becomes a wild, rich, freezing town. <chuckle> The gunfighter Doc Holliday deals cards there. Later, he dies in a hotel bed in Glenwood Springs. His last words, the story goes: "This is funny."

## Part 4B

And a young woman named Margaret Tobin marries a Leadville mining engineer, J. J. Brown. His company strikes gold. They move to a mansion in Denver.

In nineteen twelve, she's on board the Titanic when it hits the iceberg. <short pause> In a lifeboat, she takes an oar, makes the other women row, and argues with the crewman in charge to go back for survivors.

<chuckle> Later, people call her the Unsinkable Molly Brown.

## Part 4C

In eighteen ninety-three, the price of silver collapses. Tabor loses everything. <sigh> Years later, Baby Doe lives alone in a shack beside his old mine, still guarding it. She's found frozen there, in nineteen thirty-five.

## Part 4D

That same summer of eighteen ninety-three, a teacher named Katharine Lee Bates rides a wagon to the top of Pikes Peak.

From the top, she sees the plains stretching east, and the mountains all around. She writes a poem.

<short pause> "America the Beautiful."

<chuckle> The mountain that Pike said no human would ever climb... now has a road, a railway, and a gift shop on top.

And also in eighteen ninety-three, Colorado's men vote to give women the right to vote. <short pause> It's the first state where men vote for women's suffrage at the ballot box.

## Part 5: Hard Times

In the coal mines of southern Colorado, miners work long, dangerous hours. In nineteen thirteen, they go on strike and move into tent colonies.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In April nineteen fourteen, at Ludlow, guards and soldiers attack the tent colony.

<breath> A fire spreads through the tents. In a pit under one tent, two women and eleven children are found dead.

<long pause> It becomes known as the Ludlow Massacre.

<short pause> The mines belong to John D. Rockefeller, the richest man in America. After Ludlow, the whole country blames him. He visits the miners himself, dances at their parties, and agrees to better conditions. <short pause> It's one of the first big public relations campaigns in history.

## Part 5C

In nineteen thirty-five, a drive-in owner in Denver named Louis Ballast trademarks a name for a hamburger with cheese on it. <chuckle> The cheeseburger.

In the nineteen-thirties, the dry eastern plains turn to dust. Giant dust storms roll over farm towns. Many families give up and leave.

<short pause> In the Second World War, the army builds a giant factory outside Denver to make chemical weapons. The Rocky Mountain Arsenal. For decades, the land is badly poisoned. <chuckle> After a huge clean-up, it's now a wildlife refuge. With bison, bald eagles and prairie dogs, a few miles from downtown.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

During the Second World War, Japanese American families are held at a camp called Amache, on the plains of southeast Colorado.

## Part 6: Ski Country

Also during the war, the army needs soldiers who can fight in the mountains of Europe. On skis.

At Camp Hale, high in the Colorado Rockies, it trains the Tenth Mountain Division. Skiers, climbers, and mountain men.

After the war, many of them come back. And they build ski resorts. Veterans of the Tenth help create places like Vail and Arapahoe Basin, and help Aspen become a ski town.

<short pause> Colorado discovers its real treasure. Snow.

<chuckle> Aspen becomes a town of skiers, musicians and writers. In nineteen seventy, the wild writer Hunter S. Thompson runs for county sheriff, promising, among other things, to tear up the streets and plant grass. He loses. Narrowly.

<short pause> And near Denver, there's a natural concert hall, between two giant red sandstone rocks. Red Rocks. In nineteen sixty-four, the Beatles play there.

## Part 6B

In nineteen seventy, Denver wins the right to host the Winter Olympics of nineteen seventy-six.

<chuckle> And then Colorado's voters say... no thanks. Too expensive. Too many people. They vote down the money in nineteen seventy-two.

<gasp> Denver becomes the only city ever to win the Olympics, and then give them back. The games go to Austria instead.

## Part 6C

In the Cold War, the military digs a command centre deep inside Cheyenne Mountain, near Colorado Springs, behind blast doors weighing twenty-five tons. It watches the skies over North America.

<laugh> And every Christmas Eve, it tracks Santa. It all started in nineteen fifty-five, when a newspaper ad printed the wrong phone number. Children who called to talk to Santa reached the military's top-secret line. The colonel on duty told them where Santa was on the radar.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

In April nineteen ninety-nine, two students attack Columbine High School, near Denver. <long pause> Thirteen people are killed.

<breath> And on the last days of twenty twenty-one, in a winter with almost no snow, hurricane-force winds drive a grass fire into the suburbs near Boulder. In a few hours, more than a thousand homes burn.

## Part 6E

Today, Denver is booming. People pour in for the mountains, the sunshine, and the jobs. Colorado has more fourteen-thousand-foot peaks than any other state. And in two thousand twelve, it became one of the first two states to legalise marijuana for adults.

<short pause> The Colorado River starts as snowmelt high in its Rocky Mountains. Downstream, it supplies water to around forty million people, in seven states and Mexico. <sigh> And it's shrinking.

<chuckle> Outside Denver's airport stands a giant blue horse with glowing red eyes. People call it Blucifer. <gasp> While it was being built, part of it fell on its sculptor, and killed him.

And in nineteen ninety-three, a young chef opened a burrito shop near the University of Denver. Chipotle.

## Part 6F

So how does a gold rush named after the wrong mountain build one of the fastest-growing states in America?

<short pause> It starts with a mountain an explorer swore no one would ever climb.

That mountain gave the gold rush its name. The gold rush built Denver. The silver made it rich, then broke it. And then people came back, again and again, not for what was under the mountains... but for the mountains themselves.

<long pause> Colorado's real gold was never in the ground.

<chuckle> It was in the view. And maybe the burritos.
