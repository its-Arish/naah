## Hook

The teddy bear? <short pause> It's named after a bear hunt in Mississippi.

Elvis Presley? Born in Mississippi. B.B. King, Muddy Waters, and the blues itself? Mississippi.

Mississippi only finished officially approving the end of slavery... in two thousand thirteen.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-five, a fourteen-year-old boy from Chicago, Emmett Till, was murdered here, after being accused of whistling at a white woman.

And today, Mississippi is the poorest state in America.

<short pause> So how does the poorest state in America end up giving the world the blues, and rock and roll?

<whispers> It starts with some of the richest soil on Earth.

## Part 1: Mound Builders

The Mississippi River is the biggest river in North America. Every spring, for thousands of years, it floods. And every time it floods, it leaves behind a fresh layer of dark, rich mud.

In the northwest of the state, between the Mississippi and the Yazoo rivers, that mud has built up a giant, flat, fertile plain. The Mississippi Delta.

Some of the richest farm soil in the world.

## Part 1B

For centuries, people build mound towns here. The Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Natchez live across the land. The Natchez are ruled by a leader called the Great Sun, carried on a litter.

In fifteen forty-one, the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto becomes the first European to see the Mississippi River. He dies near it a year later.

In sixteen ninety-nine, the French build a fort at Biloxi, on the Gulf Coast.

## Part 1C (quiet style)

In seventeen twenty-nine, the Natchez rise up against French settlers taking their land. The French and their allies strike back. Within a few years, the Natchez nation is destroyed. Survivors are scattered, or sold into slavery in the Caribbean.

## Part 2: Cotton Kingdom

In eighteen seventeen, Mississippi becomes the twentieth state.

And then the cotton gin turns that rich soil into money. Enormous amounts of money.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

But first, the land has to be taken.

In eighteen thirty, the Choctaw are pressured into signing a treaty at Dancing Rabbit Creek. They give up their homeland. They are the first nation removed under the Indian Removal Act.

The Chickasaw are forced out soon after.

## Part 2C

Planters pour in. They clear the forests and plant cotton. And they bring hundreds of thousands of enslaved people, many sold away from their families in Virginia and Maryland, and forced to walk south in chains.

Cotton makes some planters fabulously rich. In Natchez, on the bluffs above the river, they build huge mansions with white columns. Before the Civil War, Natchez is said to have more millionaires for its size than almost anywhere in America.

By eighteen sixty, more than half the people in Mississippi are enslaved.

## Part 3: Civil War

In January eighteen sixty-one, Mississippi becomes the second state to leave the Union. A Mississippi senator, Jefferson Davis, becomes President of the Confederacy.

## Part 3B

The city of Vicksburg sits on high bluffs over the Mississippi River. Its cannons control the river. Lincoln calls it "the key".

In eighteen sixty-three, General Ulysses S. Grant surrounds it.

For forty-seven days, the city is shelled. People dig caves in the hillsides to live in. They eat mules and rats.

On July fourth, eighteen sixty-three, Vicksburg surrenders. The Union controls the Mississippi River.

<short pause> For decades afterward, it is said, the city of Vicksburg would not celebrate the Fourth of July.

## Part 3C

After the war, something amazing happens.

In eighteen seventy, Mississippi sends Hiram Revels to Washington. He becomes the first Black United States senator in history.

And the seat he takes in the Senate? The one Jefferson Davis left behind.

## Part 3D

But it doesn't last. By eighteen ninety, Mississippi writes a new constitution with poll taxes and literacy tests, designed to stop Black citizens from voting. Other Southern states copy it.

Most Black families in the Delta end up as sharecroppers. They farm someone else's land, and are paid with a share of the crop. Many end every year deeper in debt.

## Part 4: Delta Blues

And out of that hard life comes a sound.

In the cotton fields, workers sing field hollers and work songs. In the evenings, on porches and in small country juke joints, musicians play guitars, sometimes with a knife or a bottleneck slid along the strings.

They sing about hard work, hard love, trains, floods and leaving.

The blues.

On a big Delta cotton plantation called Dockery, a guitar player named Charley Patton becomes one of the first blues stars. Others follow. Son House. Robert Johnson, who, legend says, sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads in exchange for his guitar skills.

<chuckle> Those who heard him play said that seemed like the only explanation.

## Part 4B

In nineteen-oh-two, President Theodore Roosevelt goes bear hunting in Mississippi. His guides tie a tired old bear to a tree for him to shoot. He refuses. He says it's unsportsmanlike.

A cartoonist draws the scene. A toymaker in New York makes a stuffed bear and calls it "Teddy's bear".

The teddy bear.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In nineteen twenty-seven, the Mississippi River breaks through its levees. The Great Flood covers much of the Delta for months.

Hundreds of thousands of people lose their homes. Many Black families are forced to stay in levee camps under armed guard, and made to work.

## Part 4D

And so, Black Mississippians leave. By the hundreds of thousands. On the Illinois Central railroad, north to Chicago. The Great Migration.

In nineteen forty-three, a Delta sharecropper named McKinley Morganfield gets on that train. In Chicago, he plugs in his guitar.

Muddy Waters. The electric blues.

## Part 4E

And in nineteen thirty-five, in a two-room house in Tupelo, Mississippi, a boy named Elvis Presley is born. He grows up poor, listening to gospel at church, and to Black blues and white country music on the radio.

In Memphis, he mixes them together.

Rock and roll.

The Rolling Stones even named themselves after a Muddy Waters song.

## Part 5: Civil Rights

But at home, Mississippi is the hardest place in America to be Black.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In August nineteen fifty-five, fourteen-year-old Emmett Till, visiting relatives from Chicago, is kidnapped and murdered.

His mother insists on an open coffin, so the world can see what was done to her son. The men accused are found not guilty. Months later, they admit to the killing in a magazine.

## Part 5C

In nineteen sixty-two, James Meredith becomes the first Black student at the University of Mississippi. Riots break out. The President sends in the army.

A sharecropper named Fannie Lou Hamer is beaten for trying to register to vote. She becomes one of the bravest voices of the movement. "I'm sick and tired of being sick and tired," she says.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In nineteen sixty-three, civil rights leader Medgar Evers is shot and killed in his own driveway in Jackson.

In the summer of nineteen sixty-four, three young civil rights workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, are murdered near Philadelphia, Mississippi.

## Part 6: Today

Change comes slowly. In nineteen ninety-five, Mississippi votes to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery. But nobody sends the paperwork to Washington.

In two thousand twelve, a professor watches the movie "Lincoln", gets curious, and looks it up. The paperwork is finally filed in two thousand thirteen.

And in two thousand twenty, Mississippi retires the last state flag with a Confederate battle emblem. The new flag has a magnolia on it.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In August two thousand five, Hurricane Katrina's storm surge smashes the Mississippi coast. Whole towns are wiped away. More than two hundred people in Mississippi are killed.

## Part 6C

So how does the poorest state in America end up giving the world the blues, and rock and roll?

It starts with some of the richest soil on Earth.

The Delta's soil made cotton king. Cotton brought slavery, and then sharecropping. And out of all that work and all that pain came a music nobody else could have made. It rode the trains north, into Chicago, into Memphis, into Elvis, and out to the whole world.

<short pause> Mississippi's soil grew cotton. Its people grew something far more valuable.

<chuckle> And every guitar solo you've ever heard owes them a little thank-you.
