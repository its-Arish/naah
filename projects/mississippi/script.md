# The Entire History of Mississippi

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,450 spoken words, so roughly 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, warm, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen fifty-five"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm river storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Rich and grand, a little rowdy, then honest and heavy. |
| Part 3 | Tense and steady, then quietly amazed. |
| Part 4 | Bluesy, soulful, slow-burning, with a grin. |
| Part 5 | Brave and serious. |
| Part 6 | Reflective, then warm, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Mound Builders 10,000 BC to 1699 · French and Spanish 1699 to 1798 · Cotton Kingdom 1798 to 1860 · Civil War 1861 to 1865 · Delta Blues 1865 to 1945 · Civil Rights 1954 to 1970 · Today 1970 to now.

## Hook

The teddy bear? <short pause> It's named after a bear hunt in Mississippi. Kermit the Frog? His creator grew up in Mississippi. And the first Coca-Cola ever sold in a bottle? Vicksburg, Mississippi.

<chuckle> Elvis Presley? Born in Mississippi. B.B. King, Muddy Waters, and the blues itself? Mississippi.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-five, a fourteen-year-old boy from Chicago, Emmett Till, was murdered here, after being accused of whistling at a white woman.

And today, Mississippi is the poorest state in America.

<short pause> So how does the poorest state in America end up giving the world the blues, and rock and roll?

<whispers> It starts with some of the richest soil on Earth.

## Part 1: Mound Builders

The Mississippi River is the biggest river in North America. <short pause> Every spring, for thousands of years, it floods. And every time it floods, it leaves behind a fresh layer of dark, rich mud.

In the northwest of the state, between the Mississippi and the Yazoo rivers, that mud has built up a giant, flat, fertile plain. Two hundred miles long. <gasp> In places, the mud is dozens of feet deep.

The Mississippi Delta. Some of the richest farm soil in the world.

## Part 1B

For centuries, people build towns of earth mounds here. Near Natchez stands Emerald Mound, one of the biggest mounds in North America. It covers about eight acres. Built by hand, one basket of earth at a time.

The Choctaw, the Chickasaw and the Natchez live across the land. The Natchez are ruled by a leader called the Great Sun. <short pause> He's said to be descended from the sun itself. When he travels, he's carried on a litter, so his feet never touch the ground.

<short pause> And the Choctaw have a sacred mound called Nanih Waiya. The Mother Mound. In one of their stories, the first Choctaw people climbed out of the earth there, and dried themselves in the sun.

In fifteen forty-one, the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto becomes the first European to see the Mississippi River. <sigh> He dies near it a year later. His men sink his body in the river, so the local people won't find out he was only human.

In sixteen ninety-nine, the French build a fort at Biloxi, on the Gulf Coast.

## Part 1C (quiet style)

In seventeen twenty-nine, the Natchez rise up against French settlers taking their land.

<breath> The French and their allies strike back. Within a few years, the Natchez nation is destroyed.

Survivors are scattered among other nations, or sold into slavery in the Caribbean.

## Part 2: Cotton Kingdom

In the early eighteen-hundreds, Mississippi is the wild frontier.

Farmers in Ohio and Kentucky float their crops down the river on big wooden flatboats, to Natchez and New Orleans. <chuckle> When they get there, they sell the crops... and then sell the boat, for firewood and timber.

Then they walk home. Hundreds of miles, along an old Native trail through the forest called the Natchez Trace. <groan> Through swamps, past snakes, and robbers waiting for men with money in their pockets.

At the bottom of the cliffs in Natchez, there's a rough riverside neighbourhood called Natchez-Under-the-Hill. Gambling, fighting, drinking. <laugh> Even the boatmen think it's rowdy.

In eighteen seventeen, Mississippi becomes the twentieth state.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

And then the cotton gin turns that rich soil into money. Enormous amounts of money.

But first, the land has to be taken.

<breath> In eighteen thirty, the Choctaw are pressured into signing a treaty at Dancing Rabbit Creek. They give up their homeland, more than ten million acres. They are the first nation removed under the Indian Removal Act.

<long pause> Thousands die on the way west. The Chickasaw are forced out soon after.

## Part 2C

Planters pour in. They clear the forests and plant cotton. <sigh> And they bring hundreds of thousands of enslaved people, many sold away from their families in Virginia and Maryland, and forced to walk south in chains.

Cotton makes some planters fabulously rich. In Natchez, on the bluffs above the river, they build huge mansions with white columns. <gasp> Before the Civil War, Natchez is said to have more millionaires for its size than almost anywhere in America.

By eighteen sixty, more than half the people in Mississippi are enslaved.

## Part 3: Civil War

In January eighteen sixty-one, Mississippi becomes the second state to leave the Union. A Mississippi senator and cotton planter, Jefferson Davis, becomes President of the Confederacy.

## Part 3B

The city of Vicksburg sits on high bluffs over a bend in the Mississippi River. Its cannons control the river. Lincoln calls it "the key".

General Ulysses S. Grant tries everything to get at it. <tsk> He tries digging a canal, to move the river away from the city. It doesn't work. He tries sending boats through the swamps. They get stuck.

Finally, one dark night, he runs his gunboats straight past Vicksburg's cannons, marches his army around the city, and surrounds it.

<breath> For forty-seven days, the city is shelled. People dig caves in the hillsides to live in. They eat mules and rats.

On July fourth, eighteen sixty-three, Vicksburg surrenders. The Union controls the Mississippi River. <short pause> The Confederacy is cut in two.

For decades afterward, it's said, the city of Vicksburg would not celebrate the Fourth of July.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

The war is a catastrophe for Mississippi.

<breath> Tens of thousands of its soldiers die. Thousands more come home missing arms or legs.

In eighteen sixty-six, the year after the war, Mississippi spends about a fifth of its whole state budget on artificial arms and legs.

## Part 3D

After the war, something amazing happens.

<short pause> On Jefferson Davis's brother's plantation, a formerly enslaved man named Benjamin Montgomery, who had run the business side of the plantation for years, buys the land. The plantation of the Confederate President's family, owned by the people who had been enslaved there.

In eighteen seventy, Mississippi sends Hiram Revels to Washington. He becomes the first Black United States senator in history.

<gasp> And the seat he takes in the Senate? The one Jefferson Davis left behind.

A few years later, Mississippi sends another Black senator, Blanche K. Bruce. The first to serve a full six-year term.

## Part 3E

But it doesn't last. <sigh> By eighteen ninety, Mississippi writes a new constitution with poll taxes and literacy tests, designed to stop Black citizens from voting. Other Southern states copy it.

Most Black families in the Delta end up as sharecroppers. They farm someone else's land, and are paid with a share of the crop. After the landowner takes out what they owe for seeds, tools, food and rent... many end every year deeper in debt.

Born in Holly Springs, Mississippi, in the middle of the Civil War, a young journalist named Ida B. Wells starts investigating lynchings, and publishing the truth about them. <short pause> Angry mobs destroy her newspaper office. She keeps writing anyway.

## Part 4: Delta Blues

And out of that hard life comes a sound.

In the cotton fields, workers sing field hollers and work songs. In the evenings, on porches and in small country juke joints, musicians play guitars, sometimes sliding a knife or the neck of a broken bottle along the strings.

They sing about hard work, hard love, trains, floods... and leaving.

<short pause> In nineteen-oh-three, a bandleader named W.C. Handy is waiting for a late train at the Tutwiler station in the Delta. A man sits down next to him and plays guitar with a knife, singing about where the railroads cross. <chuckle> Handy later calls it the weirdest music he'd ever heard. He goes on to write it down, and becomes known as the Father of the Blues.

## Part 4B

On a big Delta cotton plantation called Dockery, a guitar player named Charley Patton becomes one of the first blues stars. He plays the guitar behind his head, and between his knees. Others follow. Son House. Howlin' Wolf.

And Robert Johnson. <whispers> Legend says he went to a crossroads at midnight, and sold his soul to the devil, in exchange for his guitar skills.

<chuckle> Those who heard him play said that seemed like the only explanation.

<sigh> He records just twenty-nine songs. He dies at twenty-seven. And those songs go on to inspire almost every rock guitarist who ever lived.

## Part 4C

In nineteen-oh-two, President Theodore Roosevelt goes bear hunting in Mississippi. After days with no luck, his guides chase down a tired old black bear and tie it to a tree for him to shoot.

<tsk> He refuses. He says it's unsportsmanlike.

A cartoonist draws the scene. A shopkeeper in New York sees it, makes a stuffed bear, and calls it "Teddy's bear".

<chuckle> The teddy bear. Named after a bear a president refused to shoot.

## Part 4D

<short pause> And Mississippi keeps inventing small things the whole world loves.

In eighteen ninety-four, in Vicksburg, a candy shop owner named Joseph Biedenharn starts putting Coca-Cola into bottles, so he can sell it to farms up the river. <laugh> Until then, you had to go to a soda fountain to get one.

And in Biloxi, Edward Barq starts selling a soft drink. Barq's root beer.

## Part 4E (quiet style)

In nineteen twenty-seven, after months of rain, the Mississippi River breaks through its levees. The Great Flood covers much of the Delta for months.

<breath> Hundreds of thousands of people lose their homes.

Many Black families are forced to stay in levee camps, under armed guard, and made to work.

## Part 4F

And so, Black Mississippians leave. By the hundreds of thousands. On the Illinois Central railroad, north to Chicago. The Great Migration.

In nineteen forty-three, a Delta sharecropper named McKinley Morganfield gets on that train. <short pause> In noisy Chicago, an acoustic guitar is too quiet to hear. So he plugs his in.

Muddy Waters. The electric blues.

<short pause> Another Delta boy, Riley King, born on a cotton plantation near Itta Bena, heads to Memphis and becomes B.B. King, the King of the Blues.

<chuckle> One night, at a dance hall, two men fighting over a woman knock over a heater, and the building catches fire. King runs out. Then he runs BACK IN, into the burning building, to save his guitar. <phew> He makes it. And from then on, he names every guitar after the woman they were fighting over. Lucille.

<chuckle> The Rolling Stones even named themselves after one of his songs.

## Part 4G

And in nineteen thirty-five, in a two-room house in Tupelo, Mississippi, a boy named Elvis Presley is born. He grows up poor, listening to gospel at church, and to Black blues and white country music on the radio.

For his eleventh birthday, he wants a rifle. <chuckle> His mother buys him a guitar instead.

In Memphis, he mixes everything he's heard together.

<gasp> Rock and roll.

## Part 4H

Mississippi also has a strange habit of producing great storytellers. <short pause> William Faulkner, who writes novel after novel about one imaginary Mississippi county, and wins the Nobel Prize. Eudora Welty. Richard Wright.

<chuckle> And in Leland, in the Delta, a boy named Jim Henson plays by the creek with his friends. One of them is called Kermit. Henson later makes a frog puppet. Many people in Leland say you can guess where its name came from.

## Part 5: Civil Rights

But at home, Mississippi is the hardest place in America to be Black.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In August nineteen fifty-five, fourteen-year-old Emmett Till, visiting relatives from Chicago, is kidnapped and murdered.

<long pause> His mother insists on an open coffin, so the world can see what was done to her son.

The men accused are found not guilty by an all-white jury. Months later, they admit to the killing, in a magazine. <breath> His death helps start the civil rights movement.

## Part 5C

In nineteen sixty-two, James Meredith becomes the first Black student at the University of Mississippi. <gasp> Riots break out on the campus. Two people are killed. The President sends in thousands of soldiers to protect one student.

<short pause> A sharecropper named Fannie Lou Hamer tries to register to vote. She's thrown off the plantation where she's worked for eighteen years. Later, she's beaten in jail.

She doesn't stop. In nineteen sixty-four, she tells the whole country, on live television, what happens to Black people who try to vote in Mississippi. "I'm sick and tired of being sick and tired," she says.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In nineteen sixty-three, civil rights leader Medgar Evers is shot and killed in his own driveway in Jackson, in front of his family.

<long pause> In the summer of nineteen sixty-four, three young civil rights workers, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, are murdered near Philadelphia, Mississippi.

<breath> That summer, hundreds of student volunteers come to Mississippi anyway, to help Black citizens register to vote. Freedom Summer.

## Part 6: Today

<short pause> In nineteen sixty-three, at the University of Mississippi Medical Center in Jackson, a surgeon named James Hardy performs the first human lung transplant in the world.

Change at the State Capitol comes more slowly. <tsk> In nineteen ninety-five, Mississippi votes to ratify the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery. But nobody sends the paperwork to Washington.

<chuckle> In two thousand twelve, a professor watches the movie "Lincoln", gets curious, and looks it up. The paperwork is finally filed in two thousand thirteen.

And in two thousand twenty, Mississippi retires the last state flag with a Confederate battle emblem. The new flag has a magnolia on it.

<short pause> And Mississippi keeps sending its children out into the world. Football legends Walter Payton and Jerry Rice. And a little girl born in Kosciusko, Mississippi, in nineteen fifty-four, who lives with her grandmother on a farm, and starts speaking in church at three years old.

<laugh> Oprah Winfrey. Still talking.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In August two thousand five, Hurricane Katrina's storm surge smashes into the Mississippi coast. A wall of water nearly thirty feet high.

<breath> Whole towns are wiped away. More than two hundred people in Mississippi are killed.

## Part 6C

So how does the poorest state in America end up giving the world the blues, and rock and roll?

<whispers> It starts with some of the richest soil on Earth.

The Delta's soil made cotton king. Cotton brought slavery, and then sharecropping. <sigh> And out of all that work and all that pain came a music nobody else could have made.

It rode the trains north, into Chicago. Down the road, into Memphis. Into a boy from Tupelo. And out to the whole world.

<short pause> Mississippi's soil grew cotton. Its people grew something far more valuable.

<chuckle> And every guitar solo you've ever heard owes them a little thank-you.
