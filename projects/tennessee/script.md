# The Entire History of Tennessee

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,700 spoken words, so roughly 14 to 15 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In the nineteen forties"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, warm, misty-mountain storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Rowdy frontier energy, wry. |
| Part 3 | Serious and steady. |
| Part 4 | Heavy, then proud and uplifting. |
| Part 5 | Lively, a little cheeky, then tense. |
| Part 6 | Hushed and secretive, then grave. |
| Part 7 | Musical, bright, then calm and certain, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1760 · Frontier 1760 to 1796 · Volunteers 1796 to 1860 · Civil War 1861 to 1877 · Monkeys and Votes 1878 to 1933 · The Valley 1933 to 1945 · Music City 1945 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Mountain Dew? <short pause> Invented in Tennessee. And back then, "mountain dew" was a nickname for moonshine.

<chuckle> Jack Daniel's whiskey? It's made in a "dry" county.

American women got the right to vote because of one twenty-four-year-old in Tennessee. And a letter from his mom.

<long pause> In the nineteen forties, Tennessee built a secret city of seventy-five thousand people that wasn't on any map.

And inside it, they made the fuel for the first atomic bomb ever used in war.

<short pause> So how does a poor mountain state, famous for moonshine, end up building the atomic bomb?

<whispers> It starts with a river that kept flooding.

## Part 1: First Peoples

The Tennessee River is a strange river. <short pause> It starts in the mountains, flows south into Alabama, then turns around and flows north, all the way back across Tennessee, to join the Ohio.

<chuckle> It's like it changed its mind halfway.

And it floods. Every spring, the river and its streams swell with rain from the mountains, and pour over the valleys.

## Part 1B

People have lived along it for more than twelve thousand years. Mound builders raise great earthen platforms, at places like Pinson, in the west.

Later, the Cherokee build towns along the rivers of the east. Their most important town, Chota, is a city of refuge. <short pause> Anyone who reaches it, even someone accused of murder, is safe inside.

Another Cherokee town is called Tanasi. Its name, written down by English settlers, becomes "Tennessee".

In the west, the Chickasaw, famous warriors, hunt the land between the Tennessee and the Mississippi.

<short pause> Among the Cherokee, a woman named Nanyehi earns the title Ghigau. <short pause> Beloved Woman. She fights in battle as a young wife, and later sits on the council and decides the fate of prisoners. Settlers call her Nancy Ward. Again and again, she tries to keep the peace between her people and the newcomers.

## Part 1C

In the east rise the Great Smoky Mountains. Blue haze hangs over them, from the moisture breathed out by millions of trees. <short pause> The Cherokee call them the place of blue smoke.

Today, they're the most visited national park in America.

<short pause> And for about two weeks every June, in the forest at Elkmont, thousands of fireflies do something almost nowhere else on Earth. <gasp> They flash in sync. All at once. Then darkness. Then all at once again.

## Part 2: Frontier

In the seventeen sixties, settlers start crossing the Appalachian Mountains. Long hunters, who disappear into the woods for months at a time, bringing back piles of deer hides. Daniel Boone. Families from Virginia and North Carolina.

<tsk> Britain has told them not to cross the mountains. They cross anyway.

In seventeen seventy-two, settlers along the Watauga River write their own rules and govern themselves. On land that isn't theirs. <chuckle> They lease it from the Cherokee. It's one of the first written constitutions west of the mountains.

## Part 2B

In the Revolution, these "Overmountain Men" cross back over the mountains in seventeen eighty, and wipe out a loyalist army at Kings Mountain, in South Carolina. <short pause> Thomas Jefferson later calls it the turn of the tide.

## Part 2C

In the winter of seventeen seventy-nine, a group of settlers led by James Robertson drive their cattle across the frozen Cumberland River and build a fort on its bluff.

Their families come the long way. By boat. <gasp> A thousand miles, down the Tennessee River, up the Ohio, and up the Cumberland. Through rapids, smallpox, ice and attacks. One of the passengers is a girl named Rachel Donelson. She'll grow up to marry Andrew Jackson.

The fort becomes Nashville.

<chuckle> And forty years later, Jackson and two partners lay out a town on the Mississippi bluffs, and name it after an ancient city on the River Nile. <short pause> Memphis.

## Part 2D

After the Revolution, the land belongs to North Carolina. But North Carolina is far away, and doesn't help.

<chuckle> So in seventeen eighty-four, the settlers in the east just... declare a new state. They call it Franklin, after Benjamin Franklin, hoping he'll like it.

Congress never accepts it. Franklin has its own governor, its own courts, and for a while, people pay taxes and salaries in animal skins. A deer skin for this. Otter skins for that. <laugh> A raccoon skin is worth one shilling and threepence.

After four years, Franklin falls apart.

In seventeen ninety-six, Tennessee becomes the sixteenth state. The first one made from federal territory.

## Part 2E

Tennessee's frontiersmen love a fight.

Andrew Jackson, a Nashville lawyer and general, is famous for his temper. In a duel in eighteen-oh-six, he lets his opponent shoot first. <gasp> The bullet hits him in the chest, near his heart. Jackson stays standing, takes careful aim, and kills the man.

The bullet stays in Jackson's chest for the rest of his life.

He wins the Battle of New Orleans. And in eighteen twenty-eight, he's elected president.

## Part 2F

Davy Crockett, a backwoods hunter and congressman from Tennessee, loses his re-election. <chuckle> He tells voters they can go to hell. He's going to Texas. He dies at the Alamo.

Sam Houston, Tennessee's governor, quits and goes to Texas too. He becomes president of Texas.

And in eighteen forty-six, when the government asks Tennessee for about twenty-six hundred volunteers for the war with Mexico, around thirty thousand men show up.

<laugh> That's why it's called the Volunteer State.

## Part 3: Volunteers

But Jackson, as president, signs the Indian Removal Act.

The Cherokee have farms, schools, a constitution, and their own writing system, invented by Sequoyah, who was born in Tennessee.

At Red Clay, in southeastern Tennessee, the Cherokee hold their last councils in the East, beside a spring that still flows today.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-eight, many Cherokee are rounded up and held in camps in southeastern Tennessee. From there, they are forced west.

<long pause> Thousands die on the Trail of Tears.

## Part 4: Civil War

In eighteen sixty-one, Tennessee is the last state to leave the Union. The mountain people of the east mostly stay loyal to the Union. The cotton counties of the west want to leave.

<short pause> Tennessee sees more battles than any state except Virginia.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In April eighteen sixty-two, near a little log church called Shiloh, the armies fight for two days.

<breath> More than twenty-three thousand men are killed, wounded or missing. More than in all of America's earlier wars put together.

<long pause> And in November eighteen sixty-four, at Franklin, Confederate soldiers charge across two miles of open fields, into Union guns. In about five hours, six Confederate generals are killed.

## Part 4C

After the war, a Tennessean named Andrew Johnson becomes president, after Lincoln is killed. <tsk> He becomes the first president ever impeached. He survives by one vote.

And Tennessee is the first Confederate state let back into the Union.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

But in eighteen sixty-five, in Pulaski, Tennessee, a group of former Confederate soldiers starts a secret society. The Ku Klux Klan.

<breath> It spreads terror against Black citizens across the South.

## Part 4E

But out of that same time comes something beautiful.

In Nashville, a new school for freed people, Fisk University, is running out of money. <short pause> So in eighteen seventy-one, a group of its students, most of them formerly enslaved, set out on a singing tour. They sing spirituals. Songs their families sang in slavery.

At first, audiences don't know what to make of them. Then they're a sensation. They sing for President Grant. They sing for Queen Victoria.

<gasp> They raise enough money to save the school. The Fisk Jubilee Singers.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

In eighteen seventy-eight, yellow fever sweeps through Memphis.

<breath> Most people who can, flee the city. Of those who stay, thousands die. More than five thousand people in all.

<long pause> Memphis is so devastated that it gives up its city charter for years.

## Part 4G

In Memphis, a young Black teacher and journalist named Ida B. Wells writes about lynching. She investigates every case, and publishes the facts. <short pause> A mob destroys her newspaper's office. She keeps writing. From the North. For the rest of her life.

## Part 5: Monkeys and Votes

In nineteen twenty, America is one state away from letting women vote. Thirty-five states have approved the Nineteenth Amendment. They need thirty-six.

It comes down to Tennessee. <short pause> And the vote is tied.

A twenty-four-year-old legislator named Harry Burn is wearing a red rose. Red means "vote no". But in his pocket is a letter from his mother. "Hurrah, and vote for suffrage," she wrote. "Be a good boy."

<gasp> He votes yes.

<short pause> And that's how women won the vote in America. <chuckle> Because a young man listened to his mom.

## Part 5B

Two years earlier, in the First World War, a red-headed farmer and turkey shooter from the hills of Pall Mall, Tennessee, named Alvin York, is sent to France.

<gasp> In October nineteen eighteen, his small patrol is pinned down by German machine guns. York, using the sharpshooting he learned hunting in the Tennessee hills, takes out the gunners. <short pause> He and a few men march back with a hundred and thirty-two German prisoners.

<chuckle> He comes home a national hero. Turns down fortunes. And uses his fame to build a school back home.

## Part 5C

Five years later, Tennessee is in the news again.

A new law bans teaching evolution in public schools. In the little town of Dayton, a teacher named John Scopes is put on trial for it. Reporters come from everywhere. The newspapers call it the Monkey Trial.

<laugh> Scopes is found guilty and fined a hundred dollars. The town got a lot more famous than the teacher.

<short pause> Down the river, Chattanooga invents things too. In eighteen ninety-nine, two lawyers there buy the rights to bottle Coca-Cola, for one dollar. <gasp> The first Coca-Cola bottling plant. And in nineteen seventeen, a Chattanooga bakery asks coal miners what snack they'd like. Something filling, they say, as big as the moon. <chuckle> The Moon Pie.

## Part 5D

And Tennessee's whiskey has its own story.

<short pause> A young boy named Jack Daniel learns how to make whiskey from an enslaved man named Nathan "Nearest" Green, who's working for a local preacher. After the war, Jack starts his own distillery. And he hires Nearest Green as his first master distiller.

<sigh> For more than a hundred years, the company barely mentions him. Today, his name is finally back in the story.

## Part 6: The Valley

But Tennessee has a bigger problem. <short pause> It's poor. Really poor.

The Tennessee River floods again and again, washing away farms. The soil is worn out. Most farms have no electricity. Families light their homes with kerosene lamps, and pump water by hand.

In nineteen thirty-three, during the Great Depression, President Franklin Roosevelt creates the Tennessee Valley Authority. The TVA.

It builds dam after dam on the Tennessee River and its streams. <short pause> The dams stop the floods. And they make cheap electricity. Lots of it.

<gasp> For the first time, farm families get electric lights. Refrigerators. Radios.

<chuckle> The TVA even builds a model town for its dam workers, Norris, with houses that are all-electric. Electric stoves. Electric heat. Something most Tennesseans had never seen.

## Part 6B

And then, the Second World War.

The United States needs to build an atomic bomb. To do it, it needs to separate a rare kind of uranium. That takes giant factories, and an enormous amount of electricity.

<short pause> Where can you find that much power? In a quiet, hidden valley in East Tennessee, near the new TVA dams.

In nineteen forty-two, the army buys up the farms. Thousands of people are given a few weeks to leave.

## Part 6C

And they build a city from nothing. Houses, schools, shops and giant plants. One of them, K-twenty-five, is the largest building in the world.

<short pause> Oak Ridge's plants swallow an enormous share of all the electricity being made in America. All of it, flowing out of those TVA dams.

They call it Site X. Then Oak Ridge. By nineteen forty-five, seventy-five thousand people live there.

<whispers> It's not on any map.

Billboards tell workers to keep their mouths shut. Most workers have no idea what they're making. <chuckle> Young women, fresh from high school, are hired to watch dials and turn knobs all day. They're better at it than the scientists.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

On August sixth, nineteen forty-five, a uranium bomb is dropped on Hiroshima, Japan.

<long pause> The uranium inside it was made in Oak Ridge.

<short pause> Only then do the people of Oak Ridge find out what they had been building.

## Part 7: Music City

After the war, Tennessee becomes famous for something much louder.

In Nashville, the Grand Ole Opry radio show, which started in nineteen twenty-five, turns country music into a national sound.

<short pause> Two years later, in nineteen twenty-seven, a record producer sets up a microphone in an empty building in Bristol, on the Tennessee-Virginia line, and invites local musicians to come and record. <gasp> In two weeks, he records the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers. People call those sessions the big bang of country music.

In Memphis, in nineteen fifty-four, at a little studio called Sun Records, a truck driver named Elvis Presley records "That's All Right". <gasp> Rock and roll is born. Johnny Cash, Jerry Lee Lewis and Carl Perkins follow.

And in Memphis, Stax Records makes soul music, and the blues fill Beale Street.

## Part 7B

In nineteen sixty, Black college students in Nashville sit down at whites-only lunch counters and refuse to leave. They are arrested, beaten and insulted. <short pause> They keep coming back. Within months, Nashville's lunch counters integrate.

<short pause> In the mountains near Monteagle, a little school called Highlander trains activists. In the summer of nineteen fifty-five, a seamstress from Montgomery takes a workshop there. <short pause> Her name is Rosa Parks. A few months later, she refuses to give up her bus seat.

At Highlander, an old church song is slowed down and changed, and becomes the anthem of the movement. We Shall Overcome.

## Part 7C (quiet style)

In April nineteen sixty-eight, Martin Luther King Jr. comes to Memphis to support striking sanitation workers.

<long pause> On April fourth, he is shot and killed on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel.

The motel is now the National Civil Rights Museum.

## Part 7D

And from the Smoky Mountains comes a girl who grew up with eleven brothers and sisters in a one-room cabin. <short pause> Dolly Parton.

She becomes one of the biggest stars in country music, builds a theme park called Dollywood, and sends free books to millions of children.

<short pause> And a girl from Clarksville, Wilma Rudolph, gets polio as a small child. Doctors say she'll never walk properly. She wears a metal brace on her leg for years.

<gasp> At the nineteen sixty Olympics in Rome, she wins three gold medals in sprinting. The fastest woman in the world.

<short pause> In Memphis, Elvis buys a mansion called Graceland, and lives there until he dies in nineteen seventy-seven. Today, it's one of the most visited homes in America.

<chuckle> In nineteen seventy-three, a company called Federal Express starts flying packages overnight, from Memphis. Today, every night, hundreds of planes land at Memphis, packages are sorted, and they all take off again before dawn.

<chuckle> And Nashville today is one of the fastest-growing cities in America. Honky-tonks on Broadway play live music from morning until late at night.

## Part 7E

So how does a poor mountain state, famous for moonshine, end up building the atomic bomb?

<short pause> It starts with a river that kept flooding.

To stop the floods, the TVA built the dams. The dams made cheap power. And when America needed more electricity than anywhere could give, to build a weapon nobody had ever built... <short pause> it came to the valley that the river had made.

<long pause> Tennessee has always been a place where ordinary people end up changing history. A boy who listened to his mother. Students who sang to save their school. A truck driver with a guitar. A secret city of people who didn't know what they were making.

<chuckle> And a soft drink named after moonshine.
