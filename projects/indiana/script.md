# The Entire History of Indiana

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "And in the nineteen-twenties"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm storyteller of forests and rivers, then daring. |
| Part 2 | Frontier storyteller, gentle and warm with young Lincoln. |
| Part 3 | Booming, inventive and a little silly. |
| Part 4 | Roaring race-day commentator energy. |
| Part 5 | Serious, then lively, sporty and proud; smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1732 · Frontier 1732 to 1816 · Hoosiers 1816 to 1865 · Gas Boom 1865 to 1909 · Brickyard 1909 to 1920 · Dark Years 1920 to 1930 · Hoops and Roads 1930 to now.

## Hook

The Indy 500? <short pause> The biggest single-day sporting event on Earth.

Abraham Lincoln? He grew up in Indiana. Not Illinois.

<chuckle> In eighteen ninety-seven, Indiana's lawmakers almost passed a law that said pi equals three point two. And there's a town in Indiana called Santa Claus. Every Christmas, kids send it thousands of letters.

<long pause> And in the nineteen-twenties, the Ku Klux Klan practically ran Indiana. Hundreds of thousands of members. Its governor. Its legislature.

<short pause> So how does a quiet farm state end up hosting the biggest one-day sporting event in the world?

<whispers> It starts with a race track paved with three million bricks.

## Part 1: First Peoples

On the shore of Lake Michigan, the wind has piled up giant dunes of sand, some taller than a twenty-storey building. <short pause> They creep slowly inland, swallowing forests. Scientists studying how plants take over these dunes help invent a whole new science. Ecology.

A thousand years ago, on the Ohio River, people of the Mississippian culture build a town of mounds and wooden houses, with a tall wall around it. Today it's called Angel Mounds.

Later, the forests and prairies of Indiana are home to the Miami, the Potawatomi, the Shawnee, the Lenape and others. <chuckle> The name "Indiana" simply means "land of the Indians".

## Part 1B

In seventeen thirty-two, French traders build a fort at Vincennes, on the Wabash River.

During the Revolution, the British take it. <gasp> So in the middle of winter, an American frontiersman named George Rogers Clark leads about a hundred and seventy men across flooded prairie, for more than two weeks. The freezing water is sometimes up to their shoulders. They run out of food.

<chuckle> When they finally reach Vincennes, Clark marches his men back and forth behind a low hill, waving lots of flags, so the British think his army is much bigger than it is.

<laugh> The British surrender.

## Part 1C

After the Revolution, the Miami and their allies fight to keep their land. <gasp> In seventeen ninety-one, the Miami war chief Little Turtle and the Shawnee leader Blue Jacket destroy an American army near the Indiana border. More than six hundred soldiers are killed. <short pause> It's the worst defeat the U.S. Army ever suffers against Native nations.

## Part 2: Frontier

In eighteen-eleven, the Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his brother, the Prophet, gather many nations at Prophetstown, on the Wabash, to resist the Americans together.

While Tecumseh is away, Governor William Henry Harrison attacks. The Battle of Tippecanoe. Prophetstown is burned.

<chuckle> Harrison turns it into a campaign slogan, and decades later, rides it into the White House. "Tippecanoe and Tyler too!"

<sigh> He gives the longest inaugural speech in history, in the cold, without a coat. A month later, he's dead. The shortest presidency in history.

## Part 2B

In December eighteen sixteen, Indiana becomes the nineteenth state. Its first capital is the little town of Corydon.

<short pause> The same month, a family crosses the Ohio River from Kentucky into the deep woods of southern Indiana. The father is Thomas Lincoln. His son is seven years old. Abraham.

Abe grows up in Indiana. He clears land with an axe. <chuckle> He reads every book he can borrow, sometimes walking miles to get one. He spends fourteen years here, from seven to twenty-one.

At nineteen, he takes a flatboat full of goods down the river, all the way to New Orleans. <sigh> There, he sees enslaved people being sold.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

When Abe is nine, his mother, Nancy, drinks milk from a cow that has eaten a poisonous plant. It's called milk sickness.

<breath> She dies. Abe helps his father make her coffin.

<long pause> Ten years later, his older sister, Sarah, dies giving birth to her first child.

## Part 2D

<chuckle> In eighteen twenty-five, a rich Welsh factory owner named Robert Owen buys a whole town in Indiana, on the Wabash. New Harmony. He wants to build a perfect society. No money. No private property. Everybody equal. Science and education for all.

Scientists and teachers arrive by boat. People call it the "Boatload of Knowledge".

<groan> It falls apart in about two years. Too many thinkers, and not enough farmers. But it leaves behind libraries, schools and scientists.

<chuckle> Wandering through Indiana in those years is a barefoot man wearing a tin pot for a hat, or so the legend says, planting apple tree nurseries everywhere he goes. John Chapman. Johnny Appleseed. <short pause> He dies near Fort Wayne in eighteen forty-five, and he's buried there.

## Part 2E

Indiana is a free state, right across the river from slave states. <short pause> In a little town near the Ohio border, a Quaker couple, Levi and Catharine Coffin, hide people escaping slavery in their house. <whispers> Behind beds, in the attic, in a hidden room.

<gasp> Over about twenty years, they help more than a thousand people escape north. Levi Coffin is nicknamed the President of the Underground Railroad.

<gasp> In July eighteen sixty-three, during the Civil War, a Confederate cavalry general, John Hunt Morgan, crosses the Ohio River and raids southern Indiana with more than two thousand horsemen. They rob stores, burn bridges and steal horses. <tsk> Then they gallop off into Ohio, where they're caught.

<short pause> One of Indiana's Union generals, Lew Wallace, later becomes a writer. He writes a novel about a chariot race in ancient Rome. Ben-Hur. It becomes one of the best-selling books of the century.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-eight, soldiers force more than eight hundred Potawatomi people out of northern Indiana at gunpoint.

<breath> They are marched to Kansas, in the heat of late summer.

<long pause> More than forty die on the way, many of them children. The Potawatomi call it the Trail of Death.

## Part 3: Gas Boom

People from Indiana are called "Hoosiers". Nobody knows exactly why. There are dozens of theories. <chuckle> None of them are proven.

One theory says pioneers answered a knock at the cabin door by shouting "Who's here?" <short pause> Another says a canal builder named Hoosier liked hiring men from Indiana. <laugh> The poet James Whitcomb Riley joked that it came from brawling settlers biting off each other's ears, and shouting, "Whose ear?"

## Part 3B

In the eighteen-eighties, drillers in central Indiana hit natural gas. Huge amounts. <laugh> Towns light giant gas torches day and night, just to show off. Gas is so cheap that people leave the lights on all day. Factories rush in.

The Ball brothers move their glass jar factory to Muncie. Ball jars.

<short pause> In Kokomo, in eighteen ninety-four, an inventor named Elwood Haynes drives one of the first petrol-powered cars in America down the road at about seven miles an hour. <short pause> In South Bend, the Studebaker brothers build wagons, then cars.

<sigh> They waste so much gas that, within about fifteen years, it runs out. But the factories stay.

## Part 3C

In nineteen-oh-six, the U.S. Steel company builds a whole new city on the sand dunes of Lake Michigan, around a giant steel mill. It's named after the company's chairman. Gary.

<short pause> In Indianapolis, a woman who started out washing clothes for a dollar and a half a day, Madam C.J. Walker, builds a business making hair products for Black women. <gasp> She becomes one of the first women in America to become a millionaire by her own work.

<short pause> And in the south of the state, quarrymen cut huge blocks of pale limestone out of the hills. <gasp> Indiana limestone. It's used to build the Empire State Building, the Pentagon, and the Washington National Cathedral.

<chuckle> In Elkhart, factories build band instruments. Trumpets, trombones, flutes. Later, the town builds so many motorhomes that it calls itself the RV Capital of the World.

Indiana fills with car makers. Dozens of them. <chuckle> And they need somewhere to test their cars.

## Part 3D

Meanwhile, in eighteen ninety-seven, an amateur mathematician convinces an Indiana lawmaker to introduce a bill. <tsk> Its maths would make pi equal to three point two.

The House passes it. Sixty-seven to nothing.

<laugh> But a Purdue maths professor happens to be visiting the statehouse that day. He explains the problem. The Senate puts the bill away. Forever.

## Part 4: Brickyard

Now, the bricks.

In nineteen-oh-nine, a businessman named Carl Fisher builds a giant oval track in Indianapolis. A place for car companies to test their cars, and race them.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

The first races are a disaster. The track is gravel and tar. It breaks apart under the speeding cars.

<long pause> Drivers, mechanics and spectators are killed.

## Part 4C

So Fisher repaves it. With bricks. About three point two million of them.

<chuckle> Yes. Three point two. Indiana really loves that number.

People start calling it the Brickyard.

<chuckle> The Speedway is so big that you could fit Churchill Downs, Yankee Stadium, the Rose Bowl, the Roman Colosseum and the whole of Vatican City inside the oval. At the same time.

On May thirtieth, nineteen eleven, the first Indianapolis five hundred is held. Five hundred miles. Forty cars. <gasp> The winner, Ray Harroun, drives alone, without the usual mechanic riding beside him to watch for other cars. So he sticks a mirror on his car, to see behind him. <short pause> One of the first rear-view mirrors.

He averages about seventy-five miles an hour.

## Part 4D

Today, the cars go well over two hundred miles an hour. More than three hundred thousand people come to watch. It's the biggest single-day sporting event in the world.

<laugh> And the winner drinks a bottle of milk. Ever since a driver in the nineteen-thirties asked for buttermilk, and a dairy company saw an opportunity.

<short pause> At the finish line, one yard of the original bricks is still there. Winners kneel down and kiss it.

## Part 5: Dark Years (quiet style)

In the nineteen-twenties, the Ku Klux Klan grows huge in Indiana. Hundreds of thousands of members. Maybe a third of the white men in the state.

<breath> It attacks Black people, Catholics, Jews and immigrants. It helps elect the governor and many lawmakers.

<long pause> Then, in nineteen twenty-five, its leader, D.C. Stephenson, is convicted of the murder of a young woman named Madge Oberholtzer.

The Klan's power in Indiana collapses.

## Part 5B

<chuckle> In nineteen thirty-four, the bank robber John Dillinger, from Indianapolis, is locked in a jail in Crown Point that's called escape-proof.

He carves a fake gun out of wood, blackens it with shoe polish, and bluffs his way out. <laugh> He escapes in the sheriff's own car.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In the Second World War, a quiet, skinny reporter from a farm in Dana, Indiana, Ernie Pyle, writes about the war from the foxholes. <breath> Not about generals. About ordinary soldiers. Their mud, their fear, their jokes. Millions of Americans read him every day.

<long pause> In April nineteen forty-five, on a small island near Okinawa, he is killed by a Japanese machine gun.

## Part 5D

Indiana is crazy about basketball. They call it Hoosier Hysteria. <gasp> Some high-school gyms hold more people than the whole town they're in.

In nineteen fifty-four, a tiny high school from the town of Milan, with fewer than two hundred students, wins the state championship against a big-city school, with a last-second shot. <short pause> It becomes the movie "Hoosiers".

<chuckle> And a kid from French Lick, Larry Bird, becomes one of the greatest players of all time.

<chuckle> And in South Bend, the University of Notre Dame plays football like it's a religion. Under its famous coach Knute Rockne, the "Fighting Irish" become one of the most famous teams in the country.

## Part 5E

And Indiana gives the world the Jackson Five, from Gary. <short pause> Wilbur Wright, one of the brothers who invented the airplane, is born on a farm in Indiana.

The writer Kurt Vonnegut comes from Indianapolis. The actor James Dean grows up on a farm in Fairmount. <chuckle> And the late-night TV host David Letterman starts out as a weatherman in Indianapolis. He once congratulated a tropical storm on being upgraded to a hurricane.

## Part 5F (quiet style)

On April fourth, nineteen sixty-eight, Senator Robert Kennedy is campaigning for president in a Black neighbourhood of Indianapolis. On the way, he hears that Martin Luther King Junior has been shot and killed.

<breath> The crowd doesn't know yet. Kennedy climbs onto a flatbed truck, and tells them.

<long pause> He speaks quietly, about his own brother, who was also murdered. He asks for compassion, not revenge.

That night, riots break out in more than a hundred American cities. Indianapolis stays calm.

Purdue University trains so many astronauts that it calls itself the "Cradle of Astronauts". <gasp> The first man to walk on the Moon, Neil Armstrong, studied there. So did the last, Gene Cernan.

In the town of Santa Claus, volunteers called "elves" answer every letter.

<laugh> And Indiana couldn't decide about daylight saving time for decades. Different counties, different clocks. The whole state only started springing forward together in two thousand six.

## Part 5G

So how does a quiet farm state end up hosting the biggest one-day sporting event in the world?

<whispers> It starts with a race track paved with three million bricks.

Indiana's gas boom brought factories, and the factories brought car makers. The car makers needed a test track. <short pause> A businessman built one in Indianapolis, and paved it with bricks. And the race he started there grew into the biggest party in sport.

<breath> Indiana calls itself the Crossroads of America. And for one day every May, the whole world drives through it.

<chuckle> At well over two hundred miles an hour.
