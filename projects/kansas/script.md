# The Entire History of Kansas

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "But before the Civil War"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Wide, calm prairie storyteller, with a wink. |
| Part 2 | Tense and urgent, a little wry. |
| Part 3 | Grim and steady, then proud. |
| Part 4 | Dusty cowtown swagger, then hopeful and stubborn. |
| Part 5 | Bright, airborne and inventive. |
| Part 6 | Determined and warm, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1854 · Bleeding Kansas 1854 to 1861 · War 1861 to 1865 · Cowtowns 1865 to 1890 · Wheat and Wings 1890 to 1945 · Brown 1945 to 1960 · Today 1960 to now.

## Hook

Pizza Hut? <short pause> Started in Kansas. White Castle, often called the first fast-food hamburger chain? Also Kansas.

<chuckle> Scientists once measured it. Kansas really is flatter than a pancake.

And Wichita builds so many airplanes, it calls itself the Air Capital of the World.

<long pause> But before the Civil War, Kansas had its own war. Neighbours murdered neighbours. Towns were burned. People called it Bleeding Kansas.

<short pause> So how does a flat, quiet prairie become the place where the Civil War really began?

<whispers> It starts with crates of rifles, labelled "Bibles".

## Part 1: First Peoples

Eighty million years ago, Kansas was underwater. <gasp> A shallow sea cut North America in half, from the Gulf of Mexico to the Arctic.

In it swam giant sea lizards called mosasaurs, longer than a bus. Sharks. And a monster fish called Xiphactinus, bigger than a man.

<chuckle> In a Kansas museum, there's a fossil of one of those giant fish... with another six-foot fish inside its stomach. It swallowed its lunch whole, and seems to have died from it.

## Part 1B

When the sea dried up, Kansas became grass. An ocean of tall grass and short grass, rolling to the horizon. Grass taller than a man on horseback, in the east. <short pause> And millions of buffalo.

Today, the Flint Hills of Kansas hold most of the tallgrass prairie left anywhere on Earth. The rocky ground was too hard to plough, so it was saved.

The Kansa people, who give the state its name, the Osage, the Pawnee and the Wichita live along its rivers. The Cheyenne, the Arapaho, the Kiowa and the Comanche hunt on its western plains.

## Part 1C

In fifteen forty-one, Coronado marches all the way to Kansas, still hunting for a rich city of gold called Quivira.

<chuckle> He finds the Wichita people, living in big, round houses made of grass. Lovely houses. No gold.

<tsk> He has his guide, who had promised him the gold, executed. And goes home.

In the eighteen-twenties, the Santa Fe Trail crosses Kansas, carrying wagon trains of goods to New Mexico. <short pause> Wagon ruts from that trail are still cut into the Kansas prairie today.

<chuckle> Before that, on July fourth, eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark camp near today's Atchison, Kansas. They fire their cannon, hand out an extra ration of whiskey, and name a stream Independence Creek. <short pause> It's the first Fourth of July celebrated west of the Mississippi.

Two years later, the explorer Zebulon Pike visits a Pawnee village in Kansas. Spanish soldiers had been there just before him, and left a Spanish flag flying. <tsk> Pike politely insists they take it down, and raise the American flag instead. The Pawnee do.

## Part 1D (quiet style)

The government moves eastern nations, like the Shawnee, the Delaware, the Kickapoo and the Potawatomi, onto reservations in Kansas.

<breath> It promises the land will be theirs forever.

<long pause> Within a few decades, most of them are moved again.

## Part 2: Bleeding Kansas

Now, the rifles.

In eighteen fifty-four, America is splitting over slavery. Every new state tips the balance in Congress. Slave or free.

<short pause> So Congress passes the Kansas-Nebraska Act. It says: let the settlers decide. Let the people of Kansas vote on whether to allow slavery.

<gasp> And immediately, both sides race to fill Kansas with their own voters.

## Part 2B

From New England, anti-slavery groups pay for families to move to Kansas. They found towns like Lawrence and Topeka.

<whispers> And they send them guns. Sharps rifles. Packed into crates, sometimes labelled "Bibles".

<chuckle> A famous preacher, Henry Ward Beecher, says a rifle might do more good in Kansas than a Bible. So people start calling the rifles "Beecher's Bibles".

## Part 2C

From next-door Missouri, a slave state, armed men called "Border Ruffians" ride into Kansas on election days. They vote. Then they ride home.

<tsk> In one election, Kansas has fewer than three thousand eligible voters. More than six thousand votes are counted.

<laugh> Kansas ends up with two governments. One for slavery. One against. Each says the other is fake.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

In May eighteen fifty-six, a pro-slavery posse rides into Lawrence, and burns its hotel and smashes its newspaper presses.

<breath> Days later, an anti-slavery man named John Brown and his sons drag five pro-slavery settlers from their homes along Pottawatomie Creek, and kill them with swords.

<long pause> Raids and revenge killings spread. Dozens of people die.

## Part 2E

Bleeding Kansas is the Civil War before the Civil War. <short pause> It even spills into Congress. A senator who gives a fierce speech about it, called "The Crime Against Kansas", is beaten nearly to death at his desk.

John Brown leaves Kansas, and in eighteen fifty-nine, tries to start an uprising of enslaved people at Harpers Ferry, in Virginia. <sigh> He is caught and hanged. Half of America calls him a murderer. The other half calls him a martyr.

<short pause> In January eighteen sixty-one, as Southern states are leaving the Union, Kansas finally joins it. As the thirty-fourth state. A free state.

## Part 2F

Kansas starts its life as a state with some very modern ideas. Its new constitution lets women vote in school elections, thanks to a campaigner named Clarina Nichols, who sat through the whole convention knitting, and lobbying.

<chuckle> And in eighteen eighty-seven, in the little town of Argonia, some men who don't like women in politics put a woman's name on the ballot for mayor, as a joke. They expect her to get embarrassed and lose.

<laugh> Susanna Salter wins. Two-thirds of the vote. She becomes the first woman mayor in America.

## Part 3: War

During the war, Kansas sends more of its men into the Union army, for its size, than almost any other state.

<gasp> And in October eighteen sixty-two, the First Kansas Colored Infantry, men who had escaped slavery, fight Confederate guerrillas at a place called Island Mound. <short pause> It's the first time Black soldiers fight in combat in the Civil War. Months before the famous fifty-fourth Massachusetts.

<short pause> In eighteen sixty-four, near Mine Creek, one of the biggest cavalry battles of the war is fought in Kansas. About ten thousand horsemen. <gasp> The Union wins, and the Confederates never threaten Kansas again.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In August eighteen sixty-three, a Confederate guerrilla named William Quantrill leads about four hundred raiders into Lawrence at dawn.

<breath> They go house to house, with lists of names.

<long pause> They kill around a hundred and fifty men and boys, and burn the town.

## Part 4: Cowtowns

After the war, Texas cowboys drive giant herds of cattle north, for weeks, to the new railroads in Kansas. Abilene. Wichita. Dodge City.

<chuckle> Cowtowns. Saloons. Gambling halls. Gunfights. Lawmen like Wyatt Earp and Wild Bill Hickok.

<short pause> A cattle dealer named Joseph McCoy builds the stockyards in Abilene, and promises Texas ranchers a fair price. <chuckle> Some say that's where the phrase "the real McCoy" comes from. Others say it isn't. Either way, the cattle keep coming. Millions of them, up the Chisholm Trail.

<laugh> Dodge City gets so rowdy, it buries people on a hill. Boot Hill. Because they died with their boots on.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

Hunters with long rifles shoot the buffalo by the millions, for their hides and to feed railroad workers. <breath> A young hunter named William Cody kills thousands in Kansas, and earns the nickname "Buffalo Bill".

<long pause> By the eighteen-eighties, the herds that once darkened the plains are almost gone.

## Part 4C

In eighteen seventy-nine, thousands of Black families leave the South, where Reconstruction has ended, and head to Kansas. <short pause> The free state John Brown fought for.

They're called Exodusters, after the Exodus in the Bible. Some settle in Black towns like Nicodemus, founded a couple of years before, out on the treeless prairie. <chuckle> The first families live in dugouts in the ground. Nicodemus still stands today.

## Part 4D

And Mennonite farmers from Russia arrive, carrying a tough kind of wheat called Turkey Red. Some say the children hand-picked the best seeds, one by one, before they left. <short pause> It survives Kansas winters.

<gasp> Kansas becomes the breadbasket of America.

## Part 4E

Kansas is also stubborn about alcohol. In eighteen eighty-one, it becomes the first state to ban alcohol in its constitution.

<chuckle> Saloons keep operating anyway. So a six-foot-tall woman named Carry Nation walks into them with a hatchet... and smashes them to pieces. <laugh> She calls it "hatchetation". She sells little souvenir hatchets to pay her fines.

<short pause> Kansas farmers get angry about banks and railroads, too. A fiery speaker named Mary Elizabeth Lease is said to tell them: raise less corn, and more hell.

## Part 4F

<chuckle> And in nineteen hundred, a writer who had never even lived in Kansas publishes a book about a girl from a grey Kansas farm, swept away by a tornado to a magical land.

The Wonderful Wizard of Oz. <short pause> Even in a land of magic, Dorothy just wants to get back to grey, flat Kansas. Because there's no place like home.

## Part 5: Wheat and Wings

In the early nineteen-hundreds, Wichita starts building airplanes. Clyde Cessna. Walter Beech. Lloyd Stearman. <chuckle> Flat land, steady wind, and plenty of room to crash.

In the Second World War, Wichita's factories build thousands of bombers. After the war, small planes for the whole world. The Air Capital.

<short pause> And a girl from Atchison, Kansas, Amelia Earhart, becomes the first woman to fly alone across the Atlantic.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In March nineteen eighteen, at Camp Funston, an army camp in Kansas, soldiers begin falling sick with the flu.

<breath> It's one of the first recorded outbreaks of the nineteen eighteen flu pandemic.

<long pause> It goes on to kill tens of millions of people around the world.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In the nineteen-thirties, the drought comes. Kansas farmers have ploughed up the grass, and the wind lifts the dry soil into the sky.

<breath> Black blizzards of dust roll across the plains. Children are sent to school wearing masks. Some people die of "dust pneumonia".

## Part 5D

In nineteen twenty-one, a Wichita cook opens a little hamburger stand. White Castle. Often called the first fast-food chain. <chuckle> Five-cent burgers.

In nineteen fifty-eight, two brothers in Wichita borrow six hundred dollars from their mother and open a small pizza place. <laugh> Its sign only has room for eight letters. Pizza Hut.

<short pause> Walter Chrysler, the car maker, grows up in Ellis, Kansas. And a boy who grew up in Abilene, Kansas, Dwight Eisenhower, leads the Allies to victory in Europe, then becomes president.

<chuckle> And a baby born in a little Kansas town, to a family of travelling show performers, takes a tumble down a flight of stairs at six months old. He's fine. Legend says a friend of the family, the great magician Harry Houdini, laughs: "That was a real buster!" <short pause> The name sticks. Buster Keaton, one of the greatest comedians of silent film.

The poet Langston Hughes grows up in Lawrence. The poet Gwendolyn Brooks is born in Topeka.

<laugh> And the man who invented basketball, James Naismith, moves to the University of Kansas to coach. He becomes the father of one of the greatest basketball programmes in America. <chuckle> He's also the only coach in its history with a losing record.

## Part 6: Brown

In Topeka, in the nineteen-fifties, a little girl named Linda Brown has to walk past a white school near her home and cross dangerous railroad tracks to get to her bus to a Black school.

<sigh> Her father, and other Black parents, try to enrol their children in the white schools near home. They're turned away. So they sue the Topeka school board.

<gasp> In nineteen fifty-four, the Supreme Court rules on Brown versus Board of Education. All nine justices agree. Separate schools for Black and white children are unconstitutional.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In May two thousand seven, a giant tornado, almost two miles wide, hits the little town of Greensburg at night.

<breath> About ninety-five percent of the town is destroyed. Eleven people are killed.

<short pause> The people who stay decide to rebuild the whole town green, with wind turbines and energy-saving buildings.

## Part 6C

<chuckle> Today, near the little town of Lebanon, there's a stone marker for the geographic centre of the forty-eight connected states. Right in the middle of Kansas.

<chuckle> And here's one that confuses everyone. The big Kansas City? Most of it is in Missouri.

And in two thousand three, scientists actually compared Kansas to a pancake from a famous pancake restaurant. <laugh> They scanned the pancake with a laser. Kansas was flatter.

<short pause> Deep under the town of Hutchinson, there's a giant old salt mine. <whispers> Inside, in tunnels that stay cool and dry all year, movie studios store their original film reels, and companies store secret records. <chuckle> And a museum in Hutchinson holds a real spacecraft. The capsule from Apollo Thirteen.

## Part 6D

So how does a flat, quiet prairie become the place where the Civil War really began?

<whispers> It starts with crates of rifles, labelled "Bibles".

Congress let Kansas decide for itself about slavery. So both sides poured in, with votes and with guns. <short pause> The fighting in Kansas showed America that the argument over slavery could only end in war.

<breath> And a century later, it was a little girl from Topeka whose name went on the case that ended segregation in America's schools.

<chuckle> Kansas may be flatter than a pancake. But it has never been boring.
