# The Entire History of Virginia

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,800 spoken words, so roughly 15 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "The first English settlers"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, curious storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Tense, then wry, then serious. |
| Part 3 | Rising, grand, revolutionary, then honest. |
| Part 4 | Serious and steady, then amazed at the McLean story. |
| Part 5 | Measured, then hopeful and modern. |
| Part 6 | Calm and certain, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Powhatan 10,000 BC to 1607 · Jamestown 1607 to 1699 · Colony 1699 to 1775 · Revolution 1775 to 1788 · Slavery and War 1800 to 1865 · Jim Crow 1865 to 1965 · Modern 1965 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Eight American presidents were born in Virginia. <short pause> More than any other state.

The Pentagon? Virginia. The CIA? Also Virginia.

<chuckle> Arlington National Cemetery used to be Robert E. Lee's front yard.

<long pause> The first English settlers here got so hungry, they ate their own dead.

And in one single summer, sixteen nineteen, Virginia gave America its first elected assembly. And its first enslaved Africans.

<short pause> So how does one colony give America freedom and slavery in the same summer?

<whispers> It starts with a plant the king of England hated.

## Part 1: Powhatan

Before the English, this land is called Tsenacommacah. <short pause> The densely inhabited land.

It's ruled by a powerful leader named Wahunsenacah, whom the English call Powhatan. He leads an alliance of around thirty nations, maybe fifteen thousand people or more, along the rivers that flow into Chesapeake Bay.

They live in towns of bark-covered houses, surrounded by fields of corn, beans and squash. They fish with weirs and nets. They trade copper and shell beads. Each town has its own chief, and each chief pays tribute to Powhatan.

<short pause> And Powhatan has a young daughter. Her real name is Matoaka. But everyone knows her childhood nickname. Pocahontas. Roughly... the playful one.

## Part 1B

Virginia had seen Europeans before.

In the fifteen-sixties, a Spanish ship kidnaps a young man from this coast and takes him to Spain and Mexico. The Spanish name him Don Luis. He learns Spanish, meets the King of Spain, and becomes a Catholic.

<short pause> Ten years later, Spanish Jesuit priests bring him home, to help them build a mission. Don Luis walks off into the forest, back to his people.

<breath> A few months later, he returns with warriors. And the mission is wiped out.

<sigh> Some historians think Don Luis may even have been Opechancanough. Powhatan's brother. If so, he spent the rest of his long life watching the sea for the next Europeans.

## Part 2: Jamestown

In May sixteen-oh-seven, three English ships sail into the Chesapeake, and up a river they name the James, after their king. About a hundred men and boys build a triangle-shaped fort on a swampy island.

Jamestown. The first permanent English settlement in America.

<chuckle> The spot is easy to defend. It's also a mosquito-filled swamp, with salty, dirty water.

They're a company. They're here to make money. And many of them are gentlemen who have never worked with their hands. <tsk> They look for gold. They find fool's gold, and load a ship with it. They find disease and hunger.

<short pause> They also build America's first factory. A glass furnace, in sixteen-oh-eight, run by Polish and German craftsmen.

<chuckle> And in sixteen nineteen, when the Polish workers are told they can't vote in the colony's new assembly, they put down their tools and refuse to work. They win. It's often called the first strike in American history.

## Part 2B

One of their leaders is Captain John Smith. Short, bossy, and a brilliant self-promoter.

He's captured by Powhatan's warriors. Years later, he writes that he was about to be clubbed to death, when young Pocahontas threw herself over him and saved his life.

<chuckle> Historians argue about whether it really happened. It might have been a ceremony he didn't understand. Or a story he made better with time.

What we do know: Smith takes charge, and sets a hard rule. He who does not work, shall not eat.

Then, in sixteen-oh-nine, he's badly burned in a gunpowder accident, and sails home.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

The winter that follows is called the Starving Time.

<breath> The Powhatan surround the fort. Supply ships are lost in a hurricane. The colonists eat their horses. Then their dogs. Rats. Snakes. Shoe leather.

<long pause> In two thousand thirteen, archaeologists confirmed what the old reports said. They found the bones of a fourteen-year-old English girl, with cut marks. Some colonists ate the bodies of the dead.

<short pause> Of around five hundred people, only about sixty survive the winter.

## Part 2D

In the spring, the survivors give up. They board their ships and start sailing home.

<gasp> And at the mouth of the river, they meet a fleet coming in. A new governor, with fresh settlers and supplies.

They turn around. Jamestown lives.

## Part 2E

Now, the plant.

A colonist named John Rolfe plants a sweeter kind of tobacco, from seeds brought from the Caribbean. And it grows beautifully.

King James the First hates tobacco. He wrote a whole pamphlet against it. He called smoking "loathsome to the eye, hateful to the nose, harmful to the brain, dangerous to the lungs".

<laugh> He was right. And it didn't matter. England goes crazy for it.

Tobacco saves Virginia. <chuckle> Soon it's planted everywhere. Even in the streets of Jamestown. Tobacco becomes money. People pay taxes in it. Fines in it. Even a bride's passage from England is paid in tobacco.

## Part 2F

Pocahontas is captured by the English and held as a hostage. While she's held, she becomes a Christian, and takes the name Rebecca. In sixteen fourteen, she marries John Rolfe. <short pause> For a few years, there's peace.

The Virginia Company takes her to England, to show investors what a success their colony is. She meets the king. She's treated as a celebrity.

<sigh> And as she's about to sail home, she falls ill. She dies at Gravesend, at about twenty-one years old.

## Part 2G

Now, tobacco needs two things. Land. And lots and lots of workers.

So the company makes Virginia more attractive. Pay your own way over, and you get fifty acres. Pay for someone else to come, and you get their fifty acres too.

And in July sixteen nineteen, the company lets settlers elect their own representatives.

<short pause> The General Assembly meets in the church at Jamestown. The first elected lawmaking body in English America. It's the ancestor of every state legislature in the country.

<chuckle> And that December, settlers arriving at a plantation called Berkeley Hundred are ordered to give thanks every year on the day they landed. <short pause> Virginians like to point out that's two years before the Pilgrims' famous feast.

## Part 2H (quiet style)

And a few weeks later, in late August sixteen nineteen, a ship called the White Lion arrives at Point Comfort.

<breath> It carries more than twenty Africans, captured from a Portuguese slave ship. They are traded for food.

They are the first recorded Africans in English Virginia.

<long pause> Over the next decades, Virginia's laws turn slavery into something permanent. In sixteen sixty-two, a law says that if your mother is enslaved, you are enslaved. Forever. Passed from mother to child.

<short pause> Freedom for some. Slavery for others. Both arrive in the same summer. And both, in their own way, for tobacco.

## Part 2I (quiet style)

In sixteen twenty-two, as settlers keep taking land, Powhatan's brother, Opechancanough, leads a surprise attack on the English settlements.

<breath> More than a quarter of the colonists are killed in a single day.

The English take bloody revenge. For years. By the end of the century, the Powhatan nations have lost almost all of their land.

## Part 2J

And in sixteen seventy-six, poor settlers, both white and Black, servants and freed men, rise up together against the rich planters who run the colony. Led by a young planter, Nathaniel Bacon, they burn Jamestown to the ground.

<short pause> The rebellion collapses when Bacon dies of dysentery. But the planters learn a lesson. Poor whites and Black workers fighting together is dangerous to them.

<sigh> So they start passing laws to divide them. More rights for poor whites. Fewer and fewer for Black people. And they buy more enslaved Africans instead of servants.

## Part 3: Colony

By the seventeen hundreds, Virginia is the biggest and richest of the colonies. The capital moves to Williamsburg. Planters build grand brick houses on the rivers. Their tobacco ships load right at their own docks.

And they raise a generation of leaders.

<short pause> In sixteen ninety-three, Virginia founds the College of William and Mary. The second oldest college in America. Jefferson, Monroe, and a future chief justice, John Marshall, all study there.

## Part 3B

In seventeen seventy-five, in a church in Richmond, Patrick Henry stands up and says, "Give me liberty, or give me death!"

<short pause> Thomas Jefferson, from Virginia, writes the Declaration of Independence. "All men are created equal."

George Washington, from Virginia, leads the army.

And in seventeen eighty-one, at Yorktown, Virginia, Washington and the French trap the British army against the sea. <gasp> The British surrender. The Revolution is won. In Virginia.

<chuckle> According to legend, the British band plays a song called "The World Turned Upside Down".

## Part 3C

But in Virginia, the Revolution has another side.

In seventeen seventy-five, Virginia's royal governor, Lord Dunmore, makes an announcement. Any enslaved person who escapes from a rebel master and fights for the British... <short pause> will be free.

<gasp> Thousands run. Some of them fight in a unit with "Liberty to Slaves" stitched on their uniforms. For many enslaved Virginians, it's the British, not the Americans, who are offering freedom.

## Part 3D

And one enslaved Virginian helps win the war... for the Americans.

His name is James Armistead. With his enslaver's permission, he joins the army, and the French general Lafayette sends him on a dangerous mission. He pretends to be a runaway, and gets a job in the camp of the British general, Cornwallis. <short pause> The British even ask him to spy on the Americans.

<chuckle> He's a double agent. He feeds the British fake information, and sends Lafayette the real plans. Those plans help trap Cornwallis at Yorktown.

<sigh> After the war, he's sent back into slavery. It takes years, and a letter from Lafayette himself, for Virginia to free him. He takes a new name to honour his general. James Armistead Lafayette.

## Part 3E

James Madison, from Virginia, becomes the "Father of the Constitution", and writes the Bill of Rights.

Washington. Jefferson. Madison. Monroe. <short pause> Four of the first five presidents are Virginians.

And Jefferson writes the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom. Government, it says, can't force anyone to belong to any church. It's so important to him that he asks for it to be on his tombstone. <chuckle> Being president? Not on the tombstone.

## Part 3F

But here's the contradiction.

<breath> Washington and Jefferson both enslave hundreds of people. The men who wrote "liberty" live in mansions built and run by enslaved people.

<long pause> Jefferson writes that all men are created equal. At Monticello, he enslaves more than six hundred people over his lifetime. Including Sally Hemings, who, most historians now agree, was the mother of several of his children.

<short pause> Washington, at the end of his life, writes in his will that the people he enslaved should be freed after his wife's death. He's the only one of the slave-holding founding presidents to do it.

## Part 4: Slavery and War

By eighteen sixty, nearly half a million people in Virginia are enslaved. Virginia sells hundreds of thousands of them south, to the new cotton fields. <sigh> Richmond becomes one of the biggest slave markets in the country.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-one, an enslaved preacher named Nat Turner leads a revolt in Southampton County. About sixty white people are killed.

<long pause> Then many more Black people, most of them with nothing to do with the revolt, are killed in revenge. Virginia passes even harsher laws, banning enslaved people from learning to read.

<breath> Thirty years earlier, in eighteen hundred, an enslaved blacksmith named Gabriel had planned a huge revolt in Richmond. A storm washed out the roads on the night it was meant to start. Someone betrayed the plan. <long pause> Gabriel and more than twenty others were hanged.

## Part 4C

In eighteen sixty-one, Virginia leaves the Union. Richmond becomes the capital of the Confederacy.

But the mountain counties in the west don't want to go. <chuckle> So they leave Virginia. In eighteen sixty-three, they become a new state. West Virginia.

More Civil War battles are fought in Virginia than in any other state. Bull Run. Fredericksburg. Chancellorsville. The Wilderness. Petersburg.

<short pause> And in March eighteen sixty-two, at Hampton Roads, the war changes forever. A Confederate ship covered in iron plates, the Virginia, steams out and sinks wooden Union warships. Cannonballs bounce off her.

<gasp> The next morning, a strange little Union ironclad arrives. The Monitor. It looks like a tin can on a raft. For hours, the two iron ships pound each other at close range. <chuckle> Neither can sink the other.

It's the first battle ever between ironclad warships. Overnight, every wooden navy in the world is out of date.

## Part 4D

During the war, the Union army takes Robert E. Lee's own home at Arlington, overlooking Washington. And it starts burying soldiers on his land. Right up near the house. <short pause> So he can never go back.

That's how Arlington National Cemetery begins.

## Part 4E

And here's a strange one.

The first big battle of the war, Bull Run, is fought on a farm owned by a man named Wilmer McLean. A cannonball crashes through his kitchen fireplace.

<chuckle> So he moves his family far away from the war. To a quiet village in southern Virginia. Appomattox Court House.

In April eighteen sixty-five, General Lee needs a place to surrender to General Grant. <gasp> And the house they choose... is Wilmer McLean's.

<laugh> The war started in his yard. And ended in his parlour.

<short pause> A few days before that, in early April, the Confederate government flees Richmond, setting fire to warehouses as it goes. Much of the city burns.

<breath> The next day, Abraham Lincoln walks through the smoking streets of the Confederate capital. Freed people crowd around him, crying, singing, trying to touch him. One man kneels. <short pause> Lincoln tells him: don't kneel to me. Kneel only to God.

## Part 4F

A nine-year-old boy, born enslaved in the hills of Virginia, is freed at the end of the war. He works in salt furnaces and coal mines. He wants to go to school more than anything.

<short pause> So at sixteen, he walks most of five hundred miles, sleeping under wooden sidewalks, to a new school for Black students at Hampton, Virginia. He arrives with fifty cents. To get in, he's asked to sweep a room. He sweeps it three times, and dusts it four.

He gets in. His name is Booker T. Washington. He grows up to found the Tuskegee Institute, and becomes one of the most famous educators in America.

## Part 5: Jim Crow

After the war, for a few years, Black Virginians vote and serve in the legislature. Then Jim Crow laws separate Black and white people in schools, trains, theatres and drinking fountains.

## Part 5B

But Virginia also gives the fight for equality some of its heroes.

In nineteen fifty-one, sixteen-year-old Barbara Johns leads a walkout of her whole school in Farmville, protesting its leaky, overcrowded buildings. <gasp> Her case becomes part of Brown versus Board of Education.

When the Supreme Court orders schools to integrate in nineteen fifty-four, Virginia's leaders call for "Massive Resistance". <tsk> In Prince Edward County, they close all the public schools rather than integrate them. For five years.

## Part 5C

Richard and Mildred Loving, a white man and a Black woman, marry in Washington, D.C. When they come home to Virginia, police burst into their bedroom in the middle of the night, and arrest them.

<short pause> Their case goes to the Supreme Court. In nineteen sixty-seven, it rules that every state must allow people of different races to marry.

Loving versus Virginia.

And in nineteen ninety, Virginia elects Douglas Wilder. The first elected Black governor in American history. The grandson of enslaved people, governing from Richmond, the old capital of the Confederacy.

<short pause> And a boy from Richmond, Arthur Ashe, who wasn't allowed to play on the city's whites-only tennis courts, grows up to win the US Open, and Wimbledon. <short pause> Today, there's a statue of him in Richmond. On Monument Avenue, where statues of Confederate generals used to stand.

## Part 5D

In the nineteen forties, the army builds the biggest office building in the world, in Arlington. Five sides. Seventeen miles of corridors. <chuckle> Built during the war in just sixteen months. The Pentagon.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

On September eleventh, two thousand one, a hijacked plane crashes into the Pentagon. <long pause> One hundred and eighty-four people are killed.

## Part 6: Today

Today, Northern Virginia is full of tech companies and giant data centres. Much of the world's internet traffic passes through them. <short pause> The old tobacco state runs on cables now.

<chuckle> Virginia's tourism slogan, since nineteen sixty-nine, has been "Virginia is for Lovers". People drive the Skyline Drive along the Blue Ridge Mountains, and every July, on the island of Chincoteague, wild ponies are herded across a channel, swimming, while crowds cheer from the shore.

And at Jamestown, archaeologists are still digging up the old fort, finding armour, coins, and the stories of the people who lived and died there.

## Part 6B

So how does one colony give America freedom and slavery in the same summer?

<short pause> It starts with a plant the king of England hated.

Tobacco saved Jamestown. To grow it, Virginia needed people. To attract them, it gave land and a vote. To work the fields, it bought enslaved Africans. One plant. Two inheritances. And America has been arguing about both of them ever since.

<long pause> Virginia gave the country its first assembly, its most famous words about liberty, and the battlefields where slavery finally ended.

<chuckle> Not bad for a place that was nearly abandoned, because nobody wanted to farm.
