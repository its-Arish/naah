# The Entire History of Michigan

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,400 spoken words, so roughly 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And in two thousand thirteen"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, watery storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, then cheeky. |
| Part 3 | Busy and rugged, then principled. |
| Part 4 | Fast, mechanical, booming. |
| Part 5 | Musical, then heavy and serious. |
| Part 6 | Quiet, then hopeful, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Three Fires 10,000 BC to 1701 · French and British 1701 to 1796 · Wolverines 1796 to 1837 · Copper and Lumber 1837 to 1900 · Motor City 1900 to 1945 · Motown 1945 to 1970 · Decline 1970 to 2013 · Today 2013 to now.

## Hook

Corn Flakes? <short pause> Invented in Michigan, at a health spa, by a doctor who thought bland food made people behave better.

In Michigan, you're never more than about six miles from a lake or a stream.

<chuckle> Michigan once fought a war with Ohio, over the city of Toledo. Michigan lost... and got a whole extra peninsula as a consolation prize.

Detroit is the city that put the whole world on wheels.

<long pause> And in two thousand thirteen, Detroit became the biggest city in American history to go bankrupt.

<short pause> So how does the city that put the world on wheels end up broke?

<whispers> It starts with a farm boy who hated farming.

## Part 1: Three Fires

Michigan is two peninsulas surrounded by four of the five Great Lakes. The lower one is shaped like a mitten. <chuckle> People here point to their own hand to show you where they live.

It has more freshwater coastline than any other state. More than three thousand miles.

<short pause> Out in Lake Superior lies Isle Royale. Around a hundred years ago, moose swam or walked over the ice to it. Then, in a cold winter in the nineteen-forties, wolves crossed the ice too. <chuckle> Scientists have been watching the wolves and moose chase each other there ever since. It's the longest-running study of predators and prey in the world.

## Part 1B

Thousands of years ago, on the Keweenaw Peninsula, in the far north, people find something extraordinary lying in the ground. <short pause> Pure copper. Chunks of it, some huge.

They dig it out with stone hammers, heat it, and pound it into knives, spear points, fish hooks and bracelets. <gasp> It's some of the oldest metalworking in the Americas. More than five thousand years old.

## Part 1C

For thousands of years, Michigan is home to the Anishinaabe. Three nations, the Ojibwe, the Odawa and the Potawatomi, call themselves the Three Fires. <short pause> The Ojibwe are the older brother, the keepers of faith. The Odawa, the middle brother, the traders. The Potawatomi, the younger brother, the keepers of the fire.

They travel the lakes in birchbark canoes. They fish, gather wild rice, and tap maple trees for sugar every spring.

<short pause> Anishinaabe stories say their ancestors came from the far east, by the salt ocean, long ago. A sacred shell rose from the water and led them west, stop by stop, until they reached the place where food grows on the water. <short pause> Wild rice.

## Part 2: French and British

In the sixteen hundreds, French fur traders and priests arrive. They paddle the lakes, trading for beaver skins.

In sixteen sixty-eight, a French priest, Jacques Marquette, founds a mission at the rapids between Lake Superior and Lake Huron. Sault Sainte Marie. <short pause> It's the oldest city in Michigan.

In seventeen-oh-one, a French officer named Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac builds a fort on the narrow river between two lakes. The French word for "strait" is "détroit".

<short pause> Detroit.

<whispers> Remember his name. Cadillac.

## Part 2B

In seventeen sixty-three, after Britain takes over, an Odawa leader named Pontiac leads nations around the Great Lakes in a war against the British forts. He besieges Detroit for months.

<gasp> Up north, at Fort Michilimackinac, Ojibwe players start a game of lacrosse outside the walls. The British soldiers come out to watch. Then the ball flies over the wall... the players run in after it... and grab weapons their women had hidden under their blankets. <short pause> The fort falls in minutes.

<whispers> Remember his name too. Pontiac.

## Part 2C

Even after the American Revolution, the British keep Detroit until seventeen ninety-six.

In eighteen-oh-five, the whole wooden town of Detroit burns to the ground. <sigh> Only one building survives. A priest gives Detroit a motto, in Latin. We hope for better things. It will rise from the ashes. <short pause> It's still the city's motto.

Then, in the War of eighteen twelve, the American general in Detroit surrenders it without a fight. <tsk> The Americans have to win it back.

## Part 2D

In the eighteen-thirties, settlers pour in through the new Erie Canal. Michigan wants to be a state. But there's a problem. A small strip of land, with the port of Toledo, that both Michigan and Ohio claim.

In eighteen thirty-five, both sides send militias. The Toledo War.

<chuckle> The only casualty is a Michigan sheriff's deputy, stabbed with a penknife by an Ohio man. He survives.

Congress gives Toledo to Ohio. And to make up for it, Michigan gets the western Upper Peninsula. <laugh> Back then, people thought it was a frozen wasteland.

In January eighteen thirty-seven, Michigan becomes the twenty-sixth state.

## Part 3: Copper and Lumber

And then, the "worthless" Upper Peninsula turns out to be full of copper. <gasp> And iron. Lots of it.

In the eighteen-forties, America's first big mining rush hits the Keweenaw. Miners from Cornwall, Finland and Italy come to dig, in some of the deepest mines in the world. For decades, Michigan produces most of America's copper.

<chuckle> The Cornish miners bring their lunch: a pastry folded around meat, potato and turnip, that stays warm deep in the mine. The pasty. People in the Upper Peninsula, who call themselves Yoopers, still eat them everywhere.

In eighteen fifty-five, the Soo Locks open, so ships can carry the ore around the rapids between Lake Superior and the other lakes.

Michigan's forests are cut down too. For a while, Michigan produces more lumber than any other state.

<chuckle> Ohio got Toledo. Michigan got a mountain of metal.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

On Christmas Eve, nineteen thirteen, in the copper town of Calumet, striking miners' families gather for a children's party upstairs at the Italian Hall.

<breath> Someone shouts "Fire!" There is no fire. In the panic, people rush down the narrow stairway.

<long pause> Seventy-three people are crushed to death. Most of them children.

Nobody ever finds out who shouted.

## Part 3C

Michigan is a free state, right across the river from Canada.

<short pause> For people escaping slavery, Detroit is one of the last stops on the Underground Railroad. Its secret code name is "Midnight". On the other bank of the river is Canada, and freedom.

<short pause> And in July eighteen fifty-four, in the town of Jackson, thousands of anti-slavery voters gather in an oak grove, because no hall is big enough. They choose candidates for a brand new party. <chuckle> The Republican Party. Michigan and Wisconsin both claim to be its birthplace.

The great speaker Sojourner Truth, who escaped slavery in New York, spends the last decades of her life in Battle Creek, Michigan.

## Part 3D

In Battle Creek, a doctor named John Harvey Kellogg runs a famous health spa, the Sanitarium. He believes in plain, bland food, exercise and fresh air.

<chuckle> In the eighteen nineties, he and his brother Will accidentally leave some boiled wheat sitting out. They roll it flat anyway, and it comes out as flakes.

Later, they try corn. Will starts a company. Corn Flakes. One of the Sanitarium's patients, C. W. Post, starts his own cereal company across town. <short pause> Battle Creek becomes Cereal City.

<short pause> And in eighteen sixty-six, a Detroit pharmacist named James Vernor ages a ginger drink in oak barrels. Vernors. One of the oldest soft drinks in America.

## Part 4: Motor City

Now, back to the farm boy.

Henry Ford grows up on a farm near Detroit in the eighteen sixties and seventies. He hates farm work. <short pause> He loves machines. He takes apart watches and fixes them for his neighbours.

He becomes an engineer at Thomas Edison's electric company, and builds his first car in a shed in eighteen ninety-six. <chuckle> When it's finished, it's too wide to get out of the door. He knocks down part of the wall.

## Part 4B

He's not the only one. In Lansing, Ransom Olds builds a little car called the Curved Dash Oldsmobile, and becomes the first to make cars in big numbers.

In nineteen-oh-eight, Ford launches the Model T. Simple, tough, and cheap enough for ordinary families. <chuckle> You can buy it in any colour, he supposedly says, as long as it's black.

And in nineteen thirteen, he introduces the moving assembly line. Instead of workers walking around the car, the car moves past the workers. <gasp> Building a Model T goes from about twelve hours to about an hour and a half.

<short pause> Later, Ford builds the River Rouge plant, so big it has its own docks, steel mill and power plant. <gasp> Iron ore and coal go in one end. Finished cars come out the other.

## Part 4C

The next year, Ford does something shocking. He doubles his workers' pay, to five dollars a day.

<tsk> There's a catch. To get the full five dollars, Ford's inspectors visit your home, to check you're living the way Ford thinks you should. Clean. Sober. Saving money.

People rush to Detroit from everywhere. From Poland, Italy, Hungary, Lebanon, Mexico. And from the American South, especially Black families escaping Jim Crow.

Other car companies grow up around Ford. General Motors. Chrysler. And the new car brands borrow Detroit's history. Cadillac, named after the city's founder. <chuckle> Pontiac, named after the Odawa war leader.

Detroit grows from about three hundred thousand people in nineteen hundred to almost two million by nineteen fifty. The fifth biggest city in America.

<short pause> And in nineteen fifty-seven, after being threatened for years in Alabama, Rosa Parks moves to Detroit. She lives there for the rest of her life.

## Part 4D

In the nineteen-thirties, autoworkers fight for a union. At Flint, in nineteen thirty-six, they sit down inside the General Motors plants, and refuse to leave. For forty-four days. <short pause> GM gives in. The United Auto Workers becomes one of the most powerful unions in America.

And in the Second World War, the car factories switch to tanks, trucks and bombers. At Willow Run, Ford's giant plant builds a B-twenty-four bomber about once an hour.

<gasp> They call Detroit the "Arsenal of Democracy".

## Part 4E (quiet style)

But Detroit is crowded, and divided. In June nineteen forty-three, a fight on Belle Isle turns into three days of race riots.

<long pause> Thirty-four people are killed.

## Part 5: Motown

Detroit gives the world a champion. Joe Louis, the Brown Bomber, a Detroit autoworker's son, becomes heavyweight boxing champion of the world, and holds the title for almost twelve years.

<short pause> And in nineteen fifty-nine, a former car-factory worker named Berry Gordy borrows eight hundred dollars from his family and starts a record company in a small house in Detroit.

<chuckle> He runs it like an assembly line for hit songs. Writers. Singers. Choreographers. Even lessons in manners.

Motown. The Supremes, Stevie Wonder, Marvin Gaye, the Temptations, the Jackson Five.

 And from a Detroit church choir, Aretha Franklin, the Queen of Soul.

<short pause> Decades later, in Detroit's empty warehouses, young DJs mix funk and electronic beats into a brand new sound. Detroit techno. <chuckle> It takes over dance floors in Europe before most of America has heard of it.

## Part 5B

In nineteen fifty-seven, Michigan finally joins its two peninsulas. The Mackinac Bridge. <gasp> Five miles long. Before it, people waited hours, sometimes days, for car ferries.

<laugh> Michigan also has strange sports traditions. Since nineteen fifty-two, Detroit hockey fans have thrown octopuses onto the ice during the playoffs. Eight arms, for the eight wins it used to take to win the Stanley Cup.

## Part 5C

But cars do something else to Detroit.

Highways are built straight through the city, often through Black neighbourhoods. Families who can afford a car move out to new suburbs, and drive in to work. Factories move out too.

<short pause> Detroit is the city that built the cars. And the cars carried people away from it.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In July nineteen sixty-seven, after a police raid, Detroit erupts in five days of violence and fires.

<long pause> Forty-three people are killed. Thousands of buildings are damaged.

Even more people leave.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

In November nineteen seventy-five, in a violent storm on Lake Superior, a giant ore freighter, the Edmund Fitzgerald, vanishes from the radar.

<long pause> All twenty-nine men aboard are lost.

## Part 5F

In the nineteen seventies, oil prices jump. Small, cheap Japanese cars start to sell. Detroit's car companies lose ground. Factories close. Jobs disappear.

<sigh> The city shrinks. And shrinks.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-four, a congressman from Grand Rapids, Gerald Ford, becomes president, after Richard Nixon resigns. <chuckle> The only president never elected as either president or vice president.

## Part 5G (quiet style)

By two thousand thirteen, Detroit has lost more than half its people. Tens of thousands of houses stand empty. It owes around eighteen billion dollars.

<long pause> In July, it files for bankruptcy. The largest city bankruptcy in American history.

## Part 5H (quiet style)

A year later, in two thousand fourteen, to save money, the city of Flint switches its water supply. The new water corrodes old lead pipes.

<breath> Lead gets into the drinking water of a whole city, and its children.

## Part 6: Today

But Detroit doesn't disappear.

People worry that the city will have to sell the paintings in its great art museum to pay its debts. <short pause> Instead, foundations, the state and ordinary people raise hundreds of millions of dollars to save both the art, and the workers' pensions. People call it the Grand Bargain.

Detroit comes out of bankruptcy in about a year and a half. Downtown fills up again. Old factories become offices and lofts. And car companies race to build electric cars.

<chuckle> And a rapper from Detroit's Eight Mile Road, Eminem, becomes one of the best-selling artists in the world.

## Part 6B

So how does the city that put the world on wheels end up broke?

<short pause> It starts with a farm boy who hated farming.

Henry Ford wanted to free people from the farm. His cheap cars and five-dollar wages built Detroit into a giant. But Detroit put almost everything into one industry. And the cars it built let people leave. Out to the suburbs. Out of the city.

<long pause> The car built Detroit. And the car drove away from it.

<chuckle> Meanwhile, on Mackinac Island, in the middle of the lakes, cars have been banned since eighteen ninety-eight. <short pause> They still get around by horse and bicycle. And eat a lot of fudge.
