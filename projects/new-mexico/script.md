# The Entire History of New Mexico

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And on July sixteenth"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, ancient, awed storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Serious, then fierce and proud. |
| Part 3 | Proud and determined, then dusty Wild West, wry. |
| Part 4 | Bright and arty, then brave. |
| Part 5 | Secretive and tense, then heavy. |
| Part 6 | Quirky and bright; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 11,000 BC to 1540 · New Spain 1540 to 1680 · Pueblo Revolt 1680 to 1692 · Mexico 1821 to 1846 · Territory 1846 to 1912 · Statehood 1912 to 1943 · Secret City 1943 to 1945 · Today 1945 to now.

## Hook

Microsoft? <short pause> Started in Albuquerque. Roswell, the UFO town? New Mexico.

<chuckle> Some Americans think New Mexico is a foreign country. People from New Mexico have actually been asked for their passports.

Its capital, Santa Fe, was a capital ten years before the Pilgrims landed.

<long pause> And on July sixteenth, nineteen forty-five, in the New Mexico desert, the world's first atomic bomb exploded. The families living nearby were never warned.

<short pause> So how does the oldest capital in America end up building the newest, most terrifying weapon on Earth?

<whispers> It starts with a boys' school on a lonely mountain mesa.

## Part 1: First Peoples

New Mexico is high, dry and huge. Mountains, mesas, lava fields, and the Rio Grande cutting down the middle.

<gasp> In the south, there are dunes of pure white gypsum, so bright they look like snow. White Sands. And underground, a cave so big that a teenage cowboy who stumbled on it in eighteen ninety-eight, by following a cloud of bats, spent years trying to get people to believe him. Carlsbad Caverns.

## Part 1B

In nineteen-oh-eight, a flood washes out a gully near the town of Folsom. A Black cowboy and ranch foreman named George McJunkin spots huge bones sticking out of the bank. <short pause> He knows they're too big to be cattle.

Years later, scientists dig there, and find a stone spear point stuck between the ribs of a giant extinct bison.

<gasp> It proves something nobody believed. People were in America during the Ice Age.

And thirteen thousand years ago, near a town now called Clovis, hunters chase mammoths with beautifully made stone spear points. Archaeologists name a whole ancient culture after the town. Clovis.

## Part 1C

In the southwest, in the Mimbres Valley, a thousand years ago, potters paint bowls with black-and-white designs. Fish, rabbits, birds, dancers, even scenes of daily life. <chuckle> Some are playful, even funny.

<breath> When someone dies, a bowl is often placed over their face, with a small hole punched through the bottom. Some believe the hole lets the spirit go free.

## Part 1D

A thousand years ago, in a remote canyon, the Ancestral Puebloans build something astonishing. Chaco Canyon.

Giant stone "great houses", some with hundreds of rooms, several storeys tall. <short pause> Dead-straight roads, up to thirty feet wide, running for miles across the desert, even straight up cliffs, with steps carved into the rock. Buildings lined up with the sun and the moon.

Chaco is the centre of a world. Traders bring in macaws from Mexico, and shells from the ocean. <sigh> And then, by around eleven fifty, people move away.

Their descendants are the Pueblo peoples. Today there are nineteen Pueblos in New Mexico. Taos Pueblo, with its many-storey adobe houses, has been lived in for about a thousand years.

The Apache and the Navajo, and later the Comanche, also make this land their home.

## Part 2: New Spain

In fifteen thirty-nine, a man named Esteban, an enslaved African from Morocco, walks north from Mexico ahead of a Spanish expedition. <short pause> He's probably the first person from outside the Americas to reach New Mexico. At the Zuni Pueblo, he's killed.

The next year, Coronado marches through, with hundreds of soldiers, looking for the Seven Cities of Gold. <tsk> He finds villages of stone and adobe. No gold.

In fifteen ninety-eight, Juan de Oñate leads settlers north from Mexico, along a road that becomes the Camino Real. The Royal Road. Sixteen hundred miles long.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

Acoma Pueblo sits on top of a mesa almost four hundred feet high. People call it Sky City.

<breath> When the people of Acoma resist, Oñate's soldiers storm the mesa. Hundreds are killed.

<long pause> And Oñate orders that men from Acoma have one foot cut off. Even Spain later punishes him for his cruelty.

## Part 2C

In sixteen ten, the Spanish build a new capital. Santa Fe. The "Holy Faith".

Its Palace of the Governors still stands. The oldest continuously used public building in the United States.

<chuckle> Ten years later, the Pilgrims are only just stepping off the Mayflower.

But New Mexico is very, very far from everything. <sigh> Caravans from Mexico City take six months to arrive, and come only every few years. It's the edge of the Spanish empire.

<short pause> The caravans bring something that will define New Mexico forever. Chile peppers, from Mexico. <chuckle> Four hundred years later, New Mexicans still argue about which is better, red or green.

## Part 3: Pueblo Revolt

The Spanish force the Pueblo peoples to work for them, pay tribute in corn and cloth, and give up their religion. Priests burn sacred masks and raid underground ceremonial rooms. <tsk> Religious leaders are whipped in public.

In sixteen eighty, a Tewa religious leader named Popé secretly organises dozens of Pueblos, speaking different languages, spread across hundreds of miles.

<whispers> Runners carry knotted cords from village to village. Each day, untie one knot. When the last knot is untied... rise up.

The Spanish catch two of the runners. So the Pueblos rise a day early. <gasp> In August sixteen eighty, they rise. All at once.

The Spanish are driven out of New Mexico completely. For twelve years.

It's one of the most successful Native uprisings against European colonisers in history.

<short pause> The Spanish come back in sixteen ninety-two. But after that, they're more careful. Pueblo languages and ceremonies survive to this day.

<short pause> In the centuries after, New Mexico's Spanish villagers and Pueblo neighbours often fight side by side, against Comanche and Apache raids. <tsk> The frontier is so dangerous that villages are built like little forts, with houses in a square, and only one way in.

## Part 3B

In eighteen twenty-one, Mexico wins independence. The Santa Fe Trail opens, bringing American traders, and wagons full of cloth and tools, nine hundred miles from Missouri.

<chuckle> Mountain men come too. The most famous, Kit Carson, makes his home in Taos.

And in eighteen forty-six, during the war with Mexico, an American army marches into Santa Fe. They don't even fire a shot. New Mexico becomes American territory.

## Part 3C

<gasp> And in eighteen sixty-two, the Civil War arrives in the mountains. A Confederate army from Texas marches up the Rio Grande, to take New Mexico, then the gold fields of Colorado, and maybe even California.

At Glorieta Pass, near Santa Fe, the two armies collide. The Confederates think they're winning. <short pause> But a small Union force sneaks around behind them, over a mountain, and finds their supply wagons. They burn all eighty of them.

<groan> With no food and no ammunition, the Confederates have to walk all the way back to Texas. People call it the Gettysburg of the West.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-four, the U.S. Army, with Kit Carson leading the campaign, destroys Navajo crops, orchards and sheep, and forces the Navajo to surrender.

<breath> More than eight thousand Navajo are marched hundreds of miles, to a bleak camp called Bosque Redondo. They call it the Long Walk.

<long pause> Thousands die, on the walk and in the camp. In eighteen sixty-eight, a treaty finally lets them go home.

## Part 4: Territory

Then New Mexico waits. And waits.

For more than sixty years, Congress refuses to make New Mexico a state. <tsk> Some say it's too wild. Many in Congress don't trust a place where most people speak Spanish and are Catholic.

Meanwhile, the Wild West happens. Cattle wars. Outlaws. <short pause> A young gunman called Billy the Kid fights in the Lincoln County War. He escapes from jail, and is shot dead in eighteen eighty-one, at about twenty-one years old.

<chuckle> Legend says he killed twenty-one men, one for every year of his life. Historians think it was closer to eight or nine.

<chuckle> In eighteen eighty, the railroad arrives. And with it, the Harvey Girls, young women who come west to work as waitresses in railway restaurants. Thousands of them stay, marry, and settle the West.

Finally, in January nineteen twelve, New Mexico becomes the forty-seventh state.

## Part 4B

Four years later, the Mexican revolutionary Pancho Villa raids the little border town of Columbus, New Mexico. <gasp> It's often called the last time foreign fighters invaded the mainland United States. The U.S. Army chases him into Mexico for months. They never catch him.

## Part 4C

In the nineteen-twenties and thirties, artists fall in love with New Mexico's light. Taos and Santa Fe become art towns. <short pause> The painter Georgia O'Keeffe moves to the desert and paints its sun-bleached bones and red hills.

And in Roswell, a scientist named Robert Goddard launches liquid-fuelled rockets into the sky. Years before the space age. <chuckle> The neighbours think he's mad.

## Part 4D

In the Second World War, Navajo Marines create a secret code, based on their own language. <short pause> A tank is a "turtle". A submarine is an "iron fish". The enemy never breaks it.

The Navajo Code Talkers.

## Part 4E (quiet style)

Many New Mexicans serve in a unit sent to the Philippines. When the islands fall to Japan in nineteen forty-two, they are forced on the Bataan Death March.

<long pause> Of about eighteen hundred New Mexicans in the unit, around half never come home.

## Part 5: Secret City

Now, the school.

In the nineteen-twenties, a young man from New York with weak health is sent to New Mexico to get stronger. <short pause> He falls in love with the mountains. He rides horses across them for days.

His name is J. Robert Oppenheimer.

High on a mesa in the Jemez Mountains, there's a small, isolated boys' boarding school. The Los Alamos Ranch School. <chuckle> Boys ride horses, wear shorts all year, and sleep on open porches, even in winter.

## Part 5B

In nineteen forty-two, the United States needs a place to design an atomic bomb. Somewhere far from everyone. Hidden. Hard to reach.

Oppenheimer, now a famous physicist, knows exactly the place.

The army buys the ranch school. <whispers> And scientists from all over the world arrive in secret. Their address is just a post office box in Santa Fe. <chuckle> Their children's birth certificates say they were born at "Box sixteen sixty-three".

Los Alamos.

## Part 5C

Famous scientists arrive under fake names. <chuckle> The Italian physicist Enrico Fermi becomes "Henry Farmer". Guards are told not to ask questions.

The town has muddy streets, wooden boardwalks, and never enough water. <laugh> A young physicist named Richard Feynman gets so bored that he teaches himself to crack the safes holding the bomb's secret papers. He leaves little notes inside, to scare the security officers.

<short pause> Meanwhile, a few of the scientists really are spies, quietly passing secrets to the Soviet Union.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

At five-thirty in the morning, on July sixteenth, nineteen forty-five, in a desert the Spanish called the Jornada del Muerto, the Journey of the Dead, they test the first atomic bomb. Trinity.

<long pause> The flash lights up the mountains. The heat is felt miles away. A blind woman more than a hundred miles away is said to have seen the light.

<breath> Radioactive dust drifts over ranches and villages in the Tularosa Basin. Families who were never told collect rainwater from their roofs, and drink it. For decades, many have said the government ignored them.

In twenty twenty-five, eighty years after the test, Congress finally makes New Mexico's downwinders eligible for federal compensation.

Three weeks later, atomic bombs destroy Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

## Part 6: Today

In July nineteen forty-seven, near Roswell, a rancher finds strange debris. The army announces it has recovered a "flying disc". <chuckle> Then says it was a weather balloon. Decades later, the Air Force says it was a secret balloon project, to listen for Soviet nuclear tests.

<laugh> Roswell has been the UFO capital of the world ever since.

In nineteen fifty, firefighters rescue a burned bear cub, clinging to a tree, from a forest fire in New Mexico. He becomes the living Smokey Bear.

And in nineteen seventy-five, two young men named Bill Gates and Paul Allen start a little software company in Albuquerque. Microsoft.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

Uranium for America's bombs is mined across northwestern New Mexico, much of it on Navajo land. <breath> Many miners are never told how dangerous it is.

In July nineteen seventy-nine, at Church Rock, a dam holding back radioactive mine waste breaks. Millions of gallons pour into a river that Navajo families use for their sheep.

<long pause> It is the biggest release of radioactive material in American history. And most Americans have never heard of it.

## Part 6C

Today, New Mexico has the biggest hot-air balloon festival in the world. <gasp> Hundreds of balloons rise over Albuquerque at dawn. A giant field of radio telescopes, the Very Large Array, listening to the universe. And a spaceport.

<gasp> In twenty twenty-one, a rocket plane from Spaceport America carries a full crew, including the company's founder, to the edge of space.

<chuckle> Out in the desert, the town of Truth or Consequences named itself after a radio quiz show, in nineteen-fifty, to win a free broadcast.

<chuckle> It has an official state question. "Red or green?" Meaning: which chile do you want on that?

And a magazine column called "One of Our Fifty Is Missing", full of stories of people who forgot it's part of America.

## Part 6D

So how does the oldest capital in America end up building the newest, most terrifying weapon on Earth?

<whispers> It starts with a boys' school on a lonely mountain mesa.

For four hundred years, New Mexico was the edge of everything. Too far, too high, too empty, for empires to bother with. <short pause> That isolation protected the Pueblos, and kept old Santa Fe old.

And when a young scientist who had fallen in love with those lonely mountains needed a place no one would find... he remembered the school on the mesa.

<breath> New Mexico was always the place that was hard to find.

<chuckle> Some people are still looking for it.
