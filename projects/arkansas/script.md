# The Entire History of Arkansas

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,200 spoken words, so roughly 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen fifty-seven"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm river-and-mountain storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Rough frontier, a little cheeky; steamy and showy at Hot Springs. |
| Part 3 | Heavy and honest, with a wry moment. |
| Part 4 | Treasure-hunt excitement, then hard times. |
| Part 5 | Brave, serious and steady. |
| Part 6 | Folksy small-town warmth growing into big ambition; smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1686 · Arkansas Post 1686 to 1836 · Statehood 1836 to 1865 · Sharecroppers 1865 to 1930 · Hard Times 1930 to 1957 · Little Rock 1957 to 1958 · Small Towns, Big Stores 1950 to now.

## Hook

Walmart? <short pause> Started in Arkansas.

<chuckle> In Arkansas, there's a diamond mine where anyone can dig. And whatever you find, you keep.

Arkansas even has a law about how to pronounce its own name.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-seven, nine Black teenagers tried to go to high school in Little Rock. The governor sent soldiers to keep them out. The president sent soldiers to get them in.

<short pause> So how does one of the poorest, most rural states in America give birth to the biggest company in the world?

<whispers> It starts with a little dime store on a town square.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Arkansas has two faces. In the northwest, the green, rugged Ozark and Ouachita mountains, full of rivers, caves and waterfalls. In the east, the flat, rich Mississippi Delta.

<short pause> A thousand years ago, near today's Little Rock, people build a ceremonial centre of tall earth mounds, around a plaza, lined up with the sun. Toltec Mounds.

<short pause> In the Ozarks, people live for thousands of years under overhanging cliffs. The dry rock shelters keep things perfectly. <gasp> Archaeologists have found baskets, sandals, woven bags and even ancient seeds, thousands of years old, still in the dust.

The Caddo build mound towns and make beautiful pottery, with swirling designs. The Osage hunt in the Ozarks. And along the rivers live the Quapaw.

<chuckle> Other nations call the Quapaw a name that sounds like "Akansa". French explorers write it down. Badly. And that's where "Arkansas" comes from.

## Part 1B

In fifteen forty-one, Hernando de Soto marches through, with hundreds of soldiers and a herd of pigs, looking for gold. <tsk> He doesn't find any. He spends a whole winter in Arkansas, then dies near the Mississippi the next year.

In sixteen seventy-three, two French explorers, Marquette and Jolliet, paddle down the Mississippi as far as the mouth of the Arkansas River. <short pause> The Quapaw tell them the river flows all the way to the sea, through Spanish lands. <gasp> Afraid of being captured, the Frenchmen turn around and paddle home.

In sixteen eighty-six, the French build a little trading post near where the Arkansas River meets the Mississippi. Arkansas Post. The first European settlement in the lower Mississippi Valley.

<chuckle> In seventeen eighty-three, Arkansas Post, now Spanish, is even attacked by British loyalists, in one of the last fights of the American Revolution.

## Part 2: Arkansas Post

After the Louisiana Purchase, Arkansas becomes American. Settlers come. Hunters, trappers, and farmers carving homesteads out of the forest.

<short pause> In the little town of Washington, Arkansas, a blacksmith named James Black is said to forge a big, sharp hunting knife for the famous frontiersman Jim Bowie.

<chuckle> The Bowie knife. Some people call it the "Arkansas toothpick".

## Part 2B

In seventeen twenty-two, a French explorer paddling up the Arkansas River notices two rocky outcrops on opposite banks. A big one, and a little one. <chuckle> He calls the small one "La Petite Roche". The little rock.

A century later, a town grows up beside it. Little Rock. In eighteen twenty-one, it becomes the capital of Arkansas Territory.

<laugh> Arkansas also gets a famous joke. A fiddle tune called "The Arkansas Traveler". In the story, a lost traveller asks a backwoods squatter for directions, food and a place to sleep. The squatter answers every question with a wisecrack, and keeps playing the same half of a tune on his fiddle. <short pause> Until the traveller takes the fiddle, and plays the other half. Then he's welcome to everything.

## Part 2C

In eighteen thirty-two, Congress protects something special. In the Ouachita Mountains, hot water bubbles out of the ground at about a hundred and forty degrees. People have soaked in it for centuries.

<gasp> Congress sets the springs aside as a federal reservation. Forty years before Yellowstone becomes the first national park.

Hot Springs becomes "the American Spa". Grand bathhouses line the street, with marble, stained glass and steam rooms. <chuckle> And later, famous gangsters like Al Capone come to soak, gamble and hide out.

Baseball teams come too. Some of the very first spring training camps happen in Hot Springs. <laugh> In nineteen eighteen, a young player named Babe Ruth hits a home run so far that it reportedly lands in a pond... at the town's alligator farm.

## Part 2D

In eighteen thirty-six, Arkansas becomes the twenty-fifth state. A slave state. Cotton plantations spread across the Delta.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

Through Arkansas pass the Cherokee, the Choctaw, the Creek and other nations on their forced journey west, on the Trail of Tears.

<breath> Many die along the way, of cold and disease.

## Part 2F

On the border with Indian Territory sits Fort Smith. <short pause> From there, a federal judge named Isaac Parker sends marshals out across the territory, to bring in outlaws.

<tsk> He sentences so many men to death that he becomes known as the Hanging Judge. More than seventy of them are hanged on his gallows.

## Part 3: Sharecroppers

In eighteen sixty-one, Arkansas joins the Confederacy. <short pause> In March eighteen sixty-two, at Pea Ridge, in the Ozarks, a Union army beats a bigger Confederate force. Missouri stays in Union hands. And the next year, Union troops take Little Rock.

<sigh> After the war, enslaved people are freed. But most Black families in the Delta, and many white families too, end up sharecropping. Farming someone else's land for a share of the crop. Usually ending each year in debt.

## Part 3B

And in eighteen eighty-one, Arkansas has an argument. How do you say the state's name?

<chuckle> Its two U.S. senators even say it differently. One says "Ar-KAN-sas". The other says "AR-kan-saw".

<laugh> So the legislature passes a resolution. It's "AR-kan-saw". It's still the official pronunciation today.

## Part 3C

<chuckle> Just a few years before that, in eighteen seventy-four, Arkansas briefly has two governors. Both claim to have won the election.

<gasp> Each one gathers an armed militia. They take over different parts of Little Rock. One side drags out a giant old cannon, and names it "Lady Baxter". <groan> There are skirmishes, and people are killed. The Brooks-Baxter War.

<tsk> In the end, the President of the United States has to decide which governor is real.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

In nineteen-nineteen, Black sharecroppers near Elaine, Arkansas, meet in a church to form a union, and demand fair prices for their cotton.

<breath> Shots are fired outside the church. Then white mobs and soldiers sweep the area.

<long pause> Over the next days, more than a hundred Black people are killed. Some historians believe the number is in the hundreds. It's one of the deadliest racial massacres in American history.

<breath> Twelve Black men are sentenced to death after trials that last just minutes. In nineteen twenty-three, the Supreme Court throws out their convictions. A landmark ruling for fair trials.

## Part 3E

<short pause> In nineteen thirty-four, Black and white sharecroppers in eastern Arkansas do something almost unheard of in the South. They form a union together. The Southern Tenant Farmers' Union. <tsk> Its members are beaten and evicted. But they make the whole country notice how poor the Delta's farmers are.

## Part 4: Hard Times

In nineteen-oh-six, a farmer named John Huddleston finds two strange, shiny crystals on his land near Murfreesboro.

<gasp> Diamonds. Sitting in the eroded crater of an ancient volcano.

He sells the land, and goes around telling people he's the Diamond King. <chuckle> In nineteen twenty-four, a forty-carat diamond is found there. Uncle Sam. The biggest diamond ever found in the United States.

## Part 4B

Today, it's a state park. Crater of Diamonds. The only place in the world where the public can dig for diamonds in their original volcanic source.

<laugh> You pay a small fee, dig in the dirt, and keep anything you find. Visitors find hundreds of diamonds every year.

 <short pause> Some of them are found by children, just looking at the ground after a rain.

## Part 4C

But Arkansas stays poor. In nineteen twenty-seven, the Great Mississippi Flood covers much of the Delta. Then the Great Depression and drought hit.

<sigh> In some places, the flood water stays for months. Farmers camp on top of the levees with their mules. In nineteen thirty-one, hungry farmers in the town of England march into town and demand food for their families.

<short pause> In nineteen thirty-two, Arkansas sends Hattie Caraway to Washington. The first woman ever elected to the U.S. Senate.

In the nineteen-thirties, the government builds a farming colony for poor families at Dyess, Arkansas. One of those families has a little boy named J.R. Cash.

<chuckle> He grows up picking cotton and singing. He becomes Johnny Cash. The Man in Black.

<short pause> In the Delta river town of Helena, a radio station starts a daily show in nineteen forty-one, sponsored by a flour company. King Biscuit Time. <chuckle> Live blues, at lunchtime, every day. It's still on the air, one of the longest-running daily radio shows in America.

<short pause> And a girl sent to live with her grandmother in the little town of Stamps, Arkansas, grows up to write "I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings". Maya Angelou.

## Part 5: Little Rock

In nineteen fifty-four, the Supreme Court rules that segregated schools are unconstitutional.

In September nineteen fifty-seven, nine Black students try to enter Little Rock's Central High School.

<sigh> Governor Orval Faubus orders the Arkansas National Guard to block them.

<short pause> Guiding the nine is Daisy Bates, who runs a Black newspaper in Little Rock with her husband. <sigh> Rocks are thrown through her windows. Crosses are burned on her lawn. She keeps going.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

One of the nine, fifteen-year-old Elizabeth Eckford, doesn't get the message to meet the others. She arrives alone.

<breath> She walks along the line of soldiers, looking for a way in. They turn her away. A white crowd follows her, screaming.

 <long pause> She sits on a bench at a bus stop, trying not to cry.

The photographs go around the world.

## Part 5C

So President Eisenhower sends in the U.S. Army. <gasp> The famous hundred-and-first Airborne Division. Soldiers escort the nine students into the school, and walk them to class every day.

<sigh> Inside, they're kicked, spat on and threatened all year. They keep going.

In May nineteen fifty-eight, Ernest Green becomes the first Black student to graduate from Central High. <short pause> Martin Luther King Junior sits in the crowd.

<tsk> The next year, rather than integrate, Little Rock closes all its public high schools for a whole year. People call it the Lost Year.

They're called the Little Rock Nine. They show America that the law will be enforced.

## Part 6: Small Towns, Big Stores

Now, the dime store.

In nineteen fifty, a young man named Sam Walton buys a small variety store on the town square of Bentonville, a little town in northwest Arkansas. Fewer than three thousand people.

<tsk> Big chain stores ignore towns like Bentonville. Too small. Too far away. Not worth it.

Sam Walton thinks the opposite. Small towns are everywhere. And people there want low prices too.

## Part 6B

In nineteen sixty-two, in nearby Rogers, he opens a bigger discount store. Walmart.

<chuckle> He drives an old pickup truck, with his bird dogs in the back. He walks into rival stores with a notepad, writing down their prices and ideas.

He builds his stores in small towns, close together, and supplies them from giant warehouses with his own trucks. He cuts prices. And keeps cutting them.

<chuckle> By the nineteen-eighties, a magazine names Sam Walton the richest man in America. He still drives his old pickup.

<gasp> Small town by small town, Walmart spreads across America. Then the world.

Today, Walmart is the biggest company on Earth by revenue. And its headquarters is still in Bentonville.

## Part 6C

And Walmart isn't alone. In the same corner of Arkansas, Tyson Foods becomes one of the biggest meat companies in the world. And J.B. Hunt becomes one of the biggest trucking companies.

<short pause> And a boy born in a little town called Hope, Arkansas, Bill Clinton, becomes governor, and then president of the United States in nineteen ninety-three.

<short pause> He grows up in Hot Springs, a town that still has illegal casinos running openly in the nineteen-sixties, until a new governor finally shuts them down.

<chuckle> Arkansas also grows about half of all the rice in America. And in two thousand eleven, Sam Walton's daughter opens a giant art museum in the woods of Bentonville. Crystal Bridges.

<laugh> And at University of Arkansas football games, seventy thousand fans put their arms in the air and call the pigs. "Woo, pig, sooie!" The team is the Razorbacks. Named after wild hogs.

## Part 6D

So how does one of the poorest, most rural states in America give birth to the biggest company in the world?

<whispers> It starts with a little dime store on a town square.

Arkansas was full of small towns everyone else ignored. Sam Walton grew up in towns like that, and opened his store in one. <short pause> He knew those customers. And he built an empire by serving them.

<breath> Arkansas was too small and too rural for the big companies. So it built its own.

<chuckle> And if you ever find yourself in Murfreesboro... bring a shovel.
