## Hook

There are about seven pigs for every person in Iowa.

The first electronic digital computer was built in Iowa.

The Field of Dreams? A real cornfield in Iowa.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-nine, a small plane crashed in an Iowa cornfield, killing Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens and the Big Bopper. The day the music died.

And every four years, people who want to be president flood into Iowa first. Shaking hands. Eating pork chops on a stick.

<short pause> So how does a quiet farm state become the first stop on the road to the White House?

<whispers> It starts with a slow copy machine.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Iowa sits between two great rivers. The Mississippi on the east. The Missouri on the west. And in between, an ocean of tall grass, growing on some of the deepest, richest black soil in the world.

A thousand years ago, people along the Mississippi build effigy mounds, in the shapes of bears and birds.

Later, the land is home to the Ioway, who give the state its name, and the Sauk, the Meskwaki, the Otoe and others.

## Part 1B

In eighteen thirty-two, after the Black Hawk War, the Sauk and Meskwaki are forced to give up land in eastern Iowa. Settlers rush in.

But the Meskwaki do something remarkable. In eighteen fifty-seven, they pool their money and buy back eighty acres of their own land. Legally. From the state.

<short pause> It grows into the Meskwaki Settlement. Not a reservation given to them. Land they bought.

## Part 2: Free State

In eighteen forty-six, Iowa becomes the twenty-ninth state. A free state.

And it does something surprising. In eighteen sixty-eight, a twelve-year-old Black girl named Susan Clark is turned away from her local school in Muscatine.

Her father sues. And the Iowa Supreme Court rules that schools cannot separate children by race.

That's eighty-six years before the U.S. Supreme Court says the same thing in Brown versus Board of Education.

## Part 2B

The next year, in eighteen sixty-nine, Arabella Mansfield passes the Iowa bar exam. She becomes the first woman licensed to practise law in the United States.

## Part 3: Corn and Hogs

Settlers break up the prairie with steel ploughs. The deep black soil grows corn like almost nowhere else on Earth.

And what do you do with all that corn? Feed it to pigs. Iowa becomes the biggest hog state in America.

## Part 3B

In the nineteen-twenties, a young Iowa farm writer named Henry A. Wallace starts breeding a new kind of corn. Hybrid corn. It grows much more per acre.

He starts a seed company in Des Moines. Farmers buy it. Corn harvests soar. Wallace later becomes Vice President of the United States.

## Part 3C

And a boy from West Branch, Iowa, Herbert Hoover, becomes president in nineteen twenty-nine. The first president born west of the Mississippi.

In nineteen thirty, an Iowa painter named Grant Wood paints a farmer with a pitchfork and a woman standing in front of a little house. American Gothic. It becomes one of the most famous paintings in America.

## Part 4: Inventors

In the late nineteen-thirties, at Iowa State College, a physics professor named John Atanasoff is frustrated. His calculations take forever.

One night, he drives two hundred miles across the border into Illinois, sits in a roadside bar, and figures out how to build a machine that does maths with electricity.

With a student, Clifford Berry, he builds it. The Atanasoff-Berry Computer. In nineteen seventy-three, a federal court rules it was the first electronic digital computer.

## Part 4B

In nineteen fifty-nine, the leader of the Soviet Union, Nikita Khrushchev, visits America. And one of the places he insists on visiting is an Iowa corn farm.

<chuckle> In the middle of the Cold War, the farmer, Roswell Garst, ends up throwing corn cobs at the reporters crowding around them.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

On February third, nineteen fifty-nine, after a concert in Clear Lake, Iowa, a small plane takes off in a snowstorm. On board are rock and roll stars Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens, and J.P. Richardson, the Big Bopper.

It crashes in a cornfield minutes later. Everyone on board is killed.

Years later, a song calls it "the day the music died".

## Part 5: First in the Nation

Now, the copy machine.

In nineteen sixty-eight, the Democratic Party has a disastrous convention in Chicago. Afterwards, it changes its rules. Ordinary voters must have more say. And local meetings have to be announced well in advance.

Iowa picks its delegates in steps. First neighbourhood meetings, called caucuses. Then county conventions. Then district conventions. Then the state convention.

And between each step, Iowa's Democrats need time. About a month. To print and mail out all the paperwork.

They have one old, slow mimeograph machine.

## Part 5B

So they count backwards from the state convention in May. Month. Month. Month.

And the first caucuses land on January twenty-fourth, nineteen seventy-two.

<chuckle> Earlier than any other state. By accident.

Almost nobody notices. But one candidate, George McGovern, does surprisingly well. Reporters notice. In nineteen seventy-six, the Republicans move their caucuses to the same night.

That year, an almost unknown peanut farmer from Georgia, Jimmy Carter, spends months in Iowa, visiting living rooms and diners. He does well in Iowa. And it helps carry him all the way to the White House.

From then on, every candidate wants to win Iowa.

## Part 5C

For fifty years, presidential hopefuls tour all ninety-nine counties, eat at the Iowa State Fair, and try a pork chop on a stick. They admire a life-size cow sculpted from butter.

The system has its critics. People say Iowa is too small, too rural, and doesn't look like the rest of America. After a messy count in twenty twenty, the Democrats moved their first contest to South Carolina. But the Republicans still start in Iowa.

## Part 5D

So how does a quiet farm state become the first stop on the road to the White House?

It starts with a slow copy machine.

New rules needed a month between every step. Iowa had four steps, and one slow mimeograph. Counting back put Iowa's caucuses in January, ahead of everyone. And once candidates and reporters showed up, Iowa never wanted to give up its spot.

<short pause> A machine that couldn't print fast enough made a small farm state one of the most important places in American politics.

<chuckle> And it made the pork chop on a stick a presidential tradition.
