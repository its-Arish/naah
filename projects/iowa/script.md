# The Entire History of Iowa

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen fifty-nine"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm prairie storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Proud and principled, quietly surprised. |
| Part 3 | Down-to-earth farm voice, cheerful and inventive. |
| Part 4 | Clever and curious, then sad. |
| Part 5 | Wry political storyteller, enjoying the irony; smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1832 · Settlement 1832 to 1846 · Free State 1846 to 1870 · Corn and Hogs 1870 to 1930 · Inventors 1930 to 1960 · First in the Nation 1972 to now.

## Hook

There are about seven pigs for every person in Iowa.

<chuckle> The first electronic digital computer was built in Iowa. The Field of Dreams? A real cornfield in Iowa.

<long pause> In nineteen fifty-nine, a small plane crashed in an Iowa cornfield, killing Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens and the Big Bopper. The day the music died.

And for fifty years, people who wanted to be president flooded into Iowa first. Shaking hands. Eating pork chops on a stick.

<short pause> So how does a quiet farm state become the first stop on the road to the White House?

<whispers> It starts with a slow copy machine.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Iowa sits between two great rivers. The Mississippi on the east. The Missouri on the west. <short pause> And in between, an ocean of tall grass, growing on some of the deepest, richest black soil in the world.

<sigh> Today, almost all of that prairie is gone. Ploughed into farms. Less than one acre in a thousand is left.

<gasp> On the western edge, wind-blown dust from the Ice Age has piled up into steep, rippled hills. The Loess Hills. Hills like this, this tall, are found in only one other place on Earth. China.

## Part 1B

A thousand years ago, people along the Mississippi build effigy mounds, in the shapes of bears and birds. <short pause> On one bluff, there's a line of ten bears, and three birds, marching along the ridge. The Marching Bear Group.

Later, the land is home to the Ioway, who give the state its name, and the Sauk, the Meskwaki, the Otoe, the Omaha, and others.

## Part 1C (quiet style)

In August eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark travel up the Missouri River, along Iowa's western edge.

<breath> A young sergeant named Charles Floyd falls sick with terrible stomach pain. Probably a burst appendix.

<long pause> He dies near today's Sioux City. He's the only member of the expedition to die on the whole two-year journey. A tall stone obelisk marks his grave.

## Part 1D

In eighteen thirty-two, after the Black Hawk War, the Sauk and Meskwaki are forced to give up land in eastern Iowa. <gasp> Settlers rush in, by the thousands.

But the Meskwaki do something remarkable. In eighteen fifty-seven, they pool their money and buy back eighty acres of their own land. Legally. From the state.

<short pause> It grows into the Meskwaki Settlement. Not a reservation given to them. Land they bought.

## Part 1E

<short pause> In eighteen forty-six, thousands of Latter-day Saints, driven out of Illinois, cross Iowa in wagons, through a muddy, rainy spring. <groan> It takes them months to cross a state you can now drive across in five hours.

On the way, one of them writes a hymn of courage for the journey. "Come, Come, Ye Saints". <short pause> Ten years later, poor European converts start out from Iowa City, pulling handcarts, all the way to Utah.

## Part 2: Free State

In eighteen thirty-nine, before Iowa is even a state, its brand-new supreme court hears its very first case.

<short pause> A man named Ralph, enslaved in Missouri, had made a deal with his owner to buy his freedom. He moves to Iowa to work in the lead mines to earn the money. He can't pay it all. So his owner hires men to grab him and take him back.

<gasp> The court rules: Ralph is free. No one in Iowa can be held as property. <short pause> That's eighteen years before the Supreme Court rules the opposite, in the Dred Scott case.

## Part 2B

In eighteen forty-six, Iowa becomes the twenty-ninth state. A free state.

And it does something surprising. In eighteen sixty-eight, a twelve-year-old Black girl named Susan Clark is turned away from her local school in Muscatine.

<tsk> Her father sues. And the Iowa Supreme Court rules that schools cannot separate children by race.

<gasp> That's eighty-six years before the U.S. Supreme Court says the same thing, in Brown versus Board of Education.

The next year, in eighteen sixty-nine, Arabella Mansfield passes the Iowa bar exam. She becomes the first woman licensed to practise law in the United States.

<short pause> Iowa's courts keep surprising people. In two thousand nine, the Iowa Supreme Court rules, unanimously, that same-sex couples can marry. Iowa becomes only the third state where they can.

## Part 2C

<whispers> In the winter before his famous raid on Harpers Ferry, John Brown hides out in a little Quaker village in Iowa, called Springdale.

He trains his men there, drilling in the snow, while the peaceful Quakers look the other way. <short pause> Iowa towns like Tabor and Grinnell become stops on the Underground Railroad, hiding people escaping slavery on their way north.

<short pause> In the Civil War, Iowa sends a bigger share of its men to fight than almost any other state. <sigh> An Iowa woman named Annie Wittenmyer sees wounded soldiers being fed rotten food in army hospitals. She sets up special hospital kitchens, with proper meals. Hundreds of them.

## Part 3: Corn and Hogs

Settlers break up the prairie with steel ploughs. <groan> The thick roots of the prairie grass are so tough that it takes teams of oxen to break the ground.

But underneath, the deep black soil grows corn like almost nowhere else on Earth.

<chuckle> And what do you do with all that corn? Feed it to pigs. Iowa becomes the biggest hog state in America.

## Part 3B

In eighteen fifty-five, a group of German religious believers arrive and build seven villages along the Iowa River. The Amana Colonies. <short pause> For eighty years, they share everything. Everyone eats in community kitchens. Nobody gets paid.

<chuckle> In the nineteen-thirties, they finally switch to ordinary businesses. One of them starts making refrigerators. <gasp> And in nineteen sixty-seven, Amana sells one of the first microwave ovens small enough for a home kitchen.

## Part 3C

In nineteen twenty, in the little town of Onawa, a schoolteacher who also runs a sweet shop watches a boy struggle to choose between ice cream and a chocolate bar.

<laugh> So he invents both at once. A block of ice cream, dipped in chocolate. He calls it the Eskimo Pie. Soon, people are buying a million a day.

<chuckle> Iowa also gets the world's biggest cereal mill, Quaker Oats, in Cedar Rapids. And Maytag washing machines, from the town of Newton.

<laugh> In Mason City, a boy named Meredith Willson grows up playing the flute in the town band. Years later, he writes a Broadway musical about a fast-talking salesman who comes to a little Iowa town and sells everyone band instruments. The Music Man. Seventy-six trombones.

## Part 3D

In the nineteen-twenties, a young Iowa farm writer named Henry A. Wallace starts breeding a new kind of corn. Hybrid corn. It grows much more per acre.

He starts a seed company in Des Moines. Farmers buy it. <gasp> Corn harvests soar. Wallace later becomes Vice President of the United States.

## Part 3E

<sigh> But in the Great Depression, corn prices crash so low that some farmers burn corn for heat. It's cheaper than coal.

<gasp> Angry farmers block the roads to stop food from reaching market, trying to force prices up. In nineteen thirty-three, in Le Mars, a crowd of farmers drags a judge out of his courtroom, because he won't stop foreclosing on farms. They put a rope around his neck. <phew> They let him go. But the governor declares martial law.

## Part 3F

And a boy from West Branch, Iowa, Herbert Hoover, becomes president in nineteen twenty-nine. The first president born west of the Mississippi.

<short pause> In nineteen thirty, an Iowa painter named Grant Wood sees a little white farmhouse with a pointed church-style window, in the town of Eldon. He imagines the people who might live there. <chuckle> He asks his sister and his dentist to pose.

A farmer with a pitchfork. A woman beside him. American Gothic. It becomes one of the most famous paintings in America.

<chuckle> And Iowa keeps making stars. John Wayne is born in Winterset. Glenn Miller is born in Clarinda. <laugh> And the town of Riverside, Iowa, has officially declared itself the future birthplace of Captain Kirk, from Star Trek. In the year twenty-two twenty-eight.

## Part 4: Inventors

In the late nineteen-thirties, at Iowa State College, a physics professor named John Atanasoff is frustrated. His calculations take forever.

<groan> One night, he gets in his car and drives. Two hundred miles, across the border into Illinois. He stops at a roadside bar, orders a drink, and figures out how to build a machine that does maths with electricity.

With a student, Clifford Berry, he builds it. The Atanasoff-Berry Computer.

 <short pause> In nineteen seventy-three, a federal court rules it was the first electronic digital computer.

## Part 4B

In nineteen fifty-nine, the leader of the Soviet Union, Nikita Khrushchev, visits America. And one of the places he insists on visiting is an Iowa corn farm.

<chuckle> In the middle of the Cold War, with hundreds of reporters trampling the fields, the farmer, Roswell Garst, gets so annoyed that he starts throwing corn cobs and silage at them.

<laugh> Khrushchev loves it.

<short pause> Another Iowa farm boy, Norman Borlaug, grows up to be a plant scientist. He breeds new kinds of wheat, short and strong, that produce far more food. <gasp> Famines are pushed back in Mexico, India and Pakistan. He wins the Nobel Peace Prize in nineteen seventy. <short pause> Some say his work saved a billion people from starvation. Every year, Des Moines hands out the World Food Prize, which he founded.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

On February third, nineteen fifty-nine, after a concert at the Surf Ballroom in Clear Lake, Iowa, a small plane takes off in a snowstorm.

<breath> On board are rock and roll stars Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens, and J.P. Richardson, the Big Bopper. Valens is seventeen. He got his seat by winning a coin toss.

<long pause> It crashes in a cornfield minutes later. Everyone on board is killed.

Years later, a song calls it "the day the music died".

## Part 5: First in the Nation

Now, the copy machine.

In nineteen sixty-eight, the Democratic Party has a disastrous convention in Chicago. <sigh> Afterwards, it changes its rules. Ordinary voters must have more say. And local meetings have to be announced well in advance.

Iowa picks its delegates in steps. First neighbourhood meetings, called caucuses. Then county conventions. Then district conventions. Then the state convention.

<short pause> And between each step, Iowa's Democrats need time. About a month. To print and mail out all the paperwork.

<chuckle> They have one old, slow mimeograph machine.

## Part 5B

So they count backwards from the state convention in May. Month. Month. Month.

And the first caucuses land on January twenty-fourth, nineteen seventy-two.

<laugh> Earlier than any other state. By accident.

Almost nobody notices. But one candidate, George McGovern, does surprisingly well. Reporters notice. <short pause> In nineteen seventy-six, the Republicans move their caucuses to the same night.

That year, an almost unknown peanut farmer from Georgia, Jimmy Carter, spends months in Iowa, visiting living rooms and diners, sleeping in supporters' guest rooms. <gasp> He does well in Iowa. And it helps carry him all the way to the White House.

From then on, every candidate wants to win Iowa.

<short pause> In two thousand eight, a first-term senator named Barack Obama wins the Democratic caucuses, and goes on to become president. On the Republican side, Iowa's winners often don't win the nomination at all. <chuckle> In twenty sixteen, Ted Cruz beats Donald Trump in Iowa. Trump wins the nomination anyway.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In the nineteen-eighties, the farm economy crashes. Land prices fall by more than half. Interest rates soar.

<breath> Thousands of Iowa families lose farms that had been in their families for generations. Small towns empty out.

<breath> In two thousand eight, the Cedar River floods downtown Cedar Rapids, more than ten feet deeper than ever before. Thousands of homes are flooded.

<short pause> And in August twenty twenty, a storm called a derecho, a straight line of hurricane-force wind, flattens millions of acres of Iowa corn in a single afternoon.

## Part 5D

For decades, presidential hopefuls tour all ninety-nine counties, eat at the Iowa State Fair, and try a pork chop on a stick. <chuckle> They admire a life-size cow sculpted from butter. There's been one every year since nineteen eleven.

<chuckle> More than a million people visit the fair every August. To eat things on sticks, look at the biggest boar in the state, and watch politicians try not to drop their food on camera.

<laugh> In nineteen eighty-nine, a movie about a farmer who builds a baseball field in his corn is filmed near Dyersville. "If you build it, he will come." Tourists still come to play catch on that field.

## Part 5E

<tsk> The caucus system has its critics. People say Iowa is too small, too rural, and doesn't look like the rest of America. After a messy count in twenty twenty, the Democrats moved their first contest to South Carolina.

<sigh> In August twenty twenty-six, they left Iowa out of their early states for twenty twenty-eight, too. <short pause> But the Republicans still start in Iowa.

## Part 5F

So how does a quiet farm state become the first stop on the road to the White House?

<whispers> It starts with a slow copy machine.

New rules needed a month between every step. Iowa had four steps, and one slow mimeograph. Counting back put Iowa's caucuses in January, ahead of everyone. <short pause> And once candidates and reporters showed up, Iowa never wanted to give up its spot.

<breath> A machine that couldn't print fast enough made a small farm state one of the most important places in American politics.

<chuckle> And it made the pork chop on a stick a presidential tradition.
