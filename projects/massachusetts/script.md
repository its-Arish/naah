# The Entire History of Massachusetts

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,000 spoken words, so roughly 16 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused, playful for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And in sixteen ninety-two"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, gentle storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Curious and a little wry. |
| Part 3 | Rising energy, rebellious, fun. |
| Part 4 | Brisk, industrious, then principled. |
| Part 5 | Inventive and upbeat, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Warm and steady, then proud, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1620 · Pilgrims 1620 to 1630 · Puritans 1630 to 1692 · Revolution 1765 to 1783 · Mills and Whales 1810 to 1860 · Inventions 1846 to 1920 · Modern 1920 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Basketball? <short pause> Invented in Massachusetts. Volleyball? Also Massachusetts. Four years later, just up the road.

The chocolate chip cookie was invented at an inn in Massachusetts.

<gasp> In nineteen nineteen, a giant tank burst in Boston, and a wave of molasses swept through the streets.

<long pause> And in sixteen ninety-two, Massachusetts hanged its own neighbours for witchcraft.

<short pause> So how does a colony that hanged people for being witches become the place the whole world sends its smartest kids?

<whispers> It starts with a law about the Devil.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Long before anyone in Europe had heard of it, this land belonged to the Wampanoag, the Massachusett, the Nipmuc, the Pocumtuc and other peoples.

They fish the coast and the rivers. They hunt in the forests. And they grow corn, beans and squash together in the same mounds. The corn grows tall. The beans climb the corn. The squash leaves shade the ground. <short pause> The Three Sisters.

The state's name comes from the Massachusett people. It means something like "at the great hill".

## Part 1B

And the sea off this coast is packed with fish. <short pause> Especially cod.

In sixteen-oh-two, an English captain named Bartholomew Gosnold catches so many cod off a hooked sandy peninsula that he names it after them. Cape Cod.

<chuckle> Cod is so important to Massachusetts that, since the seventeen hundreds, a carved wooden fish has hung in the State House. The Sacred Cod. When college pranksters stole it in nineteen thirty-three, lawmakers refused to meet until it came back.

## Part 1C (quiet style)

Between sixteen sixteen and sixteen nineteen, a terrible sickness spreads along the coast, probably brought by European sailors and traders.

<breath> Whole villages die. In some places, almost nobody is left.

<long pause> One of the empty villages is called Patuxet.

## Part 2: Pilgrims

In sixteen twenty, a small ship called the Mayflower sails from England. On board are about a hundred passengers, many of them religious separatists who want to worship their own way. We call them the Pilgrims.

They're supposed to land near the Hudson River. But storms and dangerous shoals push them north, to Cape Cod. <tsk> They're outside their legal area.

So before they even get off the ship, the men sign an agreement to govern themselves. <short pause> The Mayflower Compact.

They settle on the site of the empty village of Patuxet. They call it Plymouth.

<chuckle> And Plymouth Rock? Nobody mentions it for a hundred and twenty years. Then a ninety-four-year-old man says his father told him that's where the Pilgrims stepped ashore. People have been chipping off souvenirs ever since.

## Part 2B

The first winter is brutal. <sigh> About half of them die.

Then, in March, a man walks into their settlement and says, in English, "Welcome, Englishmen."

<gasp> His name is Samoset. He learned some English from fishermen. Soon after, he brings a man called Tisquantum. Squanto.

Squanto had been kidnapped years earlier, taken to Spain, then England, and found his way home... <long pause> only to discover his whole village, Patuxet, had died.

He teaches the Pilgrims how to plant corn, with fish buried in the mounds for fertiliser. And the Wampanoag leader Massasoit makes an alliance with them.

In the autumn of sixteen twenty-one, they share a harvest feast that lasts three days. About fifty Pilgrims. About ninety Wampanoag men.

<short pause> Today, we call it the first Thanksgiving.

## Part 2C

Ten years later, a much bigger wave arrives. The Puritans. Thousands of them.

Their leader, John Winthrop, tells them they will be like a "city upon a hill". The whole world will be watching.

They found Boston in sixteen thirty. And they are obsessed with one thing. <short pause> Reading the Bible.

So in sixteen thirty-five, they open a public school in Boston, Boston Latin. It's still open today. The oldest school in America.

In sixteen thirty-six, they found a college to train ministers. It's named after a young minister who leaves it his books and half his money. John Harvard.

And in sixteen thirty-nine, the first printing press in the English colonies arrives in Cambridge.

## Part 2D

And in sixteen forty-seven, they pass a law that begins, and this is real: <short pause> "It being one chief project of that old deluder, Satan, to keep men from the knowledge of the Scriptures..."

<chuckle> The Devil, they reasoned, wants people ignorant. So every town with fifty families must hire a teacher. Every town with a hundred families must start a grammar school.

The Old Deluder Satan Act. One of the very first public school laws in America.

<short pause> But reading the Bible for yourself has a side effect. <chuckle> People start having their own opinions about it.

A woman named Anne Hutchinson holds Bible meetings in her home, and tells her neighbours what she thinks the ministers are getting wrong. In sixteen thirty-eight, she's put on trial and banished. A young minister named Roger Williams, who says the government shouldn't punish people for their beliefs, is thrown out too. <short pause> They head south. And help found a whole new colony. Rhode Island.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

But the colony keeps growing, and taking land.

In sixteen seventy-five, Massasoit's son Metacom, whom the English call King Philip, leads a war to drive the settlers out.

<breath> For its size, King Philip's War is one of the deadliest wars in American history. Thousands die. Native nations are shattered. Many survivors are sold into slavery in the Caribbean.

<long pause> Metacom is killed. The English display his head on a pole in Plymouth for more than twenty years.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

And in sixteen ninety-two, in the village of Salem, a few girls begin having strange fits.

They say they are being tortured by witches. They start naming names.

<breath> Within months, more than a hundred and fifty people are accused. Nineteen are hanged. One old man, Giles Corey, is crushed to death under heavy stones for refusing to plead.

<long pause> A few years later, one of the judges stands up in church and publicly apologises. Some of the accusers later say they were wrong. <short pause> It is far too late.

## Part 2G

But thirty years after Salem, Boston gets something very right.

In seventeen twenty-one, smallpox hits the city. A Puritan minister, Cotton Mather, remembers something an enslaved African man in his house, named Onesimus, once told him.

<short pause> In Africa, Onesimus said, people take a tiny bit of matter from someone sick, and scratch it into the skin of a healthy person. They get a mild case... and then never catch it again.

<gasp> Mather convinces a doctor to try it. People are furious. Someone throws a firebomb through Mather's window.

But the results are clear. Of the people who are inoculated, far fewer die. It's one of the first big tests of inoculation in the Western world. <short pause> And it started with a man nobody had thought to listen to.

## Part 3: Revolution

Fast forward to the seventeen sixties.

Britain has just won a huge, expensive war. And it decides the colonies should help pay for it. Taxes on paper. Taxes on glass. Taxes on tea.

<chuckle> And Boston... Boston does not like that.

A group called the Sons of Liberty organise protests. They hang a tax collector's dummy from a giant elm tree, the Liberty Tree.

## Part 3B

In March seventeen seventy, a crowd throws snowballs, sticks and insults at British soldiers. The soldiers fire.

<breath> Five people are killed, including a sailor of African and Native descent, Crispus Attucks. It's called the Boston Massacre.

<short pause> And the lawyer who defends the soldiers in court? John Adams. A future president. He believes even the enemy deserves a fair trial.

## Part 3C

In December seventeen seventy-three, men dressed as Mohawk warriors board three ships and dump three hundred and forty-two chests of tea into Boston Harbor.

The Boston Tea Party. <laugh> Probably the most expensive cup of tea in history.

<short pause> Britain shuts Boston's port. Everyone prepares to fight.

## Part 3D

On the night of April eighteenth, seventeen seventy-five, Paul Revere and William Dawes ride out to warn that British troops are coming. <chuckle> No, he almost certainly didn't shout "The British are coming!" Most people back then still thought of themselves as British.

The next morning, at Lexington and Concord, someone fires. The shot heard round the world.

<long pause> The American Revolution begins in Massachusetts.

Two months later, the Battle of Bunker Hill. <chuckle> Which was actually fought mostly on Breed's Hill.

## Part 3E

Then, in March seventeen seventy-six, the British in Boston wake up to a shock.

<gasp> Overnight, George Washington's army has dragged heavy cannons onto the hills of Dorchester Heights, pointing straight down at the British ships. Cannons hauled all the way from a captured fort in New York, about three hundred miles, on sledges, through the snow.

The British sail away. Boston is free.

<chuckle> The date is March seventeenth. Boston still has a holiday for it. Evacuation Day. It just happens to also be Saint Patrick's Day.

## Part 3F

In seventeen eighty, John Adams writes the Massachusetts Constitution. It's still in use today. <short pause> The oldest written constitution still working anywhere in the world.

It says all men are born free and equal.

So an enslaved woman named Mum Bett, who heard those words read aloud, finds a lawyer and sues for her freedom. <gasp> She wins. She takes a new name. Elizabeth Freeman.

And an enslaved man named Quock Walker goes to court with the same argument. Well then, that includes me.

He wins too. By seventeen eighty-three, slavery in Massachusetts is effectively over.

## Part 3G

But peace brings a new problem. Debt.

In seventeen eighty-six, farmers in western Massachusetts are being dragged to court and losing their farms over taxes they can't pay. Many of them are war veterans who were never properly paid.

<short pause> So they rise up. Led by a former army captain, Daniel Shays, they close the courts, and march on the federal armoury at Springfield.

<gasp> The state militia fires a cannon. The rebels scatter.

Shays' Rebellion is crushed. But it frightens America's leaders. If the country can't even handle a few angry farmers, maybe it needs a stronger government. <short pause> Months later, they meet in Philadelphia, and write the Constitution of the United States.

## Part 4: Mills and Whales

In the eighteen hundreds, Massachusetts changes how America works.

In Lowell, giant water-powered mills spin cotton into cloth. Many of the workers are young women from farm families, the Lowell mill girls. They work long hours, live in company boarding houses, and go on strike for better pay. <short pause> They even publish their own magazine.

<sigh> The cotton itself is picked by enslaved people in the South.

## Part 4B

On the coast, Nantucket and New Bedford send whaling ships around the world, for years at a time. Whale oil lights the lamps of America. For a while, New Bedford is said to be one of the richest cities in the world, for its size.

<short pause> In eighteen twenty, a Nantucket whaleship, the Essex, is rammed and sunk by an angry sperm whale in the middle of the Pacific. The survivors drift for months.

A young sailor named Herman Melville hears the story. Years later, he writes Moby-Dick.

## Part 4C

And Massachusetts thinks big thoughts.

In Concord, Ralph Waldo Emerson writes about trusting yourself. His friend Henry David Thoreau builds a tiny cabin by Walden Pond, lives there for two years, and writes about living simply. <chuckle> His mum lives a short walk away. He goes home for dinner quite often.

Louisa May Alcott, also from Concord, writes Little Women.

## Part 4D

And the Old Deluder Satan Act finally gets a modern version.

In eighteen thirty-seven, a lawyer named Horace Mann becomes the head of Massachusetts' new state Board of Education. The first one in America.

He travels everywhere, visiting schools. He finds leaking roofs, freezing classrooms and teachers with no training. <tsk> So he fights for better buildings, trained teachers and schools free for everyone.

<gasp> In eighteen fifty-two, Massachusetts passes the first law in America saying that children MUST go to school.

Every other state eventually follows. Horace Mann is called the father of the American public school.

## Part 4E

And Boston becomes the centre of the fight against slavery. William Lloyd Garrison prints The Liberator. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery, gives his first big speech in Massachusetts.

In the Civil War, the Fifty-fourth Massachusetts becomes one of the first official Black regiments in the Union Army. <short pause> At Fort Wagner, in South Carolina, they charge into fire. Nearly half are killed, wounded or captured. Their courage changes what America thinks Black soldiers can do.

## Part 5: Inventions

And then, the inventions.

In October eighteen forty-six, at Massachusetts General Hospital, a dentist puts a patient to sleep with ether, and a surgeon removes a tumour from his neck. <gasp> The patient wakes up and says he felt no pain. It's the first public demonstration of anaesthesia. <short pause> The room is still there. People call it the Ether Dome.

In eighteen seventy-six, in Boston, Alexander Graham Bell speaks the first words through a telephone.

In eighteen ninety-seven, Boston runs the first Boston Marathon. And opens the first subway in America.

<short pause> Women weren't allowed to run that marathon for decades. In nineteen sixty-six, women weren't allowed to run it. So Bobbi Gibb hid in the bushes near the start, jumped in, and finished anyway. <chuckle> The next year, Kathrine Switzer signed up with just her initials. When an official spotted her, he ran into the road and tried to rip off her race number. Her friends shoved him aside. She finished too.

## Part 5B

In eighteen ninety-one, in Springfield, a teacher named James Naismith needs a game his students can play indoors in winter. He nails two peach baskets to a balcony.

<chuckle> Basketball. Every time someone scores, somebody has to climb up and get the ball out.

Four years later, in Holyoke, William Morgan wants something gentler. Volleyball.

## Part 5C

And the snacks. In eighteen ninety-one, a bakery near Boston names a fig cookie after a nearby town. Newton. <short pause> Fig Newtons.

In nineteen thirty-eight, at the Toll House Inn in Whitman, Ruth Wakefield adds chopped chocolate to her cookie dough. <chuckle> The chocolate doesn't melt in. And the world gets the chocolate chip cookie.

And in nineteen fifty, a man in Quincy opens a doughnut shop. Today you know it as Dunkin'.

## Part 5D

And the gadgets.

In nineteen-oh-one, a Boston salesman named King Gillette starts selling razors with cheap blades you throw away when they get dull. <chuckle> The idea is to sell the handle cheap, and keep selling blades. Forever.

And in nineteen forty-three, a scientist from Cambridge named Edwin Land is on holiday, taking photos of his three-year-old daughter. She asks: why can't I see the picture now?

<short pause> He can't stop thinking about it. Within a few years, he invents a camera that prints its own photo in about a minute. <gasp> The Polaroid.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

On January fifteenth, nineteen nineteen, in Boston's North End, a giant tank holding more than two million gallons of molasses bursts open.

<breath> A wave of syrup, taller than a house, rushes through the streets at more than thirty miles an hour.

<long pause> Twenty-one people die. Some say that on hot days, the neighbourhood smelled of molasses for decades.

## Part 5F (quiet style)

In nineteen twenty-one, two Italian immigrants and anarchists, Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, are convicted of murder in a robbery near Boston. Many people around the world believe the trial is unfair, full of prejudice against immigrants.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-seven, they are executed. Fifty years later, the governor of Massachusetts declares their trial was unjust.

## Part 6: Modern

The twentieth century belongs to a Massachusetts family. John F. Kennedy, from Brookline, becomes president in nineteen sixty-one.

And Boston becomes a brain city. Harvard. MIT. Dozens more colleges. In the Second World War, MIT scientists help perfect radar. Later come early computers, and biotech labs that make medicines and vaccines.

<gasp> In nineteen fifty-four, in Boston, doctors carry out the first successful organ transplant. A kidney, from one identical twin to the other.

## Part 6B

In two thousand four, Massachusetts becomes the first state in America to allow same-sex couples to marry.

<laugh> The same year, the Boston Red Sox win the World Series, for the first time since nineteen eighteen. Eighty-six years. Fans called it the Curse of the Bambino, after the team sold Babe Ruth to the Yankees.

<short pause> Boston also finishes one of the biggest building projects in American history. The Big Dig. It moves an ugly elevated highway that cut through the city... underground. It takes years longer than planned and costs many billions more. <chuckle> But today, where the highway used to be, there's a park.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In April twenty thirteen, two bombs explode near the finish line of the Boston Marathon. <long pause> Three people are killed and hundreds are hurt.

<breath> The city's answer becomes two words. Boston Strong.

## Part 6D

So how does a colony that hanged people for being witches become the place the whole world sends its smartest kids?

<short pause> It starts with a law about the Devil.

The Puritans were so afraid of Satan that they made every town teach its children to read. The same fear that hanged nineteen people in Salem also built some of the first public schools in America, and its oldest college.

Out of the schools came the readers. Out of the readers came the arguers. And out of the arguers came a revolution, the fight against slavery, and a city full of universities.

<long pause> Massachusetts has always run on ideas. Some of them were terrible. Some of them changed the world.

<chuckle> And one of them was putting chocolate in a cookie.
