# The Entire History of Missouri

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "And in eighteen fifty-seven"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm river storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, a little cheeky, frontier-town energy. |
| Part 3 | Bustling starting-line energy, then heavy. |
| Part 4 | Tense and grim, then warm and folksy. |
| Part 5 | Big, bright, fairground wonder; jazzy. |
| Part 6 | Proud and steady; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1764 · St. Louis 1764 to 1821 · Gateway 1821 to 1860 · Civil War 1861 to 1865 · Ragtime 1865 to 1904 · World's Fair 1904 to 1945 · The Arch 1945 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Iced tea and the ice cream cone? <short pause> Both made famous at a fair in St. Louis.

<chuckle> The Gateway Arch is more than twice as tall as the Statue of Liberty.

The Oregon Trail, the Santa Fe Trail and the Pony Express all started in Missouri.

<long pause> And in eighteen fifty-seven, the Supreme Court ruled that Dred Scott, an enslaved man from St. Louis, could never be a citizen. And neither could any Black American.

<short pause> So how does one riverside trading post become the doorway to the whole American West?

<whispers> It starts with a fourteen-year-old boy and a crew of thirty men.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Two of the biggest rivers in North America meet in Missouri. The Missouri River, flowing from the Rocky Mountains. And the Mississippi, flowing to the Gulf of Mexico.

<short pause> For a few miles after they meet, you can see the two rivers side by side in the same channel. The muddy brown Missouri, and the clearer Mississippi, not quite mixing.

<chuckle> In the south, in the Ozark hills, the ground is full of holes. Missouri has more than seven thousand known caves. It calls itself the Cave State. Springs pour out of the hillsides, some of them gushing millions of gallons of water a day.

## Part 1B

Wherever rivers meet, people meet. A thousand years ago, just across the Mississippi, the great city of Cahokia rises. Giant earth pyramids. Tens of thousands of people. <gasp> St. Louis itself once had so many ancient mounds that it was nicknamed "Mound City".

Later, the Osage and the Missouria live here. The Osage are famous for being tall. <chuckle> European visitors are amazed that many Osage men stand over six feet. The name "Missouri" probably comes from a word meaning "people of the big canoes".

## Part 1C

In the seventeen hundreds, French settlers arrive from Canada and Louisiana. They mine lead from the hills, trade furs with the Osage, and build a little village called Ste. Genevieve. <short pause> Some of its old French houses, built with upright logs stuck in the ground, are still standing.

## Part 2: St. Louis

Now, the boy.

In seventeen sixty-three, a French fur trader named Pierre Laclède goes looking for the perfect spot for a trading post. He finds it. A high limestone bluff on the Mississippi, just below where the Missouri River joins it. <short pause> Safe from floods. And close enough to trade with everyone upriver.

He goes back to get supplies. And he leaves his stepson in charge of building it.

<chuckle> The stepson is Auguste Chouteau. He's about fourteen years old.

In February seventeen sixty-four, Auguste leads a crew of about thirty men to the bluff, and they start building. Cutting trees. Clearing ground. Building cabins.

They name it after a French king. Saint Louis.

## Part 2B

<tsk> Just one problem. France has already secretly given all its land west of the Mississippi to Spain. Nobody tells the people of St. Louis for years.

The fur trading post grows anyway. Chouteau grows up to be one of the richest men in the West.

<gasp> In seventeen eighty, during the American Revolution, British soldiers and their Native allies attack St. Louis, now a Spanish town. The townspeople fight them off from behind hastily built walls. <short pause> It's the westernmost battle of the whole Revolutionary War.

## Part 2C

St. Louis keeps changing hands. Spanish. Then French again, on paper. And in eighteen-oh-three, the United States buys the whole Louisiana Territory.

<chuckle> In a ceremony in St. Louis, the Spanish flag comes down, and the French flag goes up. The next day, the French flag comes down, and the American flag goes up. It's remembered as "Three Flags Day".

In May eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark set off up the Missouri River from near St. Louis to explore the West. <short pause> When they come back two years later, the whole country knows the way west starts here.

<chuckle> The old frontiersman Daniel Boone moves to Missouri too, in his sixties. He says Kentucky has got too crowded.

## Part 2D

In the winter of eighteen eleven and twelve, the ground shakes. Three of the most powerful earthquakes in American history hit near New Madrid, Missouri.

<gasp> People feel the shaking as far away as the East Coast. Church bells ring by themselves. Land sinks. New lakes form. <short pause> And for a few hours, parts of the Mississippi River flow backwards.

## Part 3: Gateway

In eighteen twenty-one, Missouri becomes a state. The twenty-fourth.

<sigh> But America fights about it first. Missouri wants slavery. To keep the balance, Congress lets Maine in as a free state, and draws a line across the West. The Missouri Compromise. North of the line, no slavery. Except Missouri.

<short pause> Old Thomas Jefferson hears about it, and says it fills him with terror. Like a fire bell in the night.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-eight, Missouri's governor issues an order that members of the Latter-day Saints church be driven from the state, or exterminated.

<breath> At a little settlement called Haun's Mill, a militia attacks. Seventeen people are killed, including children.

<long pause> Thousands of church members are driven out of Missouri in the winter.

## Part 3C

And Missouri becomes the starting line for the West.

Steamboats bring settlers up the Missouri River to towns like Independence and Westport. There, they buy wagons, oxen, flour, bacon and coffee. <chuckle> Shopkeepers get rich selling to people who have no idea what they'll need. And they set off.

The Santa Fe Trail. The Oregon Trail. The California Trail. All starting in Missouri.

<gasp> In eighteen sixty, from St. Joseph, Missouri, the first Pony Express riders gallop west, carrying mail two thousand miles to California in about ten days. Riders change horses every ten or fifteen miles.

<chuckle> It lasts about a year and a half. Then the telegraph puts it out of business.

## Part 3D

<gasp> St. Louis booms. On some days, more than a hundred and fifty steamboats are tied up along its riverfront, puffing smoke, loading cotton, furs, lead and passengers.

<short pause> In eighteen thirty-six, a young newspaper editor in St. Louis, Elijah Lovejoy, writes against slavery. <tsk> Angry mobs wreck his printing press. He moves across the river to Illinois, and keeps writing.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

St. Louis is also a place where people are bought and sold.

<breath> An enslaved man named Dred Scott has lived with his enslaver for years in free territory. So, in eighteen forty-six, in the St. Louis courthouse, he and his wife Harriet sue for their freedom.

The case goes on for eleven years. All the way to the Supreme Court.

<long pause> In eighteen fifty-seven, the court rules against him. It says no Black person, free or enslaved, can be an American citizen.

<breath> A few months later, Dred Scott's new owners free him anyway. He dies just over a year later. <short pause> The ruling pushes the country closer to war.

## Part 4: Civil War

In the Civil War, Missouri is torn apart. Its governor wants to join the Confederacy. <gasp> Union troops march on the state militia's camp in St. Louis, and take it. Missouri stays in the Union. But many of its people fight for the South.

<sigh> More battles and skirmishes are fought in Missouri than in almost any other state. Brutal guerrilla war spreads across the countryside. Ambushes. Burned farms. Murders.

<short pause> At Wilson's Creek, in August eighteen sixty-one, the Union general Nathaniel Lyon is shot from his horse. The first Union general killed in the war.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-three, after a guerrilla massacre in Kansas, a Union general orders almost everyone living in four Missouri border counties to leave their homes.

<breath> Tens of thousands of people are driven out. Their farms are burned.

<long pause> For years after, the area is called the Burnt District.

## Part 4C

One young guerrilla is a teenager named Jesse James. After the war, he and his brother Frank rob banks and trains across the Midwest. <chuckle> Newspapers turn him into a kind of Robin Hood. He isn't one.

<short pause> In eighteen eighty-two, in his house in St. Joseph, one of his own gang shoots him in the back of the head, for the reward money.

## Part 4D

Near the little town of Diamond, Missouri, a baby born into slavery is kidnapped by raiders, with his mother, during the war. His owner gets him back... in exchange for a horse. <sigh> His mother is never found.

He grows up curious about every plant he sees. People call him the plant doctor. <short pause> George Washington Carver becomes a famous scientist, finding hundreds of uses for peanuts and sweet potatoes, to help poor Southern farmers. In nineteen forty-three, his birthplace becomes the first national monument to a Black American.

## Part 4E

And a boy grows up in the river town of Hannibal, Missouri, watching the steamboats. <chuckle> He skips school, explores caves, and gets into trouble.

He becomes a riverboat pilot. Then a writer. He takes his pen name from a riverboat call meaning the water is two fathoms deep. Safe to pass.

Mark Twain. <short pause> He writes Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn, about boys just like him, on the Mississippi.

<chuckle> He was born the year Halley's comet flew past Earth. He says he'll go out with it too. In nineteen-ten, the comet comes back. And the day after it's closest to the Sun, he dies.

## Part 5: Ragtime

In the little railroad town of Sedalia, in eighteen ninety-nine, a piano player named Scott Joplin publishes a bouncy, syncopated tune named after a club where he plays. The Maple Leaf Rag.

<laugh> It sells hundreds of thousands of copies. Ragtime takes over America. Joplin becomes the King of Ragtime.

<short pause> In St. Louis, a German immigrant named Adolphus Busch marries into a brewing family. <chuckle> He's a salesman genius. He puts beer in refrigerated railway cars, and pasteurises it so it lasts. Budweiser becomes one of the first beers sold all across America.

## Part 5B

In nineteen-oh-four, St. Louis throws a giant party. The World's Fair. <gasp> Twenty million visitors. Palaces of electricity, machines and art. A giant Ferris wheel.

It's hot. Really hot. So visitors buy cold tea with ice. Iced tea becomes a sensation. <chuckle> Ice cream vendors serve scoops in rolled-up waffles. The ice cream cone takes off.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

But the fair also has a dark side.

<breath> It puts real people on display, as exhibits. More than a thousand Filipinos, in fake villages. And a young Congolese man named Ota Benga, brought from Africa to be stared at.

<long pause> Visitors are told they're looking at "primitive" people. It's meant to show off empire.

## Part 5D

The same year, St. Louis hosts the Olympics. The first ones ever held in the United States.

<laugh> The marathon is a disaster. It's run on dusty roads, in the heat, with almost no water along the way. The first runner to cross the finish line turns out to have ridden most of the way in a car. The real winner is so exhausted that his trainers keep him going with rat poison mixed with egg whites and brandy. <phew> He survives. Just.

## Part 5E

In nineteen twenty-seven, a group of St. Louis businessmen pay for a young pilot's airplane. He names it after their city. The Spirit of St. Louis. <gasp> Charles Lindbergh flies it non-stop, alone, from New York to Paris.

In Kansas City, jazz musicians play all night, and barbecue smoke fills the streets. A young cartoonist named Walt Disney starts his first studio there. <chuckle> Legend says a friendly mouse in that studio gave him an idea.

## Part 5F

<short pause> And a girl from a poor family in St. Louis, Josephine Baker, dances her way to Paris. <gasp> She becomes one of the biggest stars in Europe. In the Second World War, she spies for the French Resistance, smuggling secret messages written in invisible ink on her sheet music.

<chuckle> And in the nineteen-fifties, a St. Louis guitarist named Chuck Berry duck-walks across the stage, and helps invent rock and roll.

## Part 6: The Arch

A farmer from Missouri, Harry Truman, becomes president. On his desk, he keeps a sign. "The buck stops here."

<short pause> In nineteen forty-six, Truman brings the former British prime minister Winston Churchill to a little college in Fulton, Missouri. And there, Churchill gives a famous speech. He says an "iron curtain" has come down across Europe. <whispers> The Cold War has a name.

<chuckle> Meanwhile, in Kansas City, a young man selling postcards starts a greeting card company. Hallmark.

<short pause> In the Ozarks, the little town of Branson fills with country music theatres, until it has more theatre seats than Broadway.

## Part 6B

In nineteen sixty-five, St. Louis finishes a giant stainless-steel arch on the riverfront. Six hundred and thirty feet tall. The tallest monument in the United States.

<gasp> When the builders get to the very top, the last piece won't fit. The sun has made the steel expand. <chuckle> So firefighters spray one leg with water, to cool it down and shrink it. The final piece slides in.

The Gateway Arch. Built to honour the city's role as the gateway to the West.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In August two thousand fourteen, in Ferguson, near St. Louis, a police officer shoots and kills an unarmed Black teenager named Michael Brown.

<breath> Protests fill the streets for weeks, and spread across the country.

## Part 6D

Missouri also calls itself the "Show-Me State". <chuckle> A Missouri congressman once said, "Frankly, I'm from Missouri. You have got to show me."

## Part 6E

So how does one riverside trading post become the doorway to the whole American West?

<whispers> It starts with a fourteen-year-old boy and a crew of thirty men.

Laclède picked the spot where the great rivers met. Auguste Chouteau built it. <short pause> Everything heading west, furs, explorers, wagons and mail, had to pass through that meeting of rivers. And the city that grew there became the gateway.

<breath> Two hundred years later, St. Louis built a giant arch to say so.

<chuckle> Not bad for a construction project run by a fourteen-year-old.
