## Hook

Iced tea and the ice cream cone? <short pause> Both made famous at a fair in St. Louis.

The Gateway Arch is more than twice as tall as the Statue of Liberty.

The Oregon Trail, the Santa Fe Trail and the Pony Express all started in Missouri.

<long pause> And in eighteen fifty-seven, the Supreme Court ruled that Dred Scott, an enslaved man from St. Louis, could never be a citizen. And neither could any Black American.

<short pause> So how does one riverside trading post become the doorway to the whole American West?

<whispers> It starts with a fourteen-year-old boy and a crew of thirty men.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Two of the biggest rivers in North America meet in Missouri. The Missouri River, flowing from the Rocky Mountains. And the Mississippi, flowing to the Gulf of Mexico.

Wherever rivers meet, people meet. A thousand years ago, just across the Mississippi, the great city of Cahokia rises.

Later, the Osage and the Missouria live here. The name "Missouri" probably comes from a word meaning "people of the big canoes".

## Part 1B

In the seventeen hundreds, French settlers arrive from Canada and Louisiana. They mine lead, trade furs, and build a little village called Ste. Genevieve.

## Part 2: St. Louis

Now, the boy.

In seventeen sixty-three, a French fur trader named Pierre Laclède goes looking for the perfect spot for a trading post. He finds it. A high bluff on the Mississippi, just below where the Missouri River joins it. Safe from floods. And close enough to trade with everyone upriver.

He goes back to get supplies. And he leaves his stepson in charge of building it.

The stepson is Auguste Chouteau. He's about fourteen years old.

In February seventeen sixty-four, Auguste leads a crew of about thirty men to the bluff, and they start building.

They name it after a French king. Saint Louis.

## Part 2B

The fur trading post grows. Chouteau grows up to be one of the richest men in the West.

And St. Louis changes hands. French. Then Spanish. Then French again for a few weeks. And in eighteen-oh-three, the United States buys the whole Louisiana Territory.

In a ceremony in St. Louis, the flags change so fast that it's remembered as "Three Flags Day".

## Part 2C

In May eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark set off up the Missouri River from near St. Louis to explore the West. When they come back two years later, the whole country knows the way west starts here.

## Part 2D

In the winter of eighteen eleven and twelve, the ground shakes. Three of the most powerful earthquakes in American history hit near New Madrid, Missouri.

People feel the shaking as far away as the East Coast. Land sinks. New lakes form. And for a few hours, parts of the Mississippi River flow backwards.

## Part 3: Gateway

In eighteen twenty-one, Missouri becomes a state. The twenty-fourth.

But America fights about it first. Missouri wants slavery. To keep the balance, Congress lets Maine in as a free state, and draws a line across the West. The Missouri Compromise. North of the line, no slavery. Except Missouri.

## Part 3B

And Missouri becomes the starting line for the West.

Steamboats bring settlers up the Missouri River to towns like Independence and Westport. There, they buy wagons, oxen, flour and bacon. And they set off.

The Santa Fe Trail. The Oregon Trail. The California Trail. All starting in Missouri.

In eighteen sixty, from St. Joseph, Missouri, the first Pony Express riders gallop west, carrying mail two thousand miles to California in about ten days.

<chuckle> It lasts about a year and a half. Then the telegraph puts it out of business.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

St. Louis is also a place where people are bought and sold.

An enslaved man named Dred Scott has lived with his enslaver in free territory. So, in eighteen forty-six, in the St. Louis courthouse, he and his wife Harriet sue for their freedom.

The case goes all the way to the Supreme Court. In eighteen fifty-seven, the court rules against him. It says no Black person, free or enslaved, can be an American citizen.

The ruling pushes the country closer to war.

## Part 4: Civil War

In the Civil War, Missouri is torn apart. It stays in the Union. But many of its people fight for the South.

Brutal guerrilla war spreads across the countryside. Ambushes. Burned farms. Murders. One young guerrilla is a teenager named Jesse James. After the war, he robs banks and trains.

## Part 4B

And a boy grows up in the river town of Hannibal, Missouri, watching the steamboats. He becomes a riverboat pilot. Then a writer. He takes his pen name from a riverboat call meaning the water is two fathoms deep. Safe to pass.

Mark Twain.

## Part 5: World's Fair

In nineteen-oh-four, St. Louis throws a giant party. The World's Fair. Twenty million visitors. Palaces of electricity, machines and art.

It's hot. Really hot. So visitors buy cold tea with ice. Iced tea becomes a sensation. Ice cream vendors serve scoops in rolled-up waffles. The ice cream cone takes off.

The same year, St. Louis hosts the Olympics. The first ones ever held in the United States.

## Part 5B

In nineteen twenty-seven, a group of St. Louis businessmen pay for a young pilot's airplane. He names it after their city. The Spirit of St. Louis. Charles Lindbergh flies it non-stop from New York to Paris.

In Kansas City, jazz musicians play all night, and barbecue smoke fills the streets.

And a farmer from Missouri, Harry Truman, becomes president. On his desk, he keeps a sign. "The buck stops here."

## Part 5C

In nineteen sixty-five, St. Louis finishes a giant stainless-steel arch on the riverfront. Six hundred and thirty feet tall. The tallest monument in the United States.

The Gateway Arch. Built to honour the city's role as the gateway to the West.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In August two thousand fourteen, in Ferguson, near St. Louis, a police officer shoots and kills an unarmed Black teenager named Michael Brown. Protests spread across the country.

## Part 5E

Missouri also calls itself the "Show-Me State". A Missouri congressman once said, "Frankly, I'm from Missouri. You have got to show me."

## Part 5F

So how does one riverside trading post become the doorway to the whole American West?

It starts with a fourteen-year-old boy and a crew of thirty men.

Laclède picked the spot where the great rivers met. Auguste Chouteau built it. Everything heading west, furs, explorers, wagons and mail, had to pass through that meeting of rivers. And the city that grew there became the gateway.

<short pause> Two hundred years later, St. Louis built a giant arch to say so.

<chuckle> Not bad for a construction project run by a fourteen-year-old.
