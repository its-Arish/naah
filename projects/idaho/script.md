# The Entire History of Idaho

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,500 spoken words, so roughly 13 to 14 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen sixty-one"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Rugged, awed storyteller of canyons and lava. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous trail voice, a little cheeky. |
| Part 3 | Gold-rush rowdy, then tough and tense. |
| Part 4 | Down-to-earth farm voice, warm and inventive. |
| Part 5 | Secretive, then wonder-struck, then grave. |
| Part 6 | Bright and playful; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1805 · Trails 1805 to 1860 · Gold and Name 1860 to 1890 · Silver Wars 1890 to 1910 · Potatoes 1910 to 1945 · Atomic Desert 1942 to 1961 · Today 1961 to now.

## Hook

About a third of America's potatoes come from Idaho.

<chuckle> The name "Idaho" was probably made up. A man claimed it meant "gem of the mountains". It doesn't mean anything at all.

And electronic television was dreamed up by a fourteen-year-old ploughing a potato field in Idaho.

<long pause> In nineteen sixty-one, an experimental reactor in the Idaho desert exploded, killing three men. The only deadly reactor accident in American history.

And a little town in Idaho became the first in the world lit up entirely by nuclear power.

<short pause> So how does a quiet state of potato farms become the birthplace of nuclear power?

<whispers> It starts with a desert so empty the Navy used it for target practice.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Idaho is wild. Rugged mountains, deep canyons and fast white-water rivers. <gasp> Hells Canyon, on the Snake River, is the deepest river gorge in North America. Deeper than the Grand Canyon.

In the south, the Snake River cuts across a giant plain of old lava, in a huge smile shape. <short pause> That plain is the track of a hotspot deep under the Earth. Over millions of years, as the continent slid over it, it burned a path of volcanoes across Idaho. <whispers> Today, that hotspot sits under Yellowstone.

On the lava plain, the river crashes over Shoshone Falls. <chuckle> It's taller than Niagara Falls.

## Part 1B

At a place called Hagerman, by the Snake River, fossil hunters dig up the bones of more than a hundred ancient horses, from more than three million years ago. <short pause> The Hagerman horse. One of the earliest members of the same family as today's horses. It's Idaho's state fossil.

<sigh> Those first American horses died out at the end of the Ice Age. For thousands of years, there were no horses in America at all.

## Part 1C

The land is home to the Shoshone, the Bannock, the Coeur d'Alene, the Kootenai, and the Nez Perce, who call themselves the Nimiipuu.

When Spanish horses finally return to the continent and reach the Nez Perce, in the seventeen hundreds, they become master horse breeders. <gasp> They breed fast, tough horses, some covered in spots. Settlers later name them after the Palouse River. Appaloosas.

## Part 2: Trails

In the summer of eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark arrive in the mountains of Idaho, desperate for horses. Their guide, Sacagawea, was actually born here, in the Lemhi Valley, before she was kidnapped as a girl.

Then they try to cross the Bitterroot Mountains, on an old Nez Perce trail. <groan> It's the worst part of the whole journey. Snow. No game to hunt. They eat candles. They eat some of their horses.

## Part 2B

In September eighteen-oh-five, they stumble out of the mountains. They're freezing, starving, and sick.

<whispers> The Nez Perce could have wiped them out, and taken their guns.

According to Nez Perce tradition, an old woman named Watkuweis speaks up. Years before, she had been captured and taken east, and white people had treated her kindly. <short pause> "Do them no harm," she says.

So instead, the Nez Perce feed them, draw them maps, help them build canoes, and look after their horses all winter.

## Part 2C

Fur traders follow. In eighteen thirty-four, a trader builds Fort Hall, on the Snake River. Soon, it becomes a famous stop on the Oregon Trail. <sigh> Exhausted pioneers trade their worn-out oxen there for fresh ones.

<short pause> In eighteen thirty-six, missionaries Henry and Eliza Spalding build a mission among the Nez Perce, at Lapwai. They bring the first printing press to the Pacific Northwest.

<chuckle> And in eighteen thirty-seven, Henry Spalding plants something new in Idaho's soil. Potatoes.

## Part 3: Gold and Name

In eighteen-sixty, gold is found at Pierce, on Nez Perce land. Then in the Boise Basin. <gasp> Miners flood in by the thousands.

For a while, a gold camp called Idaho City is said to be bigger than Portland, the biggest city in the Northwest. Saloons, theatres, a jail, a newspaper, and dozens of fights.

<short pause> Thousands of Chinese miners come too, working the claims others have given up on. By eighteen seventy, almost a third of the people in Idaho Territory are Chinese.

<short pause> One of them is a young woman named Lalu Nathoy. Her family, starving in a drought in China, sells her. She's shipped to America, and sold again, to a saloon keeper in a mining camp.

<breath> Later, she marries a man named Charlie Bemis, and they homestead on a remote ranch beside the wild Salmon River. <chuckle> Legend says Charlie won her in a poker game. She always said that wasn't true. Polly Bemis lives there for decades, famous for her garden and her kindness. Her cabin is still there.

## Part 3B

Congress needs a name for this new territory.

A few years earlier, a lobbyist named George Willing had suggested "Idaho". He said it was a Native word meaning "gem of the mountains".

<tsk> It wasn't. <chuckle> He seems to have just made it up.

Congress almost used the name for Colorado, then found out. But by eighteen sixty-three, the name had stuck to a steamboat, a mining area and a county. So the new territory gets it anyway.

<laugh> Idaho. The Gem State. Named after a word that doesn't mean anything.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

In January eighteen sixty-three, near the Bear River, in what is now southeastern Idaho, U.S. soldiers attack a winter camp of Shoshone people at dawn, in the snow.

<breath> Hundreds are killed, including many women and children.

<long pause> It's one of the deadliest attacks on Native Americans in American history.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

In eighteen seventy-seven, the government orders the Nez Perce off most of their land, the same land where they once saved Lewis and Clark.

<breath> Some young men attack settlers. War follows.

Chief Joseph and his people flee across Idaho, Wyoming and Montana, more than a thousand miles. <long pause> They're caught forty miles from Canada.

## Part 4: Silver Wars

In eighteen-ninety, Idaho becomes the forty-third state.

In the north, in the Silver Valley, miners dig some of the richest silver mines in the country, deep underground. And they fight with the mine owners. Over pay. Over danger. Over unions.

<gasp> In eighteen ninety-nine, angry miners hijack a train, load it with dynamite, and blow up a mill. The governor declares martial law. Hundreds of miners are held for months in crowded pens called "bullpens".

## Part 4B

Years later, on a snowy December evening, that former governor, Frank Steunenberg, opens the gate to his front yard... <long pause> and a bomb kills him.

A drifter confesses. And he names the bosses of a big miners' union as the men who paid him.

<short pause> Their trial in Boise, in nineteen-oh-seven, becomes one of the most famous in America. The defence lawyer is the great Clarence Darrow. <chuckle> He talks to the jury for eleven hours. The union leader, Big Bill Haywood, walks free.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In August nineteen-ten, after a dry summer, a hurricane-force wind whips hundreds of small wildfires into one giant firestorm, across northern Idaho and Montana. The Big Burn.

<breath> In two days, three million acres of forest burn. At least eighty-five people die, most of them firefighters.

<long pause> A ranger named Ed Pulaski leads his crew of about forty men into an old mine tunnel, and holds them there at gunpoint, so they won't run out into the flames. Almost all of them survive.

Later, he invents a firefighting tool, half axe, half hoe. Firefighters still use it today. It's called a Pulaski.

## Part 5: Potatoes

Meanwhile, in the south, farmers dig canals from the Snake River, build dams, and turn the dry lava plain into farmland. <chuckle> And they plant potatoes.

Idaho's volcanic soil, warm days and cool nights are perfect. They grow a big, brown, fluffy potato called the Russet Burbank. <short pause> Bake it, mash it, fry it. Idaho potatoes become famous across America.

## Part 5B

In nineteen twenty-one, a fourteen-year-old farm boy named Philo Farnsworth is ploughing a potato field near Rigby, Idaho. He looks back at the straight rows he's made, line after line.

<gasp> And he has an idea. What if you could send a picture... line by line? Scanned by a beam of electrons.

He draws it on the blackboard for his high-school chemistry teacher. <short pause> Six years later, in San Francisco, he does it. The first all-electronic television.

## Part 5C

In the Second World War, a young Idaho potato farmer named J.R. Simplot dries millions of pounds of potatoes, and onions, for the troops.

After the war, his scientists figure out how to freeze French fries, so they taste good when you cook them later. <chuckle> Nobody wants them at first.

In the nineteen sixties, he makes a deal with Ray Kroc of McDonald's. On a handshake. <laugh> Simplot becomes one of McDonald's biggest French fry suppliers, and one of the richest men in America.

There's a decent chance your fries were Idaho potatoes.

## Part 5D

<chuckle> And in the Second World War, the Navy builds a giant training base in Idaho. Hundreds of miles from the ocean.

On the shore of Lake Pend Oreille, almost three hundred thousand sailors learn the basics before they ever see the sea. <short pause> The lake is so deep and quiet that, to this day, the Navy tests silent submarine designs there, using scaled-down models.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

During the war, about thirteen thousand Japanese Americans are forced into Minidoka, a camp of tar-paper barracks in the high desert of southern Idaho.

<breath> Dust storms in summer. Freezing winds in winter.

Hundreds of young men from the camp join the army, and fight in Europe, while their families stay behind barbed wire.

## Part 6: Atomic Desert

Now, the desert.

West of Idaho Falls, there's a huge, flat stretch of sagebrush and lava. Almost nobody lives there.

<gasp> In the Second World War, the Navy uses it to test-fire giant guns from its battleships. Shells fly across empty desert.

After the war, the government is looking for a place to test something far more dangerous. Nuclear reactors. <short pause> Somewhere very big, very empty, with plenty of water underneath.

The old Navy proving ground is perfect.

## Part 6B

On December twentieth, nineteen fifty-one, at a small reactor in the desert called EBR-One, scientists switch on something new.

<whispers> Four light bulbs glow.

It's the first time anyone has ever made usable electricity from nuclear power. <chuckle> The scientists chalk their names on the wall. The writing is still there.

And on July seventeenth, nineteen fifty-five, a reactor powers the whole nearby town of Arco. For about an hour, it becomes the first town in the world lit entirely by nuclear energy.

<short pause> Over the years, more than fifty reactors are built in that desert. The prototype engine for the world's first nuclear submarine, the Nautilus, is built and tested here, in the middle of the desert, inside a fake submarine hull, sitting in a giant tank of water.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

On January third, nineteen sixty-one, a small military reactor called SL-One explodes during maintenance.

<long pause> The three young men working on it are killed.

It remains the only deadly reactor accident in America.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

On June fifth, nineteen seventy-six, a brand-new dam on the Teton River, in eastern Idaho, springs a leak.

<breath> By late morning, the dam collapses. A wall of water rushes through the towns below. Houses float away.

<long pause> Eleven people die. Thousands lose their homes. The dam had only just been filled for the very first time.

## Part 6E

In nineteen sixty-nine, Apollo astronauts come to Idaho's Craters of the Moon, a field of black lava, to practise studying volcanic rock, before they go to the real Moon.

<chuckle> In nineteen seventy-four, the daredevil Evel Knievel tries to jump the Snake River Canyon on a rocket-powered motorcycle. His parachute opens too early. <laugh> He floats gently down into the canyon. He survives, with a broken nose.

## Part 6F

<short pause> In nineteen thirty-six, a railroad company builds a ski resort in Idaho. Sun Valley. <chuckle> Its engineer looks at machines used to load bananas onto ships, with hooks hanging from a moving cable, and swaps the hooks for chairs.

The world's first chairlift.

And in nineteen seventy-eight, four men start a computer chip company in the basement of a dentist's office in Boise. Micron. Today it's one of the biggest memory-chip makers on Earth.

<laugh> And in nineteen eighty-six, Boise State University puts down a bright blue football field. People still drive for hours just to see it.

<chuckle> And in two thousand four, a tiny movie filmed in Preston, Idaho, about an awkward high-school kid, becomes a cult hit. Tourists still turn up in Preston, looking for tater tots.

## Part 6G

So how does a quiet state of potato farms become the birthplace of nuclear power?

<whispers> It starts with a desert so empty the Navy used it for target practice.

Nobody lived there. Nobody farmed it. <short pause> And that emptiness was exactly what the atomic age needed.

The same land that was too dry and rocky for potatoes became the place where four light bulbs first glowed with the power of the atom.

<breath> Idaho's name may not mean anything.

<chuckle> But its desert lit up the world. And its potatoes fed it fries.
