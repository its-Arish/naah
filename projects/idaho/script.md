## Hook

About a third of America's potatoes come from Idaho.

The name "Idaho" was probably made up. A man claimed it meant "gem of the mountains". It doesn't mean anything at all.

And electronic television was dreamed up by a fourteen-year-old plowing a potato field in Idaho.

<long pause> In nineteen sixty-one, an experimental reactor in the Idaho desert exploded, killing three men. The only deadly reactor accident in American history.

And a little town in Idaho became the first in the world lit up entirely by nuclear power.

<short pause> So how does a quiet state of potato farms become the birthplace of nuclear power?

<whispers> It starts with a desert so empty the Navy used it for target practice.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Idaho is wild. Rugged mountains, deep canyons and fast rivers. Hells Canyon, on the Snake River, is deeper than the Grand Canyon.

In the south, the Snake River cuts across a giant plain of old lava. In the north, forests and lakes.

The land is home to the Shoshone, the Bannock, the Coeur d'Alene, the Kootenai, and the Nez Perce, who call themselves the Nimiipuu.

## Part 1B

In September eighteen-oh-five, Lewis and Clark stumble out of the Bitterroot Mountains. They're freezing, starving, and sick.

The Nez Perce could have wiped them out. Instead, they feed them, draw them maps, and look after their horses all winter.

## Part 2: Trails

Fur traders follow. Then the Oregon Trail, crossing southern Idaho along the Snake River. Fort Hall becomes a famous stop.

In eighteen-sixty, gold is found at Pierce, on Nez Perce land. Then in the Boise Basin. Miners flood in.

## Part 2B

Congress needs a name for this new territory.

A few years earlier, a lobbyist named George Willing had suggested "Idaho". He said it was a Native word meaning "gem of the mountains".

It wasn't. <chuckle> He seems to have just made it up.

Congress almost used the name for Colorado, then found out. But by eighteen sixty-three, the name had stuck to a steamboat, a mining area and a county. So the new territory gets it anyway.

Idaho. The Gem State. Named after a word that doesn't mean anything.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In January eighteen sixty-three, near the Bear River in what is now southeastern Idaho, U.S. soldiers attack a winter camp of Shoshone people.

Hundreds are killed, including many women and children. It's one of the deadliest attacks on Native Americans in American history.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

In eighteen seventy-seven, the government orders the Nez Perce off much of their land. Some young men attack settlers. War follows.

Chief Joseph and his people flee across Idaho, Wyoming and Montana. They're caught forty miles from Canada.

## Part 3: Silver Wars

In eighteen-ninety, Idaho becomes the forty-third state.

In the north, in the Silver Valley, miners dig some of the richest silver mines in the country. And they fight with the mine owners. Over pay. Over danger. Over unions.

In eighteen ninety-nine, angry miners hijack a train, load it with dynamite, and blow up a mill. The governor declares martial law. Hundreds of miners are held for months in pens called "bullpens".

Years later, that governor, Frank Steunenberg, opens his front gate... and a bomb kills him.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In August nineteen-ten, wildfires join together across northern Idaho and Montana. The Big Burn.

In two days, three million acres of forest burn. At least eighty-five people die, most of them firefighters.

## Part 4: Potatoes

Meanwhile, in the south, farmers dig canals from the Snake River and turn the lava plain into farmland. And they plant potatoes.

Idaho's volcanic soil, warm days and cool nights are perfect. Idaho potatoes become famous.

## Part 4B

In nineteen twenty-one, a fourteen-year-old farm boy named Philo Farnsworth is plowing a potato field near Rigby, Idaho. He looks back at the straight rows he's made, line after line.

And he has an idea. What if you could send a picture... line by line? Scanned by a beam of electrons.

Six years later, in San Francisco, he does it. The first all-electronic television.

## Part 4C

In the Second World War, a young Idaho potato farmer named J.R. Simplot dries millions of pounds of potatoes for the troops. After the war, his company figures out how to freeze French fries.

In the nineteen sixties, he makes a deal with Ray Kroc of McDonald's. On a handshake. Simplot becomes one of McDonald's biggest French fry suppliers.

<chuckle> There's a decent chance your fries were Idaho potatoes.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

During the war, Japanese American families are held at Minidoka, a camp in the high desert of southern Idaho.

## Part 5: Atomic Desert

Now, the desert.

West of Idaho Falls, there's a huge, flat stretch of sagebrush and lava. Almost nobody lives there.

In the Second World War, the Navy uses it to test-fire giant guns from its battleships. Shells fly across empty desert.

After the war, the government is looking for a place to test something far more dangerous. Nuclear reactors. Somewhere very big, very empty, with water underneath.

The old Navy proving ground is perfect.

## Part 5B

On December twentieth, nineteen fifty-one, at a small reactor in the desert called EBR-One, scientists switch on something new.

Four light bulbs glow.

It's the first time anyone has ever made usable electricity from nuclear power.

And on July seventeenth, nineteen fifty-five, a reactor powers the whole nearby town of Arco. For about an hour, it becomes the first town in the world lit entirely by nuclear energy.

Over the years, more than fifty reactors are built in that desert. The ones that power Navy submarines are tested here.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

On January third, nineteen sixty-one, a small military reactor called SL-One explodes during maintenance.

The three men working on it are killed. It remains the only deadly reactor accident in America.

## Part 6: Today

In nineteen sixty-nine, Apollo astronauts come to Idaho's Craters of the Moon, to practise studying volcanic rock, before they go to the real Moon.

In nineteen seventy-four, the daredevil Evel Knievel tries to jump the Snake River Canyon on a rocket-powered motorcycle. His parachute opens too early. <chuckle> He floats gently down into the canyon. He survives.

Sun Valley, in Idaho, built the world's first chairlift in nineteen thirty-six.

And in nineteen seventy-eight, four men start a computer chip company in the basement of a dentist's office in Boise. Micron. Today it's one of the biggest memory-chip makers on Earth.

## Part 6B

So how does a quiet state of potato farms become the birthplace of nuclear power?

It starts with a desert so empty the Navy used it for target practice.

Nobody lived there. Nobody farmed it. And that emptiness was exactly what the atomic age needed. The same land that was too dry and rocky for potatoes became the place where four light bulbs first glowed with the power of the atom.

<short pause> Idaho's name may not mean anything.

<chuckle> But its desert lit up the world. And its potatoes fed it fries.
