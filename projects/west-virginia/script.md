# The Entire History of West Virginia

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen twenty-one"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, deep, mountain-hollow storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Stubborn, independent mountain voice, a little defiant. |
| Part 3 | Hard and gritty, then grave. |
| Part 4 | Tense, rising rebellion, then steady. |
| Part 5 | Proud and wondering, a little eerie in places; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1750 · Western Virginia 1750 to 1861 · New State 1861 to 1865 · Coal Country 1865 to 1920 · Mine Wars 1920 to 1933 · Mountain State 1933 to now.

## Hook

Mother's Day? <short pause> Started in West Virginia. <chuckle> And the woman who started it spent the rest of her life trying to cancel it.

The Golden Delicious apple was found growing on a West Virginia hillside. And "Take Me Home, Country Roads" was written by people who'd never been to West Virginia.

West Virginia only exists because it split away from Virginia. In the middle of the Civil War.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-one, ten thousand armed coal miners fought a battle on a West Virginia mountain. Planes dropped bombs on them. The biggest armed uprising in America since the Civil War.

<short pause> So how does a state born to escape one war end up fighting another?

<whispers> It starts with a black rock that burns.

## Part 1: First Peoples

West Virginia is all mountains. Steep, folded, forested ridges, and deep, narrow valleys called hollows. <chuckle> It's the only state completely inside the Appalachian region. There's hardly a flat field anywhere.

<gasp> The New River, which carved its great gorge through these mountains, is thought to be one of the oldest rivers on Earth. It was flowing before the mountains rose around it.

Two thousand years ago, the Adena people build the Grave Creek Mound, one of the biggest burial mounds in North America. Sixty-two feet high.

Later, the Shawnee, the Cherokee, the Delaware and the Haudenosaunee hunt in these mountains.

## Part 1B

Under the mountains, there's something else. Thick seams of coal. <whispers> Black rock that burns.

For a long time, almost nobody cares. It's too hard to get out of the mountains.

<short pause> What early settlers want is salt. In the Kanawha Valley, salty water bubbles out of the ground. Settlers boil it down in big iron kettles. <chuckle> While drilling for salt water, they keep hitting something annoying. Natural gas. And oil. And they burn coal to boil the kettles.

## Part 2: Western Virginia

In the seventeen hundreds, settlers push west over the Blue Ridge into Virginia's mountains. Scots-Irish and German farmers. Small farms. Few enslaved people.

<short pause> A young surveyor named George Washington maps some of this land. He ends up owning tens of thousands of acres of it.

<gasp> In seventeen seventy-four, at Point Pleasant, where the Kanawha meets the Ohio, Virginia's militia fights a long, bloody battle against the Shawnee leader Cornstalk. <sigh> Three years later, Cornstalk comes to the fort there under a flag of truce. He's held hostage, and murdered by soldiers.

<short pause> On that frontier, a widow named Anne Bailey becomes a scout. <gasp> In seventeen ninety-one, when a fort runs out of gunpowder under attack, she rides alone, about a hundred miles through the forest, and brings back the powder. <chuckle> People call her "Mad Anne".

## Part 2B

But Virginia is run by rich planters in the east, with big plantations and thousands of enslaved people. <tsk> The planters control the government. The mountain people feel ignored and overtaxed.

<short pause> In the town of Malden, in the Kanawha Valley, after the Civil War, a young boy who was born into slavery works in the salt furnaces and the coal mines, and goes to school at night. <gasp> He grows up to be one of the most famous educators in America. Booker T. Washington.

## Part 2C

In October eighteen fifty-nine, John Brown and his followers raid the federal armoury at Harpers Ferry, then in Virginia, hoping to start a slave uprising.

<sigh> The first person they kill is Heyward Shepherd, a free Black man who worked at the train station.

Brown is captured and hanged. The country moves closer to war.

## Part 2D

In eighteen sixty-one, Virginia votes to leave the Union.

But the mountain counties in the northwest don't want to go. They meet in Wheeling, and say: if Virginia can leave the United States... we can leave Virginia.

They form their own loyal government. Then they ask to become a new state.

<chuckle> The Constitution says you can't split a state without its permission. So the loyal government of Virginia, which is basically them, gives permission to itself.

Lincoln isn't sure it's legal. But he needs them. <gasp> On June twentieth, eighteen sixty-three, West Virginia becomes the thirty-fifth state.

<short pause> The only state born by breaking away from a Confederate state. Its motto: "Mountaineers are always free."

<sigh> But the war still splits West Virginia's own families. Thousands of its men fight for the Confederacy.

## Part 3: Coal Country

After the war, railroads finally push into the mountains. <short pause> The legend of John Henry, the "steel-driving man" who races a steam drill with his hammer, and wins, and dies, is often set at a West Virginia railroad tunnel.

And now, the black rock can get out.

<groan> Coal companies buy up the mountains. They build whole towns. They own the houses. The stores. The churches. Even the money. Miners are paid in company scrip, which only works at the company store.

<tsk> Armed company guards patrol the towns. Anyone who talks about a union can be thrown out of their home.

<short pause> The coal camps fill with people from everywhere. Italians, Hungarians, Poles, Slovaks, and Black families from the South. <chuckle> Italian miners invent a lunch that won't spoil underground. A soft bread roll, baked with a stick of pepperoni inside. The pepperoni roll. Today, it's West Virginia's official state food.

<chuckle> Meanwhile, the Hatfields of West Virginia and the McCoys of Kentucky are busy feuding across the Tug Fork River. For decades.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

On December sixth, nineteen-oh-seven, two connected mines at Monongah, West Virginia, explode.

<breath> Many of the miners are immigrants, from Italy, Hungary, Poland.

<long pause> Three hundred and sixty-two men and boys are killed. It's the worst mining disaster in American history.

## Part 3C

The next year, in nineteen-oh-eight, in Grafton, not far away, a woman named Anna Jarvis holds a service to honour her late mother, who had cared for wounded soldiers on both sides of the war.

She campaigns for a national Mother's Day. In nineteen fourteen, she gets it.

<laugh> Then florists and card companies cash in. Anna Jarvis is furious. She spends the rest of her life trying to get Mother's Day abolished. She's even arrested for protesting.

## Part 3D

<chuckle> And in nineteen-oh-five, on a hillside farm in Clay County, a farmer finds a strange little apple tree that's sprung up by itself. Its apples are yellow, and very sweet.

He sends some to a big nursery company. <gasp> They pay him five thousand dollars for the tree, and build a steel cage around it with a burglar alarm, so nobody can steal cuttings. <short pause> The Golden Delicious. Today it's grown all over the world.

## Part 4: Mine Wars

Now, the war.

The miners want a union. Fair pay. Safer mines. Real money. The companies refuse. <sigh> And violence breaks out.

<short pause> A tiny, white-haired union organiser in her eighties, Mother Jones, travels from camp to camp, telling miners to stand up. <chuckle> A prosecutor calls her "the most dangerous woman in America".

In May nineteen twenty, in the town of Matewan, company detectives come to throw miners' families out of their homes. <gasp> The town's police chief, Sid Hatfield, stops them. A gunfight erupts in the street. Ten people are killed.

<breath> A year later, Sid Hatfield is shot dead on the steps of a courthouse.

## Part 4B

In August nineteen twenty-one, the miners have had enough. About ten thousand of them pick up rifles and march toward the coal fields of Logan County.

Many wear red bandanas around their necks, to know who's on their side. <short pause> Some say that's where the word "redneck" comes from.

Waiting for them on Blair Mountain are around three thousand armed deputies and company guards. With machine guns. <gasp> They hire private planes to drop homemade bombs on the miners.

For five days, the two sides fight along the ridges.

Then the U.S. Army arrives. <sigh> The miners won't fight the army. Many of them are veterans. They put down their guns and go home.

## Part 4C

The miners lose the battle. But twelve years later, in nineteen thirty-three, new federal laws give workers the right to join unions.

<gasp> Within months, West Virginia's miners sign up by the tens of thousands. The company towns begin to fade.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In the early nineteen-thirties, workers dig a tunnel through a mountain at Hawks Nest to carry water to a power plant.

<breath> The rock is full of silica. They aren't given masks.

<long pause> The dust destroys their lungs. Hundreds die. Most of them are Black workers who came for jobs.

## Part 5: Mountain State

West Virginia also gives America some legends.

<short pause> The writer Pearl Buck, the first American woman to win the Nobel Prize for Literature, is born in Hillsboro, West Virginia.

In nineteen forty-seven, a farm boy from Myra, West Virginia, Chuck Yeager, becomes the first pilot to fly faster than the speed of sound. <chuckle> Two nights before, he breaks two ribs falling off a horse. He's in so much pain that he can't close the plane's door. So a friend cuts him a piece of broomstick to lever it shut.

## Part 5B

Katherine Johnson, from White Sulphur Springs, is so brilliant that she starts high school at ten. <short pause> In nineteen thirty-nine, she's one of the first Black students at West Virginia University's graduate school. <gasp> She later calculates the paths that take NASA's astronauts into orbit and to the Moon. John Glenn refuses to fly until she checks the computer's numbers by hand.

<whispers> And under a grand old resort in White Sulphur Springs, the government secretly builds a giant bunker to shelter Congress in a nuclear war. Hidden for thirty years, until a reporter reveals it in nineteen ninety-two.

## Part 5C

<chuckle> In the town of Green Bank, there's a giant radio telescope, listening to the faint signals of space. To keep things quiet, the area around it is a National Radio Quiet Zone. <laugh> Wi-Fi and microwave ovens are restricted. Some people who say they're allergic to radio waves move there on purpose.

## Part 5D

<whispers> In November nineteen sixty-six, near Point Pleasant, two young couples say they saw a huge, grey, winged creature with glowing red eyes, chasing their car. <chuckle> The Mothman. Today, Point Pleasant has a shiny metal Mothman statue, and a Mothman festival.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

<breath> Thirteen months later, at rush hour, the Silver Bridge over the Ohio River at Point Pleasant collapses.

<long pause> Forty-six people are killed.

## Part 5F

In nineteen seventy-one, a songwriting couple driving to a family gathering in Maryland, on country roads, starts writing a song. <chuckle> They swap in West Virginia because it sounds better. They'd never been there.

"Take Me Home, Country Roads" becomes West Virginia's unofficial anthem. Today, it's one of its official state songs. <laugh> At West Virginia University football games, the whole stadium sings it.

<chuckle> A kid from the little coal town of Chelyan, Jerry West, becomes a basketball legend. Many people believe the silhouette in the NBA's logo is him. <short pause> And a tiny gymnast from Fairmont, Mary Lou Retton, wins the Olympic all-around gold in nineteen eighty-four.

## Part 5G (quiet style)

In February nineteen seventy-two, a coal-waste dam breaks at Buffalo Creek. A wall of black water rushes down the hollow.

<long pause> A hundred and twenty-five people are killed.

<breath> In April twenty ten, an explosion at the Upper Big Branch mine kills twenty-nine miners.

## Part 5H

Today, there's less coal mining than there used to be. <short pause> And West Virginia is betting on its mountains instead. In twenty twenty, the New River Gorge becomes a national park.

<chuckle> In Charleston, West Virginia's State Capitol has a golden dome that's a few feet taller than the one on the U.S. Capitol in Washington.

<gasp> Across the gorge stands a steel arch bridge, almost nine hundred feet above the river. <laugh> Once a year, on Bridge Day, the road is closed, and hundreds of people jump off it with parachutes.

## Part 5I

So how does a state born to escape one war end up fighting another?

<whispers> It starts with a black rock that burns.

The mountain people left Virginia because rich planters ran everything. But once the railroads reached the coal, rich companies ran everything instead. <short pause> The towns. The stores. The money. So the miners fought back, all the way to the top of Blair Mountain.

<breath> West Virginia was born saying "nobody runs us from far away". And it kept saying it.

<chuckle> Almost heaven, they call it. Even if the songwriters had to find that out later.
