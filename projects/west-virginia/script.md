## Hook

Mother's Day? <short pause> Started in West Virginia. And the woman who started it spent the rest of her life trying to cancel it.

The Golden Delicious apple was found growing on a West Virginia hillside.

"Take Me Home, Country Roads" was written by people who'd never been to West Virginia.

And West Virginia only exists because it split away from Virginia. In the middle of the Civil War.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-one, ten thousand armed coal miners fought a battle on a West Virginia mountain. Planes dropped bombs on them. The biggest armed uprising in America since the Civil War.

<short pause> So how does a state born to escape one war end up fighting another?

<whispers> It starts with a black rock that burns.

## Part 1: First Peoples

West Virginia is all mountains. Steep, folded, forested ridges, and deep, narrow valleys. It's the only state completely inside the Appalachian region.

Two thousand years ago, the Adena people build the Grave Creek Mound, one of the biggest burial mounds in North America.

Later, the Shawnee, the Cherokee, the Delaware and the Haudenosaunee hunt in these mountains.

## Part 1B

Under the mountains, there's something else. Thick seams of coal. Black rock that burns.

For a long time, almost nobody cares. It's too hard to get out of the mountains.

## Part 2: Western Virginia

In the seventeen hundreds, settlers push west over the Blue Ridge into Virginia's mountains. Scots-Irish and German farmers. Small farms. Few enslaved people.

But Virginia is run by rich planters in the east, with big plantations and thousands of enslaved people. The planters control the government. The mountain people feel ignored and overtaxed.

## Part 2B

In October eighteen fifty-nine, John Brown and his followers raid the federal armoury at Harpers Ferry, then in Virginia, hoping to start a slave uprising. He's captured and hanged. The country moves closer to war.

## Part 2C

In eighteen sixty-one, Virginia votes to leave the Union.

But the mountain counties in the northwest don't want to go. They meet in Wheeling, and say: if Virginia can leave the United States... we can leave Virginia.

They form their own loyal government. Then they ask to become a new state.

<chuckle> The Constitution says you can't split a state without its permission. So the loyal government of Virginia, which is basically them, gives permission to itself.

Lincoln isn't sure it's legal. But he needs them. On June twentieth, eighteen sixty-three, West Virginia becomes the thirty-fifth state.

The only state born by breaking away from a Confederate state.

## Part 3: Coal Country

After the war, railroads finally push into the mountains. The legend of John Henry, the "steel-driving man" who races a steam drill with his hammer, and wins, and dies, is often set at a West Virginia railroad tunnel.

And now, the black rock can get out.

Coal companies buy up the mountains. They build whole towns. They own the houses. The stores. The churches. Even the money. Miners are paid in company scrip, which only works at the company store.

Armed company guards patrol the towns. Anyone who talks about a union can be thrown out of their home.

<chuckle> Meanwhile, the Hatfields of West Virginia and the McCoys of Kentucky are busy feuding across the Tug Fork River. For decades.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

On December sixth, nineteen-oh-seven, two connected mines at Monongah, West Virginia, explode.

Three hundred and sixty-two men and boys are killed. It's the worst mining disaster in American history.

## Part 3C

The next year, in nineteen-oh-eight, in Grafton, not far away, a woman named Anna Jarvis holds a service to honour her late mother, who had cared for wounded soldiers on both sides of the war.

She campaigns for a national Mother's Day. In nineteen fourteen, she gets it.

<chuckle> Then florists and card companies cash in. Anna Jarvis is furious. She spends the rest of her life trying to get Mother's Day abolished. She's even arrested for protesting.

## Part 4: Mine Wars

Now, the war.

The miners want a union. Fair pay. Safer mines. Real money. The companies refuse. And violence breaks out.

In May nineteen twenty, in the town of Matewan, company detectives come to throw miners' families out of their homes. The town's police chief, Sid Hatfield, stops them. A gunfight erupts. Ten people are killed.

A year later, Sid Hatfield is shot dead on the steps of a courthouse.

## Part 4B

In August nineteen twenty-one, the miners have had enough. About ten thousand of them pick up rifles and march toward the coal fields of Logan County.

Many wear red bandanas around their necks, to know who's on their side.

Waiting for them on Blair Mountain are around three thousand armed deputies and company guards. With machine guns. They hire private planes to drop homemade bombs on the miners.

For five days, the two sides fight along the ridges.

Then the U.S. Army arrives. The miners won't fight the army. Many of them are veterans. They put down their guns and go home.

## Part 4C

The miners lose the battle. But twelve years later, in nineteen thirty-three, new federal laws give workers the right to join unions.

Within months, West Virginia's miners sign up by the tens of thousands. The company towns begin to fade.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In the early nineteen-thirties, workers dig a tunnel through a mountain at Hawks Nest to carry water to a power plant. The rock is full of silica. They aren't given masks.

The dust destroys their lungs. Hundreds die. Most of them are Black workers who came for jobs.

## Part 5: Mountain State

West Virginia also gives America some legends.

In nineteen forty-seven, a farm boy from Myra, West Virginia, Chuck Yeager, becomes the first pilot to fly faster than the speed of sound.

Katherine Johnson, from White Sulphur Springs, calculates the paths that take NASA's astronauts into orbit and to the Moon.

And under a grand old resort in White Sulphur Springs, the government secretly builds a giant bunker to shelter Congress in a nuclear war. Hidden for thirty years, until a reporter reveals it in nineteen ninety-two.

## Part 5B

In nineteen seventy-one, a songwriting couple driving to a family gathering in Maryland, on country roads, starts writing a song. They swap in West Virginia because it sounds better. They'd never been there.

"Take Me Home, Country Roads" becomes West Virginia's unofficial anthem. Today, it's one of its official state songs.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In April twenty ten, an explosion at the Upper Big Branch mine kills twenty-nine miners.

## Part 5D

Today, there's less coal mining than there used to be. And West Virginia is betting on its mountains instead. In twenty twenty, the New River Gorge becomes a national park.

## Part 5E

So how does a state born to escape one war end up fighting another?

It starts with a black rock that burns.

The mountain people left Virginia because rich planters ran everything. But once the railroads reached the coal, rich companies ran everything instead. The towns. The stores. The money. So the miners fought back, all the way to the top of Blair Mountain.

<short pause> West Virginia was born saying "nobody runs us from far away". And it kept saying it.

<chuckle> Almost heaven, they call it. Even if the songwriters had to find that out later.
