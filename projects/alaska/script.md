## Hook

Balto, the hero sled dog? He has a statue in Central Park. <short pause> But the dog who ran the longest, most dangerous part of that race... was Togo.

Alaska is bigger than Texas, California and Montana put together.

It's the farthest north state. The farthest west. And, technically... the farthest EAST.

And every year, Alaska sends almost everyone who lives there a check. Just for living there.

<long pause> Yet in eighteen sixty-seven, Russia sold the whole thing for about two cents an acre. And the people who actually lived there? Nobody asked them.

<short pause> So how does a frozen colony sold for pocket change become a place that pays you to live in it?

<whispers> It starts with a fur coat.

## Part 1: First Alaskans

Twenty thousand years ago, so much water is locked in ice that the sea drops, and a land bridge appears between Asia and Alaska. A thousand miles wide. Scientists call it Beringia.

Mammoths walk across it. And so do people.

Alaska is the doorway to the Americas. Most of the first people of North and South America came this way.

The people who stay build some of the cleverest ways of life on Earth.

In the far north, the Iñupiat hunt whales from skin boats in the Arctic Ocean. In the southwest, the Yup'ik live in the great river deltas. On the Aleutian Islands, the Unangan build kayaks so fast and so well made that Europeans later copied them.

Inland, Athabascan peoples follow caribou and salmon. And in the rainforests of the southeast, the Tlingit and Haida carve great cedar totem poles and grow rich through trade.

For thousands of years, this is Alaska. Not empty. Home.

## Part 2: The Fur Coat

In seventeen forty-one, a Danish captain sailing for Russia, Vitus Bering, reaches the Alaskan coast.

His ship wrecks on the way home. Bering dies. But the survivors bring something back to Russia that changes everything.

Sea otter furs.

The sea otter has the thickest fur of any animal. Up to a million hairs in a single square inch. In China, rich buyers will pay a fortune for one coat.

So Russian fur hunters pour east across the islands.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The Unangan are the best sea otter hunters in the world. So the Russians take hostages, and force the men to hunt.

Violence and new diseases devastate the islands. Within a few generations, the Unangan population falls by most estimates to a fraction of what it was.

And the sea otters are hunted almost to extinction.

## Part 2C

In seventeen ninety-nine, the Tsar creates the Russian-American Company. Its capital is Sitka, on the southeast coast.

For a while, Sitka is the biggest town on the whole Pacific coast of North America. Bigger than San Francisco. Onion-domed churches. A governor's castle on a hill.

But by the eighteen fifties, the otters are nearly gone, the company is losing money, and Russia has just lost a war with Britain. Russia's leaders worry that Britain could simply take Alaska from Canada next door.

Better to sell it. To someone Britain can't push around.

## Part 3: The Purchase

On March thirtieth, eighteen sixty-seven, after an all-night negotiation, the American Secretary of State, William Seward, signs the deal.

Seven point two million dollars. For more than half a million square miles.

About two cents an acre.

You may have heard people laughed at it. Seward's Folly. <chuckle> Actually, most newspapers at the time thought it was a good deal. The Folly story got much bigger later.

On October eighteenth, eighteen sixty-seven, in Sitka, the Russian flag comes down and the American flag goes up. Alaskans still celebrate that day.

But the Tlingit, the Iñupiat, the Yup'ik, the Unangan... nobody asked them. As many of them put it, Russia sold something it never really owned.

## Part 4: Gold

For thirty years, America mostly ignores Alaska.

Then, in eighteen ninety-six, gold is found in the Klondike, just over the border in Canada. And the only practical way to get there is through Alaska.

A hundred thousand people set out. Gold fever.

They land at Skagway, a mud town that goes from almost nothing to thousands of people in months. Then they climb the Chilkoot Pass, an icy staircase of steps carved in the snow, carrying a year's worth of food each. Canada won't let you in without it.

Most of them never find any gold.

Then gold turns up in Alaska itself. On the beaches at Nome, people literally dig it out of the sand. Then in Fairbanks. Then Juneau, which becomes the capital.

Alaska paid for itself in gold. Many times over.

## Part 4B

And in January nineteen twenty-five, Nome needs something more precious than gold.

A deadly disease, diphtheria, breaks out among the children. The only medicine is a thousand miles away. No roads. The planes can't fly in the cold.

So they send it by dog sled. A relay of twenty mushers and more than a hundred and fifty dogs, through blizzards, at temperatures far below zero.

The longest and most dangerous stretch goes to Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog, Togo. Hundreds of miles, including a crossing of the cracking sea ice of Norton Sound.

The final leg goes to Gunnar Kaasen and his lead dog, Balto. Balto runs into Nome. Balto gets the headlines. Balto gets the statue in Central Park.

<chuckle> Togo got a movie. Eventually.

The trail they ran is now the route of the Iditarod, the most famous sled dog race in the world.

## Part 5: War

In June nineteen forty-two, Japan does something people forget. It invades Alaska.

Japanese troops land on the islands of Attu and Kiska, at the far end of the Aleutian chain. It's the only time in the war that enemy troops occupy American soil in North America.

In May nineteen forty-three, American soldiers retake Attu, in brutal fighting in fog, mud and snow.

And to get supplies north, the army builds the Alaska Highway. More than fifteen hundred miles, through mountains and forest, in about eight months. Thousands of the builders are Black soldiers, whose work was barely recognised for decades.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

During the war, the American government also removes the Unangan people from their own islands, to "keep them safe".

They're put in abandoned canneries and mines in southeast Alaska. Cold. Crowded. Little medicine.

Around one in ten of them die.

In nineteen eighty-eight, Congress apologised, and paid the survivors.

## Part 5C

And in nineteen forty-five, a Tlingit woman named Elizabeth Peratrovich stands up in Alaska's territorial Senate.

Stores in Alaska still hang signs saying "No Natives Allowed". She tells the senators that people who have five thousand years of history here shouldn't have to fight for equal rights.

The Senate passes Alaska's Equal Rights Act. It's one of the first anti-discrimination laws in the United States. Nearly twenty years before the national Civil Rights Act.

## Part 6: Statehood

On January third, nineteen fifty-nine, Alaska becomes the forty-ninth state.

Then, on Good Friday, nineteen sixty-four, the ground shakes. For four and a half minutes.

It's a magnitude nine point two. The most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America. Whole neighbourhoods of Anchorage slide toward the sea. Tsunamis hit towns all along the coast.

One hundred and thirty-one people die.

And then, in nineteen sixty-eight... oil.

At Prudhoe Bay, on the Arctic coast, they find the biggest oil field in North America.

But who owns the land? Alaska Native peoples have never signed it away. So in nineteen seventy-one, Congress passes a historic settlement. Alaska Natives receive forty-four million acres and nearly a billion dollars, managed by Native-owned corporations.

Then comes an eight-hundred-mile pipeline, from the Arctic Ocean to the port of Valdez. Finished in nineteen seventy-seven.

## Part 6B

And Alaska does something unusual with its oil money.

It saves it. In a giant fund. The Alaska Permanent Fund.

Since nineteen eighty-two, almost every Alaskan, adult and child, gets a share of the earnings every year. The Permanent Fund Dividend. A check for living in Alaska.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In March nineteen eighty-nine, the tanker Exxon Valdez hits a reef in Prince William Sound.

About eleven million gallons of oil pour into the sea.

Hundreds of thousands of seabirds die. Thousands of sea otters.

The coast is still recovering.

## Part 7: Today

Today, Alaska still has more coastline than all the other states put together. More than three million lakes. The tallest mountain in North America.

Fewer than a million people. And about one in five of them are Alaska Native.

The sea otters came back too. Protected since nineteen eleven, they've returned to much of the coast.

## Part 7B

So how does a frozen colony sold for pocket change become a place that pays you to live in it?

It starts with a fur coat. The otter fur drew Russia across the sea. When the otters ran out, Russia sold. Then came the gold, which paid the price back many times. And then the oil, which pays Alaskans every single year.

<short pause> But Alaska was never really empty, and never really cheap. It was home for fifteen thousand years before anyone put a price on it.

<chuckle> And if you're ever in Central Park, say hi to Balto. Then whisper... Togo did the hard part.
