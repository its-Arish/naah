# The Entire History of Alaska

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,700 spoken words, so roughly 19 to 21 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Curious, playful, quick for the first four facts; slower and serious from "Yet in eighteen sixty-seven"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller in a vast cold landscape. |
| Part 2 | Adventurous, then brisk and wry, then serious. |
| Part 3 | Clever and amused, like explaining a strange real-estate deal. |
| Part 4 | Excited, gold-fever energy, then tense and heroic for the serum run. |
| Part 5 | Tense and dramatic war-documentary voice. |
| Part 6 | Big, rumbling, then amazed and proud. |
| Part 7 | Bright, warm, certain, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Alaskans 13,000 BC to 1741 · Russian America 1741 to 1867 · Purchase 1867 · Gold 1896 to 1912 · Serum Run 1925 · War 1942 to 1945 · Statehood 1959 · Earthquake 1964 · Oil 1968 to 1989 · Today 1990 to now.

## Hook

Balto, the hero sled dog? <short pause> He has a statue in Central Park. But the dog who ran the longest, most dangerous part of that race... was Togo.

Alaska is bigger than Texas, California and Montana put together.

It's the farthest north state. The farthest west. And, technically... <chuckle> the farthest EAST.

And every year, Alaska sends almost everyone who lives there a check. Just for living there.

<long pause> Yet in eighteen sixty-seven, Russia sold the whole thing for about two cents an acre. And the people who actually lived there? Nobody asked them.

<short pause> So how does a frozen colony sold for pocket change become a place that pays you to live in it?

<whispers> It starts with a fur coat.

## Part 1: First Alaskans

Twenty thousand years ago, so much water is locked in ice that the sea drops. <short pause> And a land bridge appears between Asia and Alaska. A thousand miles wide. Scientists call it Beringia.

It isn't a bridge, really. It's a whole country of grass, with mammoths, giant bison, wild horses and lions roaming across it.

<breath> And people.

Alaska is the doorway to the Americas. Most of the first people of North and South America came this way.

<breath> Then the ice melts. Around eleven thousand years ago, the sea rises and swallows the land bridge. Asia and America are split apart again, by fifty-five miles of water. <short pause> The Bering Strait.

<short pause> The people who stay build some of the cleverest ways of life on Earth.

## Part 1B

In the far north, the Iñupiat hunt bowhead whales from skin boats in the Arctic Ocean. A single whale can feed a village through the winter. To celebrate, they toss people high into the air from a walrus-skin blanket. <chuckle> The blanket toss. It's still done today.

<short pause> And those bowhead whales? <gasp> They can live for more than two hundred years. In two thousand seven, Iñupiat whalers caught a bowhead with an old harpoon tip still buried in its blubber. It had been made in the eighteen-eighties. That whale had been swimming since before the Civil War.

In the southwest, the Yup'ik live in the great river deltas, and carve masks for winter dances.

On the Aleutian Islands, the Unangan build kayaks so fast, light and well-made that Europeans later copied them. They make waterproof parkas out of seal gut. <short pause> Rain jackets, thousands of years before plastic.

Inland, Athabascan peoples follow caribou and salmon along the great rivers, the Yukon and the Tanana.

And in the rainforests of the southeast, the Tlingit and Haida carve great cedar totem poles, weave blankets in bold black and yellow, and grow rich through trade.

For thousands of years, this is Alaska. <short pause> Not empty. Home.

<breath> Even the name comes from them. The Unangan word Alaxsxaq. Roughly, the mainland. The place the sea crashes against.

## Part 2: The Fur Coat

In seventeen forty-one, a Danish captain sailing for Russia, Vitus Bering, reaches the Alaskan coast.

<sigh> His ship wrecks on the way home, on a bare island in the freezing sea. Bering dies there. The survivors spend the winter in holes dug in the sand, eating whatever they can catch.

<short pause> But in the spring, they build a little boat from the wreck, and sail home. And they bring something back to Russia that changes everything.

<long pause> Sea otter furs.

## Part 2B

The sea otter has the thickest fur of any animal on Earth. <gasp> Up to a million hairs in a single square inch. More than on your whole head.

In China, rich buyers will pay a fortune for one coat.

So Russian fur hunters, called promyshlenniki, pour east across the Aleutian Islands in small, leaky boats.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

The Unangan are the best sea otter hunters in the world. So the Russians take hostages, often women and children, and force the men to hunt.

<breath> Violence and new diseases devastate the islands. Within a few generations, by most estimates, the Unangan population falls to a fraction of what it was.

The sea otters are hunted almost to extinction.

<long pause> And on the island where Bering died, the survivors had found a gentle giant. A sea cow, as long as a bus, grazing on seaweed. Hunters kill it for meat. Within twenty-seven years of its discovery, the Steller's sea cow is gone. Forever.

## Part 2D

In seventeen ninety-nine, the Tsar creates the Russian-American Company, to run Alaska like a business.

But the Tlingit are not easily pushed around. In eighteen-oh-two, Tlingit warriors destroy the first Russian fort near Sitka.

<short pause> In eighteen-oh-four, the Russians come back with a warship and cannons. After days of fighting, the Tlingit slip away through the forest at night, and the Russians build a new capital. New Archangel. Today, Sitka.

For a while, Sitka is the biggest town on the whole Pacific coast of North America. <chuckle> Bigger than San Francisco. Onion-domed churches. A governor's castle on a hill. Ballrooms, with Russian officers dancing at the edge of the wilderness.

Russia even builds a fort in California, north of San Francisco, Fort Ross, to grow food for Alaska. <tsk> The farming goes badly.

## Part 2E

Russia also brings its church.

A priest named Ioann Veniaminov spends years on the Aleutian Islands and in Sitka. He learns Unangan, builds a church with his own hands, and creates an alphabet so people can read and write in their own language. Later, he does the same for Tlingit.

<short pause> The fur traders left. The church stayed. Today, there are still Russian Orthodox churches with onion domes in Native villages all over Alaska.

## Part 2F

By the eighteen fifties, the otters are nearly gone. The company is losing money. And Russia has just lost the Crimean War against Britain and France.

Russia's leaders look at the map and get nervous. Britain owns Canada, right next door. If war comes again, Britain could simply take Alaska. And Russia could never defend it.

<short pause> Better to sell it. To someone Britain can't push around.

## Part 3: The Purchase

On March thirtieth, eighteen sixty-seven, after an all-night negotiation, the American Secretary of State, William Seward, signs the deal.

Seven point two million dollars. For more than half a million square miles.

<gasp> About two cents an acre.

You may have heard people laughed at it. Seward's Folly. Seward's Icebox. <chuckle> Actually, most newspapers at the time thought it was a good deal. The Folly story got much bigger later.

<short pause> On October eighteenth, eighteen sixty-seven, in Sitka, the Russian flag comes down and the American flag goes up. Alaskans still celebrate that day.

<chuckle> And something strange happens to the calendar. Russia used an older calendar, and Alaska was on the other side of the date line. So when Alaska switches to the American calendar and date, the people of Sitka live through Friday... and then wake up to another Friday.

## Part 3B

But the Tlingit, the Iñupiat, the Yup'ik, the Unangan, the Athabascans... <long pause> nobody asked them.

Tlingit leaders protest that the Russians never owned their land to sell. If anyone should be paid, they say, it's them.

<short pause> It will take more than a hundred years for anyone in Washington to really answer that question.

## Part 3C

For a while, the main Americans in Alaska are whalers. Hundreds of ships sail into the Arctic every summer, chasing bowheads for oil and baleen.

<gasp> In September eighteen seventy-one, the ice closes in early. Thirty-three whaling ships are trapped along the Arctic coast, being slowly crushed.

<short pause> More than twelve hundred people are on board. Including wives and children.

So they abandon the ships. All of them. They pile into small whaleboats and sail and row along the coast, between the ice and the shore, for days, to reach a few rescue ships waiting in open water.

<phew> Every single person survives. The ships are lost.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

Later, missionaries and the government open boarding schools for Native children.

<breath> Children are sent far from their families. Many are punished for speaking their own languages.

Some of those languages now have only a handful of speakers left.

## Part 3E

But Alaska Natives organise. In nineteen twelve, Tlingit and Haida men found the Alaska Native Brotherhood, and soon a Sisterhood. They fight for citizenship, for the vote, and for their land.

One of them, a Tlingit man named William Paul, becomes the first Alaska Native lawyer, and the first Alaska Native elected to the territory's legislature.

<short pause> They're the reason the land question never goes away.

## Part 4: Gold

For thirty years, America mostly ignores Alaska. The army runs it. Then the navy. Then almost nobody.

Then, in eighteen ninety-six, gold is found in the Klondike, just over the border in Canada. <gasp> And the only practical way to get there is through Alaska.

A hundred thousand people set out. Gold fever.

They land at Skagway, a mud town that goes from almost nothing to thousands of people in months. <chuckle> It's run, for a while, by a con man called Soapy Smith, whose gang cheats newcomers out of their money with fake telegraph offices and crooked card games. Until he's shot dead in a gunfight on the wharf.

## Part 4B

From the coast, the stampeders climb the Chilkoot Pass. An icy staircase of steps carved in the snow, so steep that people climb it bent over, in one long line.

Canada won't let anyone in without a year's worth of supplies. About a ton each. So they climb it again and again, carrying one load at a time. <groan> Some make the climb thirty or forty times.

Of the hundred thousand who set out, only about a third ever reach the gold fields. Most of them never find any gold at all.

## Part 4C

Then gold turns up in Alaska itself.

In eighteen ninety-nine, on the beaches at Nome, people find gold in the sand. <laugh> They dig it out with shovels, right on the beach, with the sea washing in. Within a year, Nome is the biggest city in Alaska.

Then gold in Fairbanks. Then Juneau, which becomes the capital.

<short pause> Alaska paid back its price in gold. Many times over.

## Part 4D

And in January nineteen twenty-five, Nome needs something more precious than gold.

<breath> A deadly disease, diphtheria, breaks out among the children. The town's doctor has only old, expired medicine. The fresh medicine is in Anchorage, roughly a thousand miles away. There are no roads. Planes can't fly in the cold.

So the medicine goes by train as far as the tracks go, to the town of Nenana. And from there... <short pause> by dog sled.

A relay of twenty mushers and more than a hundred and fifty dogs. Six hundred and seventy-four miles. Through blizzards, at fifty below zero. In five and a half days.

## Part 4E

The longest and most dangerous stretch goes to a Norwegian musher named Leonhard Seppala and his lead dog, Togo. <gasp> Togo is twelve years old.

They run more than two hundred and sixty miles, including a shortcut across the cracking sea ice of Norton Sound. If the ice breaks up, the whole team drifts out to sea.

It holds. <phew> Just.

The final leg goes to Gunnar Kaasen and his lead dog, Balto. In a whiteout, Kaasen can barely see his own dogs. Balto finds the trail. They run into Nome before dawn.

Balto gets the headlines. Balto gets the statue in Central Park.

<chuckle> Togo got a movie. Eventually.

The trail they ran is now the route of the Iditarod, the most famous sled dog race in the world.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

Seven years before the serum run, in nineteen eighteen, the world flu pandemic reaches Alaska.

<breath> It sweeps through Native villages with terrible speed. In the little village of Brevig Mission, seventy-two of the eighty adults die in five days.

<long pause> They're buried in a mass grave, in the frozen ground.

Almost eighty years later, a scientist digs carefully into that frozen grave. The permafrost has preserved a sample of the virus. It helps scientists finally understand the deadliest flu in history.

## Part 4G

And Alaska has the tallest mountain in North America. Twenty thousand, three hundred and ten feet. Its Koyukon Athabascan name is Denali. The high one.

<chuckle> In nineteen ten, four miners from Fairbanks decide to climb it, on a bet. They carry doughnuts, coffee... and a fourteen-foot spruce pole. Two of them reach the top of the lower north peak, and plant the pole, so people in town can see it with a telescope.

<short pause> Nobody believes them.

In nineteen thirteen, a real expedition reaches the true summit. And the first person to stand on top of the highest point in North America is a young Koyukon Athabascan man. Walter Harper.

<gasp> From the top, they can see the miners' spruce pole. Still standing, on the north peak.

## Part 5: War

In June nineteen forty-two, Japan does something most people forget. <short pause> It attacks Alaska.

Japanese planes bomb the navy base at Dutch Harbor. Then Japanese troops land on the islands of Attu and Kiska, at the far end of the Aleutian chain.

It's the only time in the war that enemy troops occupy American soil in North America.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In May nineteen forty-three, American soldiers land on Attu to take it back. They fight in fog, mud and freezing wind. Many soldiers suffer frostbite, in boots made for warmer places.

<breath> Near the end, the Japanese soldiers who are left make a final charge. Almost all of the Japanese garrison is killed. Very few surrender.

<long pause> Hundreds of American soldiers die too.

## Part 5C

Then the Allies prepare to invade Kiska. Thirty-four thousand troops land in August nineteen forty-three... <short pause> and find it empty.

<sigh> The Japanese had secretly slipped away weeks earlier, in the fog. In the fog and confusion, nervous soldiers fire at each other. A ship hits a mine. Nearly a hundred men are killed, on an island with no enemy.

## Part 5D

To get supplies north, the army builds the Alaska Highway. More than fifteen hundred miles, through mountains, swamp and frozen forest, in about eight months.

<short pause> Thousands of the builders are Black soldiers, sent north in a segregated army, whose work was barely recognised for decades.

<short pause> A general had warned about this years earlier. Billy Mitchell told Congress: whoever holds Alaska will hold the world. Now everyone understands. Alaska sits right between America, Russia and Asia.

In the Cold War, radar stations line the Arctic coast, watching for Soviet bombers coming over the North Pole.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

During the war, the American government also removes the Unangan people from their own islands, to "keep them safe".

<breath> They're put in abandoned canneries and mines in southeast Alaska. Cold. Crowded. Little medicine.

<long pause> Around one in ten of them die.

When they're allowed home, many find their houses looted by soldiers.

In nineteen eighty-eight, Congress apologised, and paid the survivors.

## Part 5F

And in nineteen forty-five, a Tlingit woman named Elizabeth Peratrovich stands up in Alaska's territorial Senate.

Stores in Alaska still hang signs saying "No Natives Allowed".

One senator has just sneered: who are these people, barely out of savagery, to ask to mix with whites, who have five thousand years of civilisation behind them?

<short pause> Elizabeth Peratrovich rises. And she turns it around. She says she never expected that she, barely out of savagery, would have to remind gentlemen with five thousand years of civilisation behind them... about the Bill of Rights.

<gasp> The gallery bursts into applause.

The Senate passes Alaska's Equal Rights Act. It's one of the first anti-discrimination laws in the United States. Nearly twenty years before the national Civil Rights Act.

Today, Alaska celebrates Elizabeth Peratrovich Day. And her face is on a U.S. dollar coin.

## Part 6: Statehood

On January third, nineteen fifty-nine, Alaska becomes the forty-ninth state.

<short pause> Getting there takes a fight. Congress keeps saying Alaska is too far, too empty, too cold.

So in nineteen fifty-five, Alaskans gather at the university in Fairbanks and write a state constitution anyway. <chuckle> Then they elect two senators and a congressman, and send them to Washington, even though Congress hasn't let them in yet. They knock on doors. They make speeches. They won't go away.

<short pause> It works.

<chuckle> Texas is no longer the biggest. Alaskans love reminding Texans that if you cut Alaska in half, Texas would be the third biggest state.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-three, mushers run the first full Iditarod, from Anchorage to Nome, following the old serum trail. It takes the winner twenty days.

In nineteen eighty-five, Libby Riddles drives her team through a blizzard that stops everyone else, and becomes the first woman to win. Then Susan Butcher wins it four times.

<laugh> Alaskans start selling T-shirts: "Alaska. Where men are men... and women win the Iditarod."

## Part 6B (quiet style)

Then, on Good Friday, nineteen sixty-four, the ground shakes.

<long pause> For four and a half minutes.

It's a magnitude nine point two. The most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America. Whole neighbourhoods of Anchorage slide toward the sea. Tsunamis hit towns all along the coast, and race all the way to California.

<breath> One hundred and thirty-one people die.

## Part 6C

And then, in nineteen sixty-eight... <short pause> oil.

At Prudhoe Bay, on the Arctic coast, they find the biggest oil field in North America.

But there's a problem. Who owns the land? Alaska Native peoples have never signed it away. No treaties. No sale.

<short pause> Remember the question from eighteen sixty-seven? Here's the answer.

In nineteen seventy-one, Congress passes a historic settlement. Alaska Natives receive forty-four million acres and nearly a billion dollars. Instead of reservations, the land and money go to new Native-owned corporations, with Alaska Natives as the shareholders.

## Part 6D

Then comes the Trans-Alaska Pipeline. Eight hundred miles, from the Arctic Ocean to the port of Valdez. Over three mountain ranges. Across hundreds of rivers. Finished in nineteen seventy-seven.

Engineers build it in zigzags, on sliding supports, so it can flex in an earthquake. <short pause> In two thousand two, a magnitude seven point nine quake hits right under it. <phew> The pipe slides. It doesn't break.

## Part 6E

And Alaska does something unusual with its oil money.

It saves it. In a giant fund. The Alaska Permanent Fund.

<gasp> Since nineteen eighty-two, almost every Alaskan, adult and child, gets a share of the earnings every year. The Permanent Fund Dividend. A check, just for living in Alaska.

## Part 6F (quiet style)

In March nineteen eighty-nine, the tanker Exxon Valdez hits a reef in Prince William Sound.

<long pause> About eleven million gallons of oil pour into the sea.

Hundreds of thousands of seabirds die. Thousands of sea otters.

The coast is still recovering.

## Part 7: Today

Today, Alaska still has more coastline than all the other states put together. More than three million lakes. The tallest mountain in North America. <chuckle> And roughly as many caribou as people.

Fewer than a million people live here. And about one in five of them are Alaska Native.

<chuckle> Its capital, Juneau, has no road to the rest of the state. You get there by boat, or by plane.

In the long winter nights, the sky over Fairbanks fills with the northern lights. In summer, the sun barely sets at all.

<short pause> The Aleutian Islands stretch so far west that they cross into the Eastern Hemisphere. That's why Alaska is the farthest north, west... and east.

And the sea otters came back too. Protected since nineteen eleven, they've returned to much of the coast.

<breath> But Alaska is also warming faster than almost anywhere in America. Sea ice forms later and melts earlier. Permafrost thaws, and the ground under roads and houses sinks. <short pause> Some coastal villages, like Newtok, are already moving their whole town to higher ground.

## Part 7B

So how does a frozen colony sold for pocket change become a place that pays you to live in it?

<short pause> It starts with a fur coat.

The otter fur drew Russia across the sea. When the otters ran out, Russia sold. Then came the gold, which paid the price back many times. And then the oil, which pays Alaskans every single year.

<long pause> But Alaska was never really empty, and never really cheap. It was home for thousands of years before anyone put a price on it.

<chuckle> And if you're ever in Central Park, say hi to Balto.

<whispers> Then tell him... Togo did the hard part.
