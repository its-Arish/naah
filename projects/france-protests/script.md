## COLD OPEN

On the day the Bastille fell, 14 July 1789, King Louis XVI sat down and wrote a single word in his diary. 'Nothing.'

He wasn't being poetic. He had been out hunting, he hadn't killed anything, and that was the entire report. While a crowd stormed a fortress twelve miles from his palace, the king of France was logging a bad day of hunting.

\[On-screen: A diary page. One word in brown ink: 'Rien.' Hold two full seconds. Then the ink bleeds outward across the page like a stain.\]

Now jump forward 237 years. On Saturday 17 October 2026, more than half a million people marched across France. A town hall in Limoges was set on fire. Nearly 1,800 people were arrested in a single day, a record 855 of them in Paris, and more than 500 police officers were injured. \[UPDATE BEFORE RECORDING\]

\[On-screen: Map of France. Red pins drop city by city: Paris, Lille, Nantes, Limoges, Bordeaux, Marseille, Strasbourg. Counter bottom-left: 'ARRESTED 1,800 / PARIS RECORD 855'.\]

And it didn't start with a coup, or a war, or an election. It started with teenagers complaining that their school didn't have enough teachers.

So a lot of people are asking the obvious question. Is France heading for another revolution?

I'm not going to give you a headline. I'm going to give you the whole story, the thousand-year version, and then we're going to run today's France through the same checklist historians use for 1789 and see what comes out. And the answer is not what either side of the internet is telling you.

To start, we have to go back about 1,500 years, to a pagan warlord who got baptised and accidentally invented a country.

## ACT 1 - THE WARLORD, THE EMPEROR AND THE SUN KING

Before France, there was Gaul. Julius Caesar conquered it around 50 BC and Rome ran it for centuries. Then Rome collapsed, and a Germanic people called the Franks moved in. Yes, that is where the word 'France' comes from. The country is literally named after the tribe that invaded it.

Their leader, Clovis, converted to Christianity around the year 500, and that one decision made the Church his ally and turned a warlord into a king. Three centuries later, Charlemagne was crowned emperor by the pope on Christmas Day, 800. Then his grandsons split the empire in three at the Treaty of Verdun in 843, a treaty being just an official agreement, and the western chunk became, roughly, France.

\[On-screen: Map. Charlemagne's empire in one block of gold, cracking into three pieces at '843'. The western piece glows.\]

In 987 the nobles picked a minor lord called Hugh Capet as king, mostly because he looked weak. His family, in various branches, would hold the throne for more than 800 years. Remember the name Capet. It comes back in a very uncomfortable way.

Then England tried to take the whole kingdom. The Hundred Years' War ran from 1337 to 1453, which is actually 116 years, because nobody checked the maths. A teenage peasant girl, Joan of Arc, lifted the siege of Orleans in 1429 and was burned at the stake two years later. She was nineteen. England lost anyway.

Then came the man who built the system: Louis XIV, the Sun King. He ruled for 72 years, the longest reign in European history. As a child he had lived through a rebellion by his own nobles, and he never forgot it. So he built Versailles, moved thousands of courtiers in, and had the greatest aristocrats in France compete for the honour of handing him his shirt. If your rivals are busy fighting over your shirt, they have no time to plot against you.

He probably never said 'I am the state.' He just ran the country as if he had.

But Versailles was expensive, and so were his wars. He died in 1715 leaving enormous debts, and that bill didn't disappear. It got handed down the family tree, with interest, for seventy years, until it landed on the desk of a shy man whose favourite hobby was making locks, and who had absolutely no idea how to fix a budget.

## ACT 2 - THE BILL

His name was Louis XVI. He became king at nineteen, in 1774, and by almost every account he was a decent, well-meaning, slightly awkward man who would rather have been in his workshop. His wife was Marie Antoinette, an Austrian princess who arrived in France as a teenager and was disliked at court almost immediately.

Here is how money worked in his kingdom. French society was split into three groups, called estates. The clergy were the first. The nobility were the second. Everyone else, about 98 percent of the country, was the Third Estate. And the first two were largely exempt from the biggest direct tax. So the richest people in the kingdom paid the least, and the poorest paid the most.

\[On-screen: Pyramid of three tiers. A coin-stream flows downward from the bottom tier, not the top. Money in green, bottom tier in red.\]

Then the king made it worse by backing the American Revolution, to hurt Britain. France helped America win independence, which cost more than a billion livres, and in exchange got bankruptcy. By the 1780s roughly half the royal budget was going on interest alone. That's like spending half your salary on the minimum payment on a credit card, forever.

Finance ministers tried to fix it by taxing the privileged. Every single time, the privileged blocked it. In 1787 the king even called an Assembly of Notables to approve reforms, and they said no. So the system was locked: the people with money wouldn't pay, and the people without money couldn't.

Then nature piled on. In July 1788 a freak hailstorm destroyed crops across northern France. Then came one of the coldest winters anyone could remember. Grain collapsed, bread prices spiked, and by the summer of 1789, by some estimates, a labourer was spending up to 88 percent of his wages on bread alone.

\[On-screen: Bar showing a labourer's daily wage. A bread-loaf icon swallows the bar until only a sliver of red remains. Label: 'UP TO 88%'.\]

And no, Marie Antoinette never said 'let them eat cake.' That line appears in Rousseau's Confessions, written when she was a child in Austria. It was a story people told about out-of-touch royals long before she arrived. It stuck because it felt true. And in a crisis, what feels true often matters more than what is.

By 1788 the king was out of options, so he did something no French king had done for 175 years. He asked the people. He had no idea he was calling the meeting that would end his dynasty.

## ACT 3 - THE TENNIS COURT, THE PRISON AND THE DIARY

The Estates-General opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789. Three estates, one hall. And here's how voting worked.

The king's answer was to move troops toward Paris and sack the popular finance minister, Jacques Necker, on 11 July. Paris heard 'the soldiers are coming' and exploded. On 14 July a crowd hunting for gunpowder and weapons went to the Bastille, a royal fortress-prison in the east of the city.

And here's the part that broke me when I was researching this. The Bastille held exactly seven prisoners. Four forgers, two men judged insane, and one nobleman locked up by his own family. That was the great symbol of tyranny. Seven men, and a lot of gunpowder.

Around a hundred attackers died in the fighting. The governor, Bernard de Launay, was killed and his head was paraded on a pike. That was day one of liberty.

In August the revolution wrote its mission statement. On the night of 4 August the nobles gave up their feudal privileges, and on 26 August the Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which says people are born free and equal in rights. It was one of the most influential documents of the modern world.

But rights don't fill stomachs, and bread was still scarce. So on 5 October 1789, thousands of Paris women, many of them market sellers, marched about twelve miles in the rain to Versailles demanding bread. By the next day they had brought the royal family back to Paris, effectively as guests of the people.

\[On-screen: Map. Dotted line Paris to Versailles, a crowd icon growing along it, rain overlay. Then reverse the line: a carriage in the middle of the crowd, returning.\]

Keep that image in your back pocket. When prices spike and the government seems deaf, the crowd goes to where the power sleeps. And notice the month. October.

For two years the king and the revolution tried to share one country. It ended with a midnight escape attempt, in disguise, with the king pretending to be somebody's servant.

## ACT 4 - THE DISGUISE

On the night of 20 June 1791, Louis XVI, Marie Antoinette and their children slipped out of the Tuileries Palace in Paris, the king dressed as a valet, heading for loyal troops near the border. They made it most of the way. Then, at a town called Varennes, they were stopped. Stories say a postmaster recognised the king from his portrait on a banknote. Historians argue about the details. They don't argue about the result: the royal family was marched back to Paris in near silence.

This mattered more than any speech. Until then, most French people wanted a king under a constitution. After Varennes, many decided the king had never believed in any of it. The idea of no king at all stopped being unthinkable.

Then came war. In April 1792 France declared war on Austria. That summer, the commander of the Austrian and Prussian armies threatened Paris with destruction if the royal family was harmed. It backfired spectacularly. On 10 August 1792 a crowd stormed the Tuileries, hundreds of the king's Swiss Guards were killed, and the monarchy was suspended. By September France was a republic.

At his trial, the king was stripped of his titles and called 'Citizen Louis Capet.' There's that name again. On 21 January 1793 he was guillotined in what is now the Place de la Concorde. Marie Antoinette followed on 16 October. October again.

\[On-screen: Calendar flipping to 21 January 1793. Cut to black. Hold for two beats of silence. No depiction of the execution itself.\]

With the king dead, every monarchy in Europe was at war with France, there were uprisings inside the country, prices were spiralling, and fear took over. The revolution needed someone to protect it, and it found a small-town lawyer who didn't drink, didn't gamble, and was nicknamed The Incorruptible. The government he dominated would sentence around seventeen thousand people to death in ten months.

## ACT 5 - THE TERROR

His name was Maximilien Robespierre, and here's the twist. In 1791 he had given speeches arguing that France should abolish the death penalty. Two years later he was the most powerful member of the Committee of Public Safety, which ran the country in a permanent state of emergency.

In September 1793 the Law of Suspects allowed people to be arrested not for what they had done, but for being suspected of opposing the revolution. Revolutionary tribunals followed. In June 1794 a new law stripped many of the accused of the right to a defence lawyer. This period is called the Terror, and the numbers are grim: around 17,000 official death sentences, and historians estimate tens of thousands more people died without trial or in prison.

And most of those executed were not aristocrats. They were ordinary people: farmers, workers, shopkeepers.

\[On-screen: Bar chart, restrained. Categories: nobles, clergy, commoners. Commoners bar is by far the tallest. No imagery of executions.\]

The guillotine, by the way, was meant to be the humane option, a quick death that was the same for rich and poor. Dr Guillotin, whose name it carries, didn't build it, and he died in his bed in 1814.

The end came on 9 Thermidor, 27 July 1794. Deputies who feared they were next turned on Robespierre. He was arrested, tried to shoot himself and shattered his jaw, and was guillotined the next day with about twenty of his allies. The man who built the machine of fear was eventually caught in it.

Meanwhile, the revolution had been busy reinventing time itself.

But first, France was exhausted, broke, and sick of ideology. It wanted order. And an artillery officer from an island France had bought a year before he was born was about to offer it.

## ACT 6 - THE EMPEROR, THE FLIGHT AND THE FLAMES

His name was Napoleon Bonaparte. In November 1799 he took power in a coup, and in 1804 he crowned himself emperor, literally taking the crown from the pope's hands. A revolution that began by beheading a king had ended with an emperor.

But he also built things that are still here. The Civil Code of 1804 is still the foundation of French law. And in 1800 he created the prefects, officials who run each region on behalf of the central government in Paris. Remember the prefects. They come back.

And the legend that he was tiny? He was about five foot six or seven, normal for his time. The myth comes from French and English inches being different lengths, and some very effective British cartoons.

He lost at Waterloo on 18 June 1815, and the Bourbon kings came back. Then France did something remarkable. It started overthrowing them on a schedule.

\[On-screen: Timeline across the screen. Government flags flip like cards: 1830, 1848, 1852, 1870.\]

In July 1830, three days of street fighting toppled Charles X, who had tried to muzzle the press. In 1848 it happened again, and the new king, Louis-Philippe, fled to England under the name 'Mr Smith.' The Second Republic elected Napoleon's nephew as president. The constitution barred him from a second term, so in 1851 he took power in a coup and became Emperor Napoleon III.

He rebuilt Paris with wide boulevards, partly, many historians say, to make barricades harder to build and troops easier to move. It didn't save him. In September 1870 he was captured by the Prussians at Sedan, and the Third Republic was declared. Paris was besieged for four months, and its starving citizens ate the zoo, including the elephants.

Then, in March 1871, Parisians rose against the national government and set up their own, the Paris Commune. It lasted 72 days. In 'Bloody Week' in May, government troops retook the city, killing somewhere between 10,000 and 20,000 people. Fires burned across Paris, including the Tuileries Palace, the same palace the king had fled in disguise eighty years earlier.

\[On-screen: Match-cut. The Tuileries from Act 4, with the king's carriage leaving, dissolves into the same palace in flames.\]

So in under a century, France had run through two empires, two republics and three kinds of monarchy. And it wasn't done. The next seventy years would cost it almost everything.

## ACT 7 - TWO WARS AND A GENERAL

World War One took around 1.4 million French soldiers. By some counts, more than a quarter of French men aged 18 to 27 were killed. Then in 1940 Germany conquered France in about six weeks. A marshal signed an armistice, a collaborating government set up in the spa town of Vichy, and a general nobody had heard of, Charles de Gaulle, spoke on the radio from London on 18 June and told the French that France had lost a battle, not the war.

After liberation in 1944, France tried again. The Fourth Republic, born in 1946, put parliament in charge and kept the president weak. It went through more than twenty governments in twelve years. When a crisis over Algeria in 1958 pushed the army toward revolt, parliament turned to the one man everybody trusted.

De Gaulle rewrote the constitution. The Fifth Republic, the one France still has, gave the president enormous power, and from 1962 the president was elected directly by the people. A strong president, and a National Assembly that can bring down the president's government. Remember that design. It matters later.

\[On-screen: Diagram. A tall central pillar labelled 'PRESIDENT', and beside it a long table labelled 'ASSEMBLY' holding a small figure labelled 'PRIME MINISTER' that can be pulled off the table. Keep this graphic for Act 12.\]

De Gaulle believed he had built a republic that couldn't be shaken. Ten years later, a rule about who could visit whose dormitory nearly proved him wrong.

## ACT 8 - MAY 1968

In the spring of 1968, students at Nanterre, a university outside Paris, were angry about plenty of things, including rules that banned men and women from visiting each other's rooms. The authorities closed the campus. Protests spread to the Sorbonne in the centre of Paris. On the night of 10 May, barricades went up across the Latin Quarter, police charged, hundreds were injured, and public sympathy swung toward the students.

Then the workers joined. A one-day strike on 13 May became an open-ended one. By late May around ten million workers, roughly two thirds of the French workforce, were on strike. Factories, trains, even broadcasting. The country stopped.

\[On-screen: Map of France with industrial sites switching off like lights, one by one. Counter: 'ON STRIKE: 10,000,000'.\]

Then on 29 May, de Gaulle vanished. For hours, nobody knew where the president of France was. He had flown to Baden-Baden in Germany to see the general commanding French forces there, widely understood as a check that the army was behind him. The next day he came back, announced elections, and in June his supporters won a landslide. The unions got a deal too, with the minimum wage jumping by around 35 percent.

That's the French pattern worth remembering. A movement that looked like a revolution ended with a pay rise and a landslide for the government. De Gaulle himself resigned less than a year later, after losing a referendum.

## ACT 9 - THE LONG SIMMER, 2005 TO 2025

The pattern since 1968 is not one big explosion. It's a series of smaller ones, each with a different trigger.

In October 2005, two teenagers, Zyed Benna and Bouna Traore, died in an electrical substation in the suburb of Clichy-sous-Bois while hiding from police. Riots spread through poor suburbs for three weeks, nearly 9,000 vehicles were burned, and the government declared a state of emergency.

In November 2018 came the Yellow Vests, named after the high-visibility vests every French driver must carry. The trigger was a rise in fuel tax. Every Saturday for months, protesters filled roundabouts and city centres. The government dropped the tax rise, but the anger was never really about the tax. It was about the cost of living and who feels it.

In 2023 the government raised the retirement age from 62 to 64 and pushed it through without a final vote in the Assembly, using a constitutional tool called Article 49.3. Millions marched. And in June 2023, a 17-year-old, Nahel, was shot dead by a police officer during a traffic stop in Nanterre, and riots spread across the country for days.

\[On-screen: Timeline strip, left to right: 2005 (a suburban substation), 2018 (a yellow vest), 2023 (a ballot box stamped 'NO VOTE'), 2023 (a police light). Each icon in the colour of its trigger. Hold the strip for the next paragraph.\]

Then politics itself began to wobble. After a snap election in 2024, the Assembly split into three blocs, none with a majority. Governments started falling. In December 2024, the Prime Minister, Michel Barnier, lost a no-confidence vote. In September 2025, his successor, Francois Bayrou, lost a confidence vote after proposing around 44 billion euros in savings. Two days later, a grassroots movement called 'Block Everything' shut down parts of the country, and Macron appointed Sebastien Lecornu, his seventh prime minister since taking office in 2017.

The triggers keep changing: a dorm rule, a fuel tax, a retirement age, a police shooting, a budget. But protesters keep describing the same complaint, that decisions are made far away by people who don't feel the cost. And governments keep giving the same answer: that the debt is real, and that it doesn't wait.

Which brings us to the autumn of 2026.

## ACT 10 - OCTOBER 2026 \[UPDATE BEFORE RECORDING\]

It began in mid-September, with two things happening at once. On the south coast, fishermen blockaded the port of Nice and a fuel depot near Sete, furious about the price of diesel. And in the suburbs of Paris and other cities, high school students started blockading their own schools, over teacher shortages, overcrowded classes and buildings that can't cope with the heat. By early October, one analysis counted more than 700 secondary schools disrupted. On 7 October, around a quarter of a million people marched.

Why schools? One union survey of more than a thousand schools found that at the start of the year, 53 percent of secondary schools were missing at least one teacher. Reports describe classes of 35 or even 40 students.

Then add money. Prices rose 3.1 percent in the year to September, up from 2.4 percent in August. Diesel is around 2 euros 37 a litre. Reports tie the energy squeeze to the war in Iran.

And then add the debt. France's public debt is about 119 percent of its entire economy, roughly four trillion dollars, the highest ratio since the aftermath of World War Two. The deficit is 5.4 percent of GDP, the 2027 budget aims for 5 percent, and that means finding about 43 billion euros in savings. Government borrowing costs hit their highest level in more than twenty years. One projection says the interest bill alone could near 100 billion dollars a year by 2027.

\[On-screen: Three green coins dropping from a ceiling into a bucket labelled 'REVENUE'. A hole in the bucket's base. Beneath it: 'INTEREST'. Then cut to a classroom with the desks packed too tight.\]

Here's the collision. Students and teachers are demanding more spending, at the exact moment a government that has to cut spending is trying to pass a budget through a split parliament.

On 17 October, the school movement merged with a revived Yellow Vest network, unions, retirees, farmers and climate campaigners into one national day of protest over the cost of living. The Interior Ministry counted about 550,000 people, around 110,000 of them in Paris. By Reuters' account, that was bigger than the Yellow Vests at their peak, and far smaller than the 2023 pension protests.

Most of them marched peacefully. But violent groups attacked town halls and police stations, set cars on fire and looted shops. The city hall in Limoges was burned. In Bordeaux, a masked crowd forced its way into the residence of the local prefect. Yes, that prefect, the office Napoleon invented 226 years ago.

The toll, as reported: nearly 1,800 arrests across the country, a record 855 in Paris, and more than 500 police officers injured. One protester died after a cardiac arrest in Paris. The Interior Minister confirmed that another person lost a hand in clashes with police, and allegations of excessive force against young protesters are being scrutinised.

The response was curfews. Overnight curfews in two areas of Paris, and curfews for under-18s in cities including Lille and Nice. The government says the protests cooled afterwards, and another national day of protest is planned this weekend. Smaller protests over living costs have also appeared in Belgium, Portugal and Italy. And Moody's, the credit rating agency, was due to rule on France's debt rating on 23 October. \[UPDATE BEFORE RECORDING: state the actual Moody's decision\]

So what's driving it? That's where people split.

## ACT 11 - SAME FIRE, DIFFERENT ARSONISTS

Everyone looking at the same smoke is describing a different fire. So let me walk through what each camp is actually saying, in the version its own supporters would recognise.

The government says a peaceful protest was hijacked by a violent minority. The President said there was no room for violence and that rioters would be prosecuted. The Prime Minister said nobody would impose a political agenda through violence, and accused the far-left France Unbowed party of using the unrest as a warm-up for the presidential election. That party's leader, Jean-Luc Melenchon, rejected the charge and told the Prime Minister to prove it or apologise. He has backed the students and criticised the use of tear gas and batons against young people.

On the other side, the far-right National Rally says the government has failed at the most basic job of a state, keeping order. Its president, Jordan Bardella, called the President's televised address lamentable. Marine Le Pen called the government exhausted and incapable, and said rioters had spoiled a demonstration held in defence of purchasing power.

Abroad, Donald Trump blamed 'mass migration' and Islam. President Macron's reply was that foreign leaders were exploiting French riots for their own election purposes, with American midterms days away.

And the economists and analysts, mostly, point at three things: debt, prices and under-funded schools. Some add that the movement has drifted from school grievances toward plain lawlessness.

Each side has a point its supporters would want you to hear. Those condemning the violence note that half a million people marched, that the people who pay for arson and looting are shopkeepers, local workers and public services, and that no grievance justifies it. Those defending the protesters note that the school staffing figures are documented, that the grievances were real before anyone threw anything, and that policing deserves scrutiny. Both of those can be true at once.

I'm not going to tell you which camp is right. What I can do is show you what history says about moments like this. But first, a look at what the people in the middle of it are actually facing.

## ACT 12 - 1789 VS 2026

The political scientist Jack Goldstone spent a career studying why states break down. His answer was that it tends to happen when three things line up. One, the state is in a fiscal crisis and can't pay its bills. Two, the elites are divided and fighting each other. Three, a large part of the population is mobilised and angry. Let's score both years.

\[On-screen: Two-column scoreboard, 1789 vs 2026. Three rows: FISCAL CRISIS, ELITE SPLIT, MASS ANGER. Plus a fourth row that starts hidden: ARMY SWITCHES SIDES. Fill the columns as each is described.\]

**1789.** Fiscal crisis: yes, the kingdom was bankrupt. Elites divided: yes, the nobles blocked every reform, the courts fought the king, and some nobles and priests ended up joining the Third Estate. Mass anger: yes, hungry crowds in Paris. And then the fourth thing that sealed it. The army split. Units of the French Guards joined the crowd at the Bastille. When the people with the guns stop obeying, a government is finished.

**2026.** Fiscal crisis: arguably, but a different kind. France is not bankrupt. Markets still lend to it, and one chief economist told Le Monde the debt problem is serious but manageable, unless political instability makes it worse. But debt at 119 percent of GDP and borrowing costs at twenty-year highs mean every budget is a war. Elites divided: yes, in a different way. Parliament is split into blocs, governments keep falling, and everyone is competing ahead of the election. But they are competing to win the presidency, not to abolish it. Mass anger: yes. Half a million marched, plus students, unions, farmers and fishermen. But it is leaderless, its demands are mixed, from schools to diesel to wages, and a violent fringe that most marchers did not join has taken much of the attention.

Now look at what's missing. One, no defection. The police and the army are doing their jobs, where in 1789 they switched sides. Two, no alternative system on offer. Nobody is marching to abolish the republic. Even the loudest critics want to win the election that's already scheduled. In 1789, the alternative to the king had to be invented from scratch. And three, a legal exit exists, about six months away. In 1789 the only road to change was a meeting that the king didn't want to hold.

But there are echoes that go beyond the checklist. In October 1789, a crowd went to Versailles because that is where the power slept. In October 2026, crowds went to town halls and prefectures. And in 1789, the summer was swept by the Great Fear, rumours that aristocrats had hired brigands to starve the villages. Peasants armed themselves against enemies who didn't exist. In 2026, every camp suspects someone else's hidden hand, and foreign leaders have weighed in. When trust collapses, every explanation starts to sound like a plot.

So is France in a revolution? By the historians' own checklist, not yet. And yes, the word 'yet' is doing a lot of work. But historians are very bad at predicting revolutions and very good at explaining them afterwards. In May 1968, nobody on the barricades knew the system would hold either, until it did.

What 2026 shares with 1789 is the cause, the question of who pays when the bill comes due. Not, so far, the outcome. And that question is almost two and a half centuries old.
