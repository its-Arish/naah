# The Entire History of Utah

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,400 spoken words, so roughly 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen fifty-seven"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller among red rocks, with a wink. |
| Part 2 | Tense and weary, then hopeful. |
| Part 3 | Desperate, then amazed. |
| Part 4 | Serious and steady. |
| Part 5 | Brisk, a little wry, then proud. |
| Part 6 | Bright, outdoorsy and confident; warm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Ancient Utah 10,000 BC to 1776 · Explorers 1776 to 1847 · Deseret 1847 to 1857 · Conflict 1857 to 1869 · Railroad 1869 to 1890 · Statehood 1890 to 1896 · Mines and Parks 1896 to 1945 · Today 1945 to now.

## Hook

Utah's official state snack? <short pause> Jell-O.

<chuckle> Utah's state bird is the California gull. A seagull. In the desert.

The Great Salt Lake is so salty, you float on it like a cork.

<long pause> In eighteen fifty-seven, a militia in Utah killed about a hundred and twenty men, women and children travelling west in a wagon train.

And Utah asked to become a state six times... and was turned down every time.

<short pause> So how does a group of refugees, chased out of every place they lived, build a state in a desert nobody else wanted?

<whispers> It starts with a plague of crickets.

## Part 1: Ancient Utah

Utah is a land of red rock. <gasp> More than two thousand stone arches. Canyons a thousand feet deep. Towers and hoodoos carved by wind, ice and water, over millions of years.

And under that rock, some of the richest dinosaur graveyards on Earth.

In nineteen-oh-nine, a fossil hunter named Earl Douglass finds a line of giant tailbones sticking out of a ridge in eastern Utah. <short pause> He keeps digging. And digging. Today, at Dinosaur National Monument, you can walk into a building and see a whole wall of rock with about fifteen hundred dinosaur bones still in it.

<chuckle> Utah has its own raptor, too. The Utahraptor. Bigger than the ones in the movies. With a killing claw on each foot as long as your hand.

<short pause> And during the last Ice Age, much of western Utah was underwater. A giant freshwater lake, Lake Bonneville, about as big as Lake Michigan is today.

<gasp> About fourteen and a half thousand years ago, it broke through a ridge of rock in Idaho. A flood of water poured out, one of the biggest floods we know of on Earth.

The lake shrank and shrank. What's left is the Great Salt Lake. <short pause> You can still see the old lake's shorelines, like bathtub rings, high up on the mountains around Salt Lake City.

## Part 1B

Long after the dinosaurs, the Fremont people and the Ancestral Puebloans farm the valleys and canyons. <short pause> They build stone granaries high up in the cliffs, to keep their corn safe. And they carve and paint thousands of figures onto the canyon walls. Hunters, animals, spirals, and strange, tall figures with wide shoulders and no faces.

One rock in southern Utah is so covered in carvings, from so many centuries, that it's called Newspaper Rock.

Later, the land belongs to the Ute, the Shoshone, the Paiute, the Goshute and the Navajo. <short pause> The state is named after the Ute.

## Part 1C

In seventeen seventy-six, the same year as the Declaration of Independence, two Spanish priests, Domínguez and Escalante, wander through Utah, looking for a route to California. <tsk> They don't find it. Winter is coming, so they turn back.

Later, fur trappers arrive. Mountain men, hunting beaver. Every summer, they gather for a giant party called a rendezvous, to trade furs, swap stories, and drink.

One of them, Jim Bridger, tastes the water of a giant lake and finds it salty. He thinks he's reached the Pacific Ocean.

<chuckle> He hasn't. It's the Great Salt Lake. The ocean is still about six hundred miles away.

## Part 1D (quiet style)

In eighteen forty-six, a wagon train of families heading for California takes a new shortcut, across the mountains and salt flats of Utah. The shortcut is a disaster. They have to cut their own road through the canyons. It costs them weeks.

<breath> By the time they reach the Sierra Nevada mountains in California, the snow has come.

They are the Donner Party. <long pause> Almost half of them die that winter.

## Part 2: Deseret

Now, the refugees.

In eighteen thirty, in New York, a young man named Joseph Smith founds a new church. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Its members are often called Mormons.

<sigh> Wherever they go, their neighbours turn against them. Their beliefs are unusual. They vote together. They grow fast. They trade with each other.

They're driven out of Ohio. Then out of Missouri, where the governor orders that they be driven out of the state... or exterminated.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

They build a city in Illinois called Nauvoo. For a while, it's one of the biggest cities in the state.

<breath> But in eighteen forty-four, a mob storms the jail where Joseph Smith is being held.

He is shot and killed.

## Part 2C

The church's new leader, Brigham Young, makes a decision. They will go west. Beyond the United States. Somewhere nobody will bother them.

In the bitter winter of eighteen forty-six, thousands of them cross the frozen Mississippi River and head out across the plains, in wagon trains.

<short pause> The next summer, the first group follows the very same shortcut the Donner Party had cut through the mountains a year before.

And on July twenty-fourth, eighteen forty-seven, Young, sick with fever, looks down from the mountains into a dry valley next to the salty lake. He says, "This is the right place."

<chuckle> At that moment, the valley isn't even in the United States. It's part of Mexico.

<laugh> A year later, after the Mexican-American War, it's in the United States again. They can't escape.

## Part 3: The Crickets

They dig ditches from the mountain streams, and flood the dry, hard ground until it's soft enough to plough. They plant crops.

And in the spring of eighteen forty-eight, the crops come up green. <short pause> The settlers have almost nothing else to eat. Everything depends on this harvest.

<gasp> And then, the crickets come.

Huge, black, armoured crickets, as long as your thumb. Millions of them. They swarm over the fields, eating everything. <groan> The settlers beat them with brooms and shovels, drown them in ditches, set fires. Nothing works.

## Part 3B

Then, from the lake, seagulls arrive. Thousands of them.

<chuckle> They land in the fields, and eat the crickets. They eat until they're full, drink some water, throw up, and eat some more.

<phew> Enough of the harvest is saved. The settlers survive.

To them, it's a miracle. Today, there's a monument to the seagulls in Salt Lake City, with two golden gulls on top. And the California gull is Utah's state bird.

## Part 3C

After the crickets, they build. And they build fast. Irrigation canals. Hundreds of towns, from Idaho to Arizona, each laid out on the same neat grid, with streets wide enough to turn a wagon and a team of oxen around.

<short pause> They call their land Deseret, a word from their scripture meaning "honeybee". A symbol of everyone working together. The beehive is still on Utah's flag, and on its highway signs.

In Salt Lake City, they start building a giant granite temple. <chuckle> It takes them forty years. Next to it, they build the Tabernacle, a huge domed hall with no pillars inside. The sound is so good that a pin dropped at the front can be heard at the back.

But Congress won't make Deseret a state. It makes it a territory, and names it after the Ute. Utah.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

Thousands more church members come from Europe, many too poor to buy a wagon. So they walk across the plains, pulling their belongings in wooden handcarts.

<breath> In eighteen fifty-six, two handcart companies set out too late in the year. Early snowstorms catch them in the mountains of Wyoming.

<long pause> More than two hundred people die of cold and hunger before rescuers from Salt Lake can reach them.

## Part 4: Conflict

And the church openly practises polygamy. Some men have more than one wife. Brigham Young has dozens. <short pause> Much of America is outraged.

In eighteen fifty-seven, President Buchanan sends an army toward Utah, to install a new governor. The territory prepares for war. Fear and rumours spread everywhere.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

In September eighteen fifty-seven, a wagon train of families from Arkansas, heading to California, camps at a place called Mountain Meadows.

<breath> Local militiamen, members of the church, along with some Paiute men they recruit, attack them.

After a siege, the militia promises the emigrants safe passage. <long pause> Then they kill about a hundred and twenty people. Only small children are spared.

Years later, only one man, John D. Lee, is ever executed for it. In two thousand seven, the church formally expressed its regret.

## Part 4C

As for the army, there's no big battle. <chuckle> Mormon raiders burn the army's supply wagons and drive off its cattle, and the soldiers spend a miserable winter stuck in Wyoming.

In the end, both sides make a deal. A new governor arrives. The war fizzles out. <tsk> Newspapers call it Buchanan's Blunder.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

The settlers also take more and more of the land and water of the Ute, the Shoshone and the Paiute.

<breath> In January eighteen sixty-three, at Bear River, soldiers attack a Shoshone winter camp. They kill hundreds of people, including women and children.

It is one of the deadliest attacks on Native Americans in the history of the West.

## Part 5: Railroad

Utah slowly connects to the world. In eighteen sixty, Pony Express riders gallop across the salt desert, carrying mail from Missouri to California in about ten days.

<short pause> In October eighteen sixty-one, the telegraph line from the east and the line from the west are joined in Salt Lake City. <chuckle> Two days later, the Pony Express is out of business.

## Part 5B

Then comes the race. Two companies are building the first railroad across America, one from each end, and the government pays them by the mile. <gasp> So they race each other into Utah.

<chuckle> They race so hard that for a while, their crews grade right past each other, building parallel roadbeds side by side, going in opposite directions.

And on one day in April eighteen sixty-nine, near the Great Salt Lake, a crew of Chinese and Irish workers lays ten miles of track in a single day. A record that has never been beaten.

## Part 5C

On May tenth, eighteen sixty-nine, at Promontory Summit, in Utah, two railroads meet. One built from the east, by many Irish workers. One built from the west, by many Chinese workers.

<gasp> A golden spike is placed in the last tie. <chuckle> According to the story, the railroad boss swings the hammer at the final spike... and misses.

But the first railroad across America is complete. A telegraph sends one word to the whole country.

"Done."

<sigh> In the famous photo, the Chinese workers who built the western half are almost nowhere to be seen.

## Part 5D

And Utah does something surprising. In eighteen seventy, it gives women the vote. A schoolteacher named Seraph Young becomes the first woman to vote under an equal suffrage law in the United States.

<tsk> But Congress, trying to crush polygamy, passes a law that takes the vote away from Utah's women again.

## Part 5E

Meanwhile, a boy from Circleville, Utah, named Robert LeRoy Parker, grows up to rob banks and trains across the West. <chuckle> Butch Cassidy. His gang hides out in a maze of red canyons, in a place called Robbers Roost.

And in Ogden, a gunsmith's son named John Moses Browning designs guns that armies around the world will use for more than a hundred years.

## Part 6: Statehood

Utah keeps asking to be a state. Six times. Six rejections. <tsk> Always because of polygamy.

So in eighteen ninety, the church's president announces that the church will stop new plural marriages.

And on January fourth, eighteen ninety-six, Utah becomes the forty-fifth state. Its women get the vote back.

<short pause> That same year, Utah elects the first woman state senator in America. Martha Hughes Cannon. A doctor. <chuckle> One of the people she beat in the election? Her own husband.

## Part 6B

In the twentieth century, miners dig the Bingham Canyon copper mine. <gasp> It becomes one of the biggest man-made holes on Earth. More than half a mile deep, and two and a half miles across.

On the Bonneville Salt Flats, a dried-up lake bed as flat as a table, drivers race cars faster and faster. <chuckle> In nineteen thirty-five, a British driver becomes the first to go faster than three hundred miles an hour.

Utah's red rock country becomes national parks. Zion. Bryce Canyon. Arches. Canyonlands. Capitol Reef.

<short pause> And a farm boy from Beaver, Utah, Philo Farnsworth, gets an idea while ploughing a field in straight lines, back and forth. <gasp> What if you could send a picture the same way, line by line? He grows up to invent the first all-electronic television.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

During the Second World War, the government forces more than eight thousand Japanese Americans into Topaz, a camp of tar-paper barracks in the Utah desert.

<breath> Dust storms. Barbed wire. Guard towers.

<long pause> An old man named James Wakasa walks too close to the fence, and is shot dead by a guard.

## Part 6D

In two thousand two, Salt Lake City hosts the Winter Olympics.

Today, Utah is one of the fastest-growing states, with one of the youngest populations in America. Tech companies fill the valley between Salt Lake and Provo. <chuckle> People call it Silicon Slopes.

<short pause> In the shallow, pink-red water of the Great Salt Lake, an artist builds a giant spiral of black rock, curling out into the lake. The Spiral Jetty. <sigh> But the lake is shrinking, as people take more and more of the water from the rivers that feed it. In twenty twenty-two, it falls to its lowest level ever recorded.

And in two thousand one, Utah's lawmakers made Jell-O the official state snack. <laugh> Utahns eat a LOT of it.

## Part 6E

So how does a group of refugees, chased out of every place they lived, build a state in a desert nobody else wanted?

<whispers> It starts with a plague of crickets.

When the crickets came, the settlers had nowhere else to go. They had to make the desert work, or die. <short pause> And when the gulls came, they took it as a sign to keep going.

They dug the canals, built the towns, and did everything together, like bees in a hive.

<breath> Nobody wanted that valley. That's exactly why they chose it.

<chuckle> And they never forgot to thank the seagulls.
