### Part 1: hook, Canaan, kingdoms, Babylon

```
You say the word "Amen," you shout "Hallelujah." Both Hebrew. You plan your week in seven days, with one day of rest. That rhythm comes from the Hebrew Bible. Christmas? Built around a Jewish teacher. Your map app, Waze, was built by Israelis. Your laptop's processor may have been designed in Haifa. Now here's the strange part. All of it came from a people who, for nearly two thousand years, had no country at all. Scattered across every continent. Expelled, again and again. Then, in nineteen forty-eight, they had one. A land the size of New Jersey, and nearly everyone on earth has an opinion about it. Almost nobody has the whole story. So where does it begin? Four thousand years ago, in a hot, dry land called Canaan. And why does it still burn? Four thousand years. Thirty minutes. Each side's story, told fairly. Let's go.

Start with a man named Abraham. <short pause> According to the Bible, he leaves his home in Mesopotamia and walks west into Canaan, the land between the Mediterranean Sea and the Jordan River. His grandson Jacob is renamed Israel, which means something like "he who struggles with God." Jacob's twelve sons become the twelve tribes. Famine drives the family to Egypt, and the story says they are enslaved there, until a leader named Moses brings them out, and at Mount Sinai they receive the law.

Historians can't confirm that story. There is no Egyptian record of an exodus. <short pause> But there IS one very old clue. Around twelve-oh-eight B.C.E., the Egyptian pharaoh Merneptah carved a victory stone listing the peoples he had crushed in Canaan. One of them is called Israel. It's the oldest known mention of the name outside the Bible. So about three thousand two hundred years ago, someone called Israel was already living in those hills.

By around a thousand B.C.E., the Bible says the tribes unite under kings. First Saul, then David, who captures a hill town called Jerusalem and makes it his capital. His son Solomon builds a great temple there, the First Temple. How big was this kingdom, really? Archaeologists argue about it. <chuckle> Some see an empire. Others see a small highland kingdom. What nobody argues about is what the Temple did. It made Jerusalem the center of Jewish life, for good.

After Solomon, the kingdom splits in two. Israel in the north, Judah in the south, with Jerusalem. In these years, prophets like Amos and Isaiah appear, denouncing kings who ignore the poor, and demanding justice from the powerful. Those demands become some of the most quoted words in the Bible. But the neighborhood gets dangerous. In seven twenty-two B.C.E., the Assyrian empire crushes the northern kingdom and scatters its people. They vanish from history. The ten lost tribes. Judah survives, barely. Then comes Babylon. In five eighty-six B.C.E., King Nebuchadnezzar burns Jerusalem, destroys Solomon's Temple, and marches Judah's leaders almost a thousand miles east, in chains. <short pause> That is the first exile. And it changes everything.
```

### Part 2: exile, Maccabees, Rome, dispersal

```
Here's the question exile forces. If your God lives in one Temple in one city, and the Temple is rubble, and you're a thousand miles away, what are you? <short pause> The exiles in Babylon answer by inventing something new. They gather. They write down their stories. They pray together without any temple at all. A people held together by a book and a calendar, instead of a building. Remember that. It's the trick that will save them again and again.

In five thirty-nine B.C.E., Persia conquers Babylon, and King Cyrus lets the exiles go home. Some go. Many stay. The ones who return build a Second Temple, and for the next few centuries Judea is a small province under one empire after another. First Persia. Then, in three thirty-two, Alexander the Great, and after him, Greek language, Greek gods, and Greek gyms. <chuckle>

Which brings us to a king named Antiochus, who in one sixty-seven B.C.E. bans Jewish practice and sets up an idol in the Temple. A family called the Maccabees rebels, and wins. They rededicate the Temple. That is Hanukkah, the festival of lights. And for roughly eighty years, the Jews have their own kingdom again.

Then Rome arrives. In sixty-three B.C.E., the general Pompey marches into Jerusalem. Rome backs a king named Herod, who rebuilds the Temple on a spectacular scale. The huge stones of its retaining wall still stand today. It's the Western Wall. <short pause> Under Rome, Judea simmers. Taxes. Rebels. Rival sects. One of them gathers around a Jewish teacher from Galilee named Jesus, who is executed by Roman authority around the year thirty. His followers start a movement that will, much later, become Christianity.

In sixty-six C.E., the Jews rise up against Rome. It is a catastrophe. In seventy, the Roman general Titus besieges Jerusalem and burns the Second Temple. Hundreds of thousands die. <breath> The Arch of Titus in Rome, still standing, shows soldiers carrying off the Temple's golden menorah. The last rebels hold out in the desert fortress of Masada, until about seventy-three.

Hope flares one more time. In one thirty-two, a leader named Simon bar Kokhba revolts again. Rome crushes it by one thirty-five, at terrible cost. The emperor Hadrian bans Jews from Jerusalem and renames the province Syria Palaestina, the name that "Palestine" comes from. <short pause> So now the people have no Temple, no capital, and in most of the land, no permission to live there. What do they have? The book. Over the next few centuries, rabbis in Galilee and in Babylon write down their traditions: the Mishnah, then the Talmud. A nation, packed into a library. And a library can travel anywhere.

Meanwhile, the little movement around Jesus grows, and slowly splits away from Judaism. In the early three hundreds, the Roman emperor Constantine embraces Christianity, and within about seventy years it is the empire's official religion. Now the land the Jews are barred from is a Christian holy land, with great churches rising over the places of the Gospels. For Jews, the next centuries bring new bans, forced conversions, and revolts that fail. But the book keeps them together.
```

### Part 3: the diaspora, Europe, Zionism

```
And so begins the long dispersal, the diaspora. For about eighteen hundred years, most Jews live as a minority somewhere else. After six thirty-eight, many live under Islamic rule, which takes Jerusalem that year. Jews and Christians there are protected but second-class. They pay a special tax and live under rules. Sometimes that is mild, sometimes it isn't. But compared with what's coming in Christian Europe, it is often calmer.

In Muslim Spain, in the tenth and eleventh centuries, Jewish poets, doctors and philosophers flourish. Maimonides, born in Córdoba in eleven thirty-eight, becomes the most influential Jewish thinker of the Middle Ages, and a physician at the court in Cairo. Meanwhile in Christian Europe, the pressure builds. In ten ninety-nine, Crusaders capture Jerusalem and slaughter Muslims and Jews alike. On the way, Crusader mobs massacre Jewish communities along the Rhine. Jews are barred from owning land and joining guilds, so many become moneylenders, work that Christians are forbidden to do, and are then hated for it. Lies spread. That Jews poison wells. That they murder Christian children for ritual blood. None of it is true, and thousands die because of it. <short pause>

Then the expulsions. England, twelve ninety. France, thirteen-oh-six. And Spain, fourteen ninety-two, the same year Columbus sails. The Alhambra Decree gives Spain's Jews a few months to convert or leave. Tens of thousands go, by some estimates far more. The Ottoman sultan welcomes many of them. They carry their Spanish with them, a language called Ladino.

And in fifteen sixteen, the city of Venice does something that gives the world a word. It orders all its Jews to live on one small island, beside an old foundry, locked in at night. The island is called the Ghetto. The word spreads across Europe, and so do the walls.

In Eastern Europe, Poland takes in Jews, and by the eighteen hundreds most of the world's Jews live under the Russian empire, held inside a zone called the Pale of Settlement. <short pause> Then, in the age of the French Revolution, a new door opens. Emancipation. Citizenship for Jews, first in France, then across the West. Jews pour into universities and professions. But a new hatred arrives with it, based not on religion but on invented ideas of "race." And in Russia, in eighteen eighty-one, after the tsar is assassinated, pogroms sweep the country. Mobs, burning, killing, year after year. Millions of Jews emigrate, mostly to America. A few thousand go somewhere else: the ancient homeland, then a quiet corner of the Ottoman Empire.

In eighteen ninety-four, a French army officer named Alfred Dreyfus, a Jew, is falsely convicted of treason. A journalist named Theodor Herzl covers the case for a Vienna newspaper, and it shakes him. If this can happen in France, the most enlightened country in Europe, he decides, then Jews will never be safe as a minority anywhere. In eighteen ninety-six he writes a pamphlet called "The Jewish State." In eighteen ninety-seven he holds the first Zionist Congress, in Basel, Switzerland. The idea is simple: Jews need a country of their own. Zionism, named for Zion, a hill in Jerusalem.

Many religious and many secular Jews at first think it's absurd, or dangerous. But the pogroms keep coming, and the idea spreads. A scholar named Eliezer Ben-Yehuda moves to Jerusalem and attempts something almost unheard of: reviving Hebrew as an everyday spoken language, after nearly two thousand years as a language of prayer and books. He raises his son speaking only Hebrew. People think he's mad. <chuckle> Today, millions of Israelis speak it.

The first wave of settlers arrives in eighteen eighty-two, mostly from Russia. They drain swamps, plant vineyards, and found farming villages, some funded by the banker Baron Edmond de Rothschild. In nineteen-oh-nine, a group founds the first kibbutz, Degania, and another founds a sandy suburb of Jaffa called Tel Aviv. To the Arab farmers nearby, the newcomers are strangers buying up land. Some trade with them. Some resent them. Few yet see what is coming.

By nineteen fourteen, a few tens of thousands of Jews live in Ottoman Palestine, alongside about six hundred thousand Arabs, Muslim and Christian, whose families have been there for centuries. And that is where the story gets complicated. <long pause> Because now, two peoples claim one land.
```

### Part 4: the Mandate

```
World War One breaks the Ottoman Empire, and Britain takes Palestine. During the war, London makes promises that can't all be kept. To Arab leaders, promises of independence. And in November nineteen seventeen, in the Balfour Declaration, a letter of a few lines: Britain "views with favour" a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, provided nothing harms the rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. Jews cheer. Arabs, then about nine in ten of the people there, see a foreign power promising away their country.

In the nineteen twenties and thirties, Jewish immigration rises, from Poland, and after Hitler, from Germany. Tel Aviv grows from a sand-dune suburb into a city. Kibbutzim spread. Land is bought, often from absent landlords, and sometimes the Arab farmers who worked it are evicted. Take nineteen twenty-nine. A dispute over access to the Western Wall explodes into riots across Palestine. In Hebron, Arab mobs kill sixty-seven Jews, and the survivors are driven out of a Jewish community that had lived there for centuries. More than a hundred Arabs are killed too, most of them by British police and troops. Trust between the communities shrinks. <short pause>

By nineteen thirty-six, with the Jewish share of the population more than a quarter and rising, Palestinian Arabs launch a revolt against both Britain and Jewish settlement. Britain crushes it, killing thousands. Jewish militias, the Haganah, and the more militant Irgun, fight back, and sometimes attack Arab civilians. Violence from both sides is already part of the story. <short pause> In the middle of the revolt, in nineteen thirty-seven, a British commission, the Peel Commission, concludes that the two peoples cannot share one government, and proposes the first partition plan: a small Jewish state, and a larger Arab one. Most Zionist leaders accept the principle, with doubts. Arab leaders reject it. Nothing happens.

Then, in May nineteen thirty-nine, as war looms and Jews need an escape more than ever, Britain issues a White Paper. It caps Jewish immigration at seventy-five thousand over five years, then ends it unless Arabs agree. The door closes at the worst possible moment.
```

### Part 5: the Holocaust (quiet style)

```
What happens next is the darkest chapter in Jewish history, and it should be told plainly. <long pause> In nineteen thirty-three, the Nazis take power in Germany. Step by step, Jews are stripped of citizenship, jobs, and homes. In November nineteen thirty-eight, on Kristallnacht, synagogues burn across Germany. Then comes war, and in nineteen forty-one, mass shootings behind the German lines in the East. By nineteen forty-two, the Nazis are running death camps, with gas chambers, built to murder Jews on an industrial scale. <long pause> Six million Jewish men, women and children were killed. About two out of every three Jews in Europe. Whole communities, in Poland, Lithuania, Greece, Hungary, simply gone. <breath>

Some resist, in the Warsaw Ghetto uprising in nineteen forty-three, and in the forests, as partisans. Some neighbors hide Jews, at the risk of their own lives. But most countries, including the United States and Britain, keep their doors mostly closed to refugees. The Nazis murder millions of others too: Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners, disabled people.

When the camps are liberated in nineteen forty-five, survivors have nowhere to go. Their villages are empty, or hostile. About a quarter of a million Jews wait in displaced-persons camps, sometimes for years. Some try to reach Palestine on crowded, rickety ships, past a British naval blockade. In nineteen forty-seven, the ship Exodus, carrying over four thousand survivors, is intercepted, and the passengers are sent back to Germany. The world sees it. <short pause> And for many people, a question that had been political becomes a moral one. Where, exactly, are these people supposed to go?
```

### Part 6: partition, 1948, the Nakba

```
Britain, exhausted, hands the problem to the new United Nations. On November twenty-ninth, nineteen forty-seven, the U.N. votes to divide Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, with Jerusalem under international control. Thirty-three countries vote yes, thirteen no, ten abstain. The Jewish leadership accepts, with reservations. The Arab leaders reject it. They argue that it gives the Jewish minority, about a third of the people, more than half the land, and that nobody asked the people who lived there. <short pause> Civil war begins the next day.

In the months that follow, atrocities happen on both sides. In April, at the village of Deir Yassin, Jewish irregulars kill over a hundred Arab villagers. Days later, Arab fighters ambush a convoy heading to Hadassah hospital and kill seventy-eight Jewish doctors, nurses and others. Fear drives people out of their homes on both sides. <short pause>

On May fourteenth, nineteen forty-eight, David Ben-Gurion stands in a Tel Aviv museum and reads the declaration of the State of Israel. Within minutes, the United States recognizes it. The next day, the armies of Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon invade. Israelis call what follows the War of Independence. Palestinians call what happens to them the Nakba, the catastrophe. <short pause>

Both names describe real things. Israel survives the invasion, and ends up with more land than the U.N. plan gave it. When the fighting stops in nineteen forty-nine, about seven hundred thousand Palestinians, most of the Arabs who lived in what became Israel, have fled or been driven from their homes. Historians still argue about how many ran from the fighting and how many were expelled. But they agree on the outcome. Israel does not let them return. They and their descendants, millions of people today, are still counted as refugees. Jordan takes the West Bank, including East Jerusalem and the Old City, and Jews are expelled from the Old City and kept from the Western Wall for nineteen years. Egypt takes Gaza.

Inside Israel, about a hundred and fifty thousand Palestinians stay and become Israeli citizens, though they live under military rule until nineteen sixty-six. In nineteen fifty, Israel passes the Law of Return, which gives any Jew in the world the right to immigrate and become a citizen. Palestinian refugees have no such right.

At the same time, another exodus is underway. Over the next few decades, around eight hundred and fifty thousand Jews leave, or are pushed out of, Arab countries and Iran. Iraq, Yemen, Morocco, Egypt, Libya. Their property is often seized. About six hundred thousand settle in Israel, a country that had seven hundred thousand people, and that now doubles in size in just a few years, housing newcomers in tent camps. Today, roughly half of Israel's Jews trace their roots to the Middle East and North Africa.

So the war ends with two refugee peoples, two founding stories, and no peace. Not one Arab state recognizes Israel.
```

### Part 7: 1956 to 1979

```
In the nineteen fifties, border raids by Palestinians, and Israeli reprisals, turn into a cycle. In nineteen fifty-six, Israel joins Britain and France in attacking Egypt over the Suez Canal, and is pressured to withdraw. By nineteen sixty-seven, tension peaks. Egypt's president Nasser expels United Nations peacekeepers from Sinai, moves troops to the border, and closes the Straits of Tiran to Israeli ships. Israel calls that an act of war. On June fifth, Israel strikes first, destroying most of Egypt's air force on the ground within hours. In six days, Israel takes the Sinai and Gaza from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria.

Israeli soldiers weep at the Western Wall, which Jews had not been able to reach since forty-eight. For many Jews, it feels like a miracle. <short pause> For the million Palestinians now under Israeli military control, it is the start of an occupation that has lasted ever since. Israel annexes East Jerusalem and offers its Palestinian residents residency, not citizenship, which most of the world does not recognize. And Israelis still argue among themselves about whether sixty-seven was a war of necessity, or a war of choice.

The Arab League answers at Khartoum with the famous three no's: no peace, no recognition, no negotiation with Israel. Israel begins building settlements in the occupied land. Most countries consider them illegal under international law. Israeli governments dispute that. Through the early seventies, Palestinian groups led by the P.L.O. carry out hijackings and attacks abroad. At the nineteen seventy-two Olympics in Munich, gunmen kill eleven Israeli athletes and coaches.

Then, on October sixth, nineteen seventy-three, Yom Kippur, the holiest day in the Jewish calendar, Egypt and Syria attack by surprise. <breath> For a few days Israel reels, and about two thousand six hundred of its soldiers die before it turns the tide. In nineteen seventy-six, Israeli commandos fly four thousand kilometers to Entebbe, Uganda, and rescue more than a hundred hostages from a hijacked plane. But the shock of seventy-three shatters Israel's confidence. And it changes something in Egypt's president, Anwar Sadat. In nineteen seventy-seven, he does what no Arab leader has done. He flies to Jerusalem and speaks to the Israeli parliament. In nineteen seventy-nine, after talks at Camp David, Egypt and Israel sign a peace treaty. Israel returns the Sinai. Egypt recognizes Israel. Sadat is assassinated by Islamist soldiers two years later. <short pause> But the peace holds, to this day. And it proves that land can be traded for peace. Whether that could work with the Palestinians is the question that dominates the next forty years.
```

### Part 8: Oslo to the Abraham Accords

```
In nineteen eighty-two, Israel invades Lebanon to drive out the P.L.O., and ends up occupying the south of the country for eighteen years. Out of that occupation grows a new enemy, Hezbollah, backed by Iran. Then, in nineteen eighty-seven, a Palestinian uprising begins in Gaza and spreads. The First Intifada, mostly stones, strikes and clashes, met by heavy Israeli force. Both sides, strangely, are exhausted. In nineteen ninety-three, secret talks in Oslo lead to a famous handshake on the White House lawn, between Israel's prime minister Yitzhak Rabin and the Palestinian leader Yasser Arafat. Israel and the P.L.O. recognize each other. A Palestinian Authority is created to govern parts of the West Bank and Gaza, while the hardest questions are left for later: Jerusalem, borders, refugees, settlements. <short pause>

Then the opponents on both sides go to work. Hamas, a Palestinian Islamist group that rejects Israel's existence, sends suicide bombers onto Israeli buses and into cafés. And on November fourth, nineteen ninety-five, Rabin is assassinated at a peace rally by a Jewish Israeli extremist who opposed the deal. <long pause>

In two thousand, talks at Camp David collapse, and each side blames the other. The Second Intifada begins: five years of suicide bombings and military crackdowns, in which about a thousand Israelis and more than three thousand Palestinians are killed. Israel builds a barrier in and around the West Bank, which it says stops the bombers, and which Palestinians say seizes their land. In two thousand five, Israel pulls all its settlers and soldiers out of Gaza. Two years later, Hamas takes control of Gaza, after a brief civil war with its rival Fatah, and Israel and Egypt blockade the territory. Rockets from Gaza. Israeli air strikes on Gaza. Wars in two thousand eight, twelve, fourteen, and twenty-one.

Meanwhile in the West Bank, Israeli settlement keeps growing. Today about half a million Israeli settlers live there, and roughly two hundred thousand more in East Jerusalem. Most of the world considers the settlements illegal. Israeli governments dispute that, and many settlers see themselves as returning to the biblical heartland. Palestinians in the West Bank live under a patchwork of Palestinian and Israeli control. A two-state solution still has supporters on both sides, but to many, it feels further away every year.

And yet, in two thousand twenty, something unexpected. The Abraham Accords. The United Arab Emirates and Bahrain, then Morocco and Sudan, normalize relations with Israel, without any deal for the Palestinians. Many governments in the region have decided that Iran is the bigger threat, and that the Palestinian question can wait. Palestinians feel forgotten.
```

### Part 9: October 7 to today (quiet style)

```
Israel in early two thousand twenty-three is deeply divided, with months of mass protests over a plan to weaken the courts. Then, on the morning of October seventh, two thousand twenty-three, a Saturday and a Jewish holiday, Hamas-led fighters break through the Gaza border fence. They kill about twelve hundred people, most of them civilians, in farming communities and at a music festival. It is the deadliest single day for Jews since the Holocaust. And they take two hundred and fifty-one people hostage into Gaza. <long pause>

Israel declares war on Hamas. The campaign that follows is the most destructive in Gaza's history. According to Gaza's health ministry, part of the Hamas-run government, more than seventy-four thousand Palestinians have been killed, about half of them women and children. The ministry does not separate civilians from fighters. U.N. agencies generally consider its counts reliable. Israel disputes them. <short pause> Most of Gaza's two million people have been displaced, many of them more than once, and much of its housing, hospitals and water systems are destroyed. Israel says it is fighting an enemy that hides among civilians, and that its goal is to destroy Hamas and bring the hostages home. Its critics say the cost to civilians is far beyond what any goal can justify. Courts and scholars are still weighing the gravest charges, and a film cannot settle that for them.

The war spreads. Hezbollah in Lebanon fires on northern Israel, and in two thousand twenty-four, Israel and Hezbollah fight a war that ends in a ceasefire that November. In June two thousand twenty-five, Israel and Iran trade strikes for twelve days, with the United States joining in against Iran's nuclear sites. In October two thousand twenty-five, after two years of war, a ceasefire brokered by the United States takes hold, and the remaining hostages are returned. But a year later, its hardest parts are stuck: who governs Gaza, whether Hamas disarms, and when Israeli forces withdraw. Most people in Gaza are still living in tents and rubble. And in Israel, an election in October two thousand twenty-six has left the political picture unsettled as this is written. <long pause>

So where does that leave us? Israel today is a country of about ten million people. Roughly three in four are Jewish, and about one in five is Arab. The world has about fifteen million Jews, with Israel and the United States each home to six or seven million. Remember the question from the beginning? Why does almost nobody have the whole story? Because it is two true stories. And for the people who live it, each one is a story of survival. <long pause>

Four thousand years ago, a man walked into Canaan with a promise. A people lost their home, kept their book, and found each other again. Another people have held on to their homes, their keys, and their names, through exile and occupation. Whether those two stories can be lived side by side is the one page nobody has written yet.
```
