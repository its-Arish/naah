# The Entire History of New Jersey

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "Alexander Hamilton"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Tense, brave, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Busy, inventive, a little cheeky, then grave. |
| Part 4 | Wonder-struck inventor energy. |
| Part 5 | Bright and showy, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Clever, modern, confident; calm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Lenapehoking 10,000 BC to 1664 · Colony 1664 to 1775 · Crossroads 1775 to 1787 · Mill Towns 1791 to 1876 · Invention Factory 1876 to 1930 · Shore and Sky 1870 to 1945 · Labs 1945 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

The first practical light bulb? <short pause> New Jersey. The first drive-in movie theatre? New Jersey. The streets in Monopoly? Named after Atlantic City.

<chuckle> In New Jersey, it's illegal to pump your own gas.

And the first movie studio in America wasn't in Hollywood. It was in New Jersey.

<long pause> Alexander Hamilton was shot in New Jersey. And the giant airship Hindenburg exploded in flames over it.

<short pause> So how does a small state, squeezed between New York and Philadelphia, end up inventing the modern world?

<whispers> It starts with a waterfall Alexander Hamilton wanted to put to work.

## Part 1: Lenapehoking

For thousands of years, this land is Lenapehoking, the land of the Lenape.

They fish the rivers and the shore. They farm corn, beans and squash. And they gather oysters, in such enormous numbers that their shell piles grow into small hills along the coast.

<short pause> In summer, many Lenape families move down to the Jersey Shore, to fish and gather shellfish. <chuckle> New Jersey's summer trip to the beach is thousands of years old.

## Part 1B

And New Jersey has dinosaurs. In eighteen fifty-eight, in a marl pit in Haddonfield, a man digging finds giant bones. They belong to a creature called Hadrosaurus. <gasp> It's the first nearly complete dinosaur skeleton ever found anywhere in the world. When it's put together and shown in Philadelphia, crowds line up for hours.

## Part 1C

In sixteen-oh-nine, Henry Hudson sails past. The Dutch claim the land as part of New Netherland. Swedish and Finnish settlers build farms in the south, along the Delaware.

In sixteen sixty-four, the English take it over. The new owners name it after the island of Jersey in the English Channel, where one of them, George Carteret, came from.

<short pause> New Jersey.

## Part 1D

From the very start, New Jersey is in the middle. New York on one side. Philadelphia on the other.

<chuckle> Benjamin Franklin supposedly called it "a barrel tapped at both ends". Everyone drinks from it, and passes through it, on the way somewhere else.

For a while, it's even split in two. East Jersey and West Jersey, with two different governments. <short pause> In seventeen-oh-two, the two halves are joined back together.

## Part 1E

In the south of New Jersey lies a huge forest of short pine trees, sandy soil and tea-coloured streams. The Pine Barrens. A million acres, right between the big cities.

<whispers> And the Pine Barrens have a monster.

According to the legend, in seventeen thirty-five, a woman called Mother Leeds learns she's having her thirteenth child. <chuckle> Exhausted, she says: let this one be the Devil. The baby is born... and turns into a creature with wings, hooves and a tail, and flies up the chimney.

The Jersey Devil. <short pause> People still claim to see it. New Jersey's hockey team is named after it.

## Part 2: Crossroads

And in the Revolution, New Jersey is right in the way.

More battles and skirmishes happen here than in almost any other state. People call it the "Crossroads of the Revolution".

<sigh> In the autumn of seventeen seventy-six, the American army is losing badly. Washington retreats across New Jersey, with the British right behind him. His army shrinks every day, as soldiers' enlistments run out and they go home.

Marching with that army is a writer named Thomas Paine. On the retreat, he starts writing an essay, on a drum head, the story goes, by the campfire.

It begins: <short pause> These are the times that try men's souls.

<breath> Washington has it read aloud to his freezing soldiers.

## Part 2B

Then, on Christmas night, Washington's freezing army crosses the icy Delaware River, in the dark, in a snowstorm.

<gasp> At dawn, they surprise the enemy at Trenton, New Jersey. Hessian soldiers, hired from Germany, are still groggy from their Christmas celebrations. <short pause> In about an hour, it's over. Almost a thousand prisoners.

A week later, Washington wins again at Princeton.

The Revolution is saved.

## Part 2C

The army spends two winters at Morristown, New Jersey. <groan> One of them is the coldest winter of the century. Snowdrifts six feet deep. Soldiers without shoes.

And at the Battle of Monmouth, in seventeen seventy-eight, on a blazing summer day, a story says a woman nicknamed Molly Pitcher carries water to the soldiers. <short pause> When her husband collapses at his cannon, she takes his place and keeps firing.

## Part 2D

New Jersey also does something unusual. Its first constitution, in seventeen seventy-six, lets "all inhabitants" with enough property vote.

<gasp> For thirty years, some women in New Jersey vote. And free Black men too. <sigh> In eighteen-oh-seven, lawmakers take it away.

## Part 2E

For a few months in seventeen eighty-three, the government of the United States meets in Princeton. Then briefly in Trenton. <chuckle> New Jersey was, for a moment, the capital of America.

In December seventeen eighty-seven, it becomes the third state.

## Part 3: Mill Towns

Now, the waterfall.

Alexander Hamilton, America's first Treasury Secretary, has a big idea. America shouldn't just farm. It should make things. Factories, machines, industry.

In seventeen ninety-one, he and his partners choose the spot. The Great Falls of the Passaic River. <gasp> A seventy-seven-foot waterfall, roaring with power.

They plan a whole city around it. Canals, called raceways, carry the water to mills. They name it after the governor. Paterson.

It's America's first planned industrial city.

## Part 3B

And to carry coal and iron across the hills of northern New Jersey, engineers build the Morris Canal. <gasp> It climbs more than nine hundred feet. Too steep for normal locks. So at the steep bits, the boats are floated onto giant cradles on rails, and hauled up the hills by water-powered cables. <chuckle> Boats, riding railways, over hills.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

Hamilton doesn't live to see it grow.

<breath> On July eleventh, eighteen-oh-four, at Weehawken, New Jersey, he fights a duel with the Vice President, Aaron Burr.

<long pause> Burr shoots him. Hamilton dies the next day.

## Part 3D

But Paterson works. Its mills spin cotton. Its factories build locomotives, and later weave so much silk that it's called "Silk City".

<short pause> In Paterson, an Irish schoolteacher named John Holland tests one of the first practical submarines in the river. <chuckle> The U.S. Navy buys one. Its first submarine.

And all across New Jersey, factories grow along the rivers and railroads, between the two big cities. Newark makes leather, beer and jewellery. Trenton makes pottery and steel cable. <short pause> The steel wire rope for the Brooklyn Bridge is made by the Roebling company in Trenton. Camden builds ships, and later... records and radios.

<chuckle> And in Camden, in eighteen sixty-nine, a fruit merchant named Joseph Campbell starts canning tomatoes. Almost thirty years later, his company sells condensed soup. <short pause> The red and white can.

<chuckle> In Trenton, there's a bridge with a sign in giant letters. Trenton Makes. The World Takes.

<sigh> But the mills are hard places to work. In nineteen thirteen, Paterson's silk workers, many of them immigrants, some of them children, go on strike for an eight-hour day. Twenty-four thousand people walk out. <short pause> They hold out for five months. And they lose.

But a few years later, the eight-hour day spreads across America anyway.

## Part 3E

In eighteen forty-six, in Hoboken, two teams play one of the first organised baseball games under written rules.

<short pause> And in eighteen sixty-nine, Rutgers and Princeton play the first college football game. <laugh> Rutgers wins. Princeton has been trying to get over it for more than a hundred and fifty years.

## Part 3F (quiet style)

In New Jersey, slavery ends very slowly. A law in eighteen-oh-four frees children born after that year... only when they grow up. <sigh> New Jersey is the last northern state to start ending slavery. And some people are still enslaved there until the Thirteenth Amendment, in eighteen sixty-five.

## Part 4: Invention Factory

In eighteen seventy-six, a young inventor named Thomas Edison moves to a quiet village called Menlo Park, New Jersey.

He builds something nobody has built before. Not just an invention. <short pause> A factory for inventions. A laboratory, with machinists, chemists and engineers, all working on new ideas.

<chuckle> He promises a small invention every ten days, and a big one every six months.

## Part 4B

In eighteen seventy-seven, he shouts into a machine and records his own voice on a cylinder wrapped in tin foil. He recites "Mary Had a Little Lamb". <gasp> And the machine plays it back.

The phonograph. People are stunned. They call him "the Wizard of Menlo Park".

Two years later, after testing thousands of materials for the filament, <laugh> including hair from a friend's beard, he makes an electric light bulb that burns for hours.

On New Year's Eve, eighteen seventy-nine, he lights up Menlo Park with electric bulbs. Special trains bring crowds to see it.

## Part 4C

Then Edison moves to a bigger lab in West Orange. There, his team builds one of the first movie cameras.

And in eighteen ninety-three, they build the first movie studio in the world. <chuckle> A black tar-paper shack that turns on a giant turntable to follow the sun. They call it the Black Maria.

<short pause> For years, Fort Lee, New Jersey, is the movie capital of America. Then filmmakers move to California, for the sunshine. <laugh> And to get away from Edison's lawyers.

## Part 5: Shore and Sky

Meanwhile, on the coast, Atlantic City builds the first boardwalk in America, in eighteen seventy. <chuckle> So people stop tracking sand into the hotels.

It becomes America's playground. Saltwater taffy. Diving horses that jump off a high platform into a pool. The Miss America pageant. And in the nineteen-thirties, a board game uses Atlantic City's street names. Boardwalk. Park Place. Monopoly.

In nineteen thirty-three, in Camden, a man named Richard Hollingshead opens the first drive-in movie theatre.

 <short pause> He'd tested it in his own driveway, with a projector on his car and a sheet nailed between trees.

## Part 5B

New Jersey has a strange thing about gas stations. In nineteen forty-nine, it passes a law. Only attendants can pump your gas. <chuckle> Lawmakers say it's for safety. Today, it's the last state in America where you still can't pump your own.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

On May sixth, nineteen thirty-seven, the giant German airship Hindenburg arrives at Lakehurst, New Jersey.

<breath> As it comes in to land, it bursts into flames. In about half a minute, it's gone.

<long pause> Thirty-six people are killed. A radio reporter cries on the air, "Oh, the humanity!"

## Part 5D

A year later, on Halloween eve, a radio play called "The War of the Worlds" tells listeners that Martians have landed in Grover's Mill, New Jersey.

<laugh> Some listeners believe it. Newspapers make the panic sound much bigger than it really was. But for one night, New Jersey was being invaded by aliens.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

In March nineteen thirty-two, the baby son of the famous pilot Charles Lindbergh is kidnapped from his family's house near Hopewell, New Jersey. A ladder is found under the window.

<breath> The family pays a ransom. <long pause> Weeks later, the baby is found dead in the woods nearby.

The case leads Congress to make kidnapping across state lines a federal crime.

## Part 5F (quiet style)

In the nineteen-twenties, young women at a factory in Orange paint glowing watch dials with radium paint. They're told it's safe. <breath> They're told to point the brushes with their lips.

<long pause> Many of them sicken and die. The Radium Girls sue. Their case helps create new rules to protect workers.

## Part 6: Labs

After the war, New Jersey keeps inventing.

At Bell Labs, in Murray Hill, scientists invent the transistor in nineteen forty-seven. <gasp> The tiny switch inside every computer and phone today.

<short pause> In nineteen fifty-six, a trucking man named Malcolm McLean loads fifty-eight metal boxes onto an old tanker at Port Newark, and sails it to Houston. <chuckle> It doesn't look like much.

It's the first successful container ship. Today almost everything you own travelled in one of those boxes. And Port Newark is one of the busiest ports in the country.

In nineteen sixty-four, two Bell Labs scientists in Holmdel hear a strange hiss in their giant antenna. They think it's pigeon droppings. <chuckle> They clean it out. The hiss stays.

It turns out to be the leftover heat of the Big Bang. They win the Nobel Prize.

And in Princeton, Albert Einstein spends the last twenty-two years of his life. <chuckle> He walks to work every day, with messy hair and no socks.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In July nineteen sixty-seven, after police beat a Black taxi driver in Newark, the city erupts. <long pause> Twenty-six people die.

## Part 6C

New Jersey also gives America the New Jersey Turnpike, more diners than almost anywhere, Frank Sinatra from Hoboken, Whitney Houston from Newark, and Bruce Springsteen.

<short pause> And from Princeton comes Paul Robeson. The son of a man who escaped slavery, he becomes an all-American football star at Rutgers, a law graduate, a great actor, and one of the most famous singers in the world. <sigh> In the nineteen-fifties, the government takes away his passport, for his politics. He fights for years to get it back.

<short pause> In the Pine Barrens, a farmer's daughter named Elizabeth White works with a scientist to tame the wild blueberry. In nineteen sixteen, they sell the first cultivated blueberries. <chuckle> Almost every blueberry in your supermarket descends from their work.

<laugh> And here's a fight New Jersey won. For years, New York and New Jersey argued over who owns Ellis Island. In nineteen ninety-eight, the Supreme Court decided: most of it is in New Jersey.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

In October twenty twelve, Hurricane Sandy slams into the Jersey Shore. <breath> Boardwalks are smashed. Houses are swept off their foundations. A roller coaster in Seaside Heights ends up standing in the ocean.

## Part 6E

Today, New Jersey is the most densely populated state in the country. And it's still a giant centre for medicine, science and chemistry. Many of the world's biggest drug companies have their American headquarters here.

<chuckle> And you still can't pump your own gas.

## Part 6F

So how does a small state, squeezed between New York and Philadelphia, end up inventing the modern world?

<short pause> It starts with a waterfall Alexander Hamilton wanted to put to work.

Hamilton made Paterson the first planned factory city. New Jersey filled with mills, machines and skilled workers, right between two of the biggest markets in America. So when Edison wanted to build a factory for inventions, he built it here. And the labs that followed him built the electronic age.

<long pause> New Jersey was always the place people passed through.

<chuckle> It just turns out a lot of the future passed through it first.
