## Hook

The Ferris wheel? <short pause> Invented for a fair in Chicago. Twinkies? Invented near Chicago.

The Chicago River flows backwards. On purpose. Engineers reversed it.

And the name "Chicago" probably comes from a Native word for a wild onion. A smelly one.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-one, the whole centre of the city burned to the ground.

And yet, Chicago went from a muddy village of about two hundred people... to more than a million, in less than sixty years.

<short pause> So how does a swamp named after a wild onion become one of the biggest cities on Earth in a single lifetime?

<whispers> It starts with a few miles of mud between two rivers.

## Part 1: Cahokia

Long before Chicago, the biggest city in Illinois is somewhere else.

Around the year eleven hundred, near where St. Louis is today, there's a city called Cahokia. Maybe ten to twenty thousand people. The largest city north of Mexico.

In the middle stands Monks Mound, a giant earthen pyramid, about a hundred feet tall, with a huge wooden building on top. Its base covers more ground than the Great Pyramid of Egypt.

By around thirteen fifty, Cahokia is empty. Nobody is sure why.

Later, the Illinois Confederation, the Potawatomi, the Sauk, the Meskwaki and other nations live on the prairies.

## Part 2: The Portage

In sixteen seventy-three, two Frenchmen, the explorer Louis Jolliet and the priest Jacques Marquette, paddle down the Mississippi River.

On the way back, their Native guides show them a shortcut.

Near Lake Michigan, there's a little river. And a few miles away, through a muddy marsh, there's another river that flows down to the Mississippi.

Drag your canoe across those few miles of mud... and you can go from the Great Lakes all the way to the Gulf of Mexico. Half a continent. By water.

The local people call the place Shikaakwa. After the wild onions growing there.

Jolliet says: dig a canal here, and you could sail from Canada to the Gulf.

It takes almost two hundred years for anyone to do it.

## Part 2B

In the seventeen-eighties, the first non-Native person to settle there builds a trading post at the mouth of the river. His name is Jean Baptiste Point du Sable. A Black man, probably born in Haiti.

He's considered the founder of Chicago.

## Part 2C

In eighteen eighteen, Illinois becomes the twenty-first state.

And here's a little-known trick. The original plan put Illinois's northern border at the bottom tip of Lake Michigan. That would have left Chicago in Wisconsin.

But the territory's delegate to Congress, Nathaniel Pope, gets the border moved about forty miles north. Why? So Illinois gets a shoreline on the lake... and the portage.

<chuckle> Wisconsin is still a little annoyed about that.

## Part 3: Boom Town

In eighteen thirty-three, Chicago becomes a town. About two hundred people. Muddy streets. Wild onions.

And then, in eighteen forty-eight, Jolliet's dream finally comes true. The Illinois and Michigan Canal opens, linking Lake Michigan to the Illinois River and the Mississippi.

That same year, the first railroad arrives. The first telegraph. The Board of Trade opens to sell grain.

Suddenly, Chicago is the place where everything meets. Boats from the east. Rivers to the south. Railroads to the west. Wheat, lumber and cattle pour in. Factories, people and money pour in after them.

Chicago becomes the fastest-growing city in the world.

## Part 3B

The city is so muddy and low that in the eighteen-fifties and sixties, Chicago raises itself. Engineers use thousands of jackscrews to lift entire buildings, some made of brick, several feet into the air, while people keep working inside.

<chuckle> A hotel was lifted while the guests were still staying in it.

## Part 3C

And in Springfield, a tall, funny prairie lawyer is getting into politics. Abraham Lincoln.

In eighteen fifty-eight, he debates Senator Stephen Douglas across Illinois about slavery. He loses that Senate race. Two years later, the Republicans meet in Chicago and pick him for president.

## Part 3D

By eighteen sixty-five, the Union Stock Yards open in Chicago. Millions of cattle and hogs arrive by train every year, to be slaughtered and shipped east in refrigerated rail cars.

Chicago becomes the "hog butcher for the world".

## Part 4: Fire

But Chicago is built almost entirely of wood. Wooden houses. Wooden sidewalks. Even wooden streets.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

On the night of October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, a fire starts in a barn on the West Side.

Strong winds carry it across the river. For more than a day, it burns through the heart of the city.

About three hundred people die. A hundred thousand are left homeless.

A newspaper blamed Mrs. O'Leary's cow for kicking over a lantern. Years later, the reporter admitted he made that part up.

## Part 4C

And Chicago rebuilds. Fast. Bigger. And this time, in stone, brick and steel.

In eighteen eighty-five, the Home Insurance Building rises with a steel frame inside. It's often called the world's first skyscraper.

In eighteen ninety-three, Chicago throws the world's biggest party. The World's Columbian Exposition. A gleaming "White City" lit by electric lights.

And to beat the Eiffel Tower from Paris, an engineer named George Ferris builds a giant turning wheel, two hundred and sixty-four feet tall.

The Ferris wheel.

By eighteen ninety, Chicago has more than a million people.

## Part 5: Big Shoulders

But Chicago has a dirty secret. Its sewage flows into the river. The river flows into Lake Michigan. And Lake Michigan is where the city gets its drinking water.

So in nineteen hundred, Chicago does something nobody had done before. It digs a giant canal and makes the river flow backwards. Away from the lake. Towards the Mississippi.

Through the old portage route.

<chuckle> The people downstream in St. Louis were not happy.

## Part 5B

Chicago is also a city of hard work and big fights.

In eighteen eighty-six, at Haymarket Square, a rally for the eight-hour work day ends with a bomb and gunfire. It's one reason why much of the world marks May Day as a day for workers.

Jane Addams opens Hull House to help poor immigrant families, and later wins the Nobel Peace Prize.

In the Great Migration, hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners move to Chicago for jobs and freedom. They bring the blues with them, and plug in electric guitars.

## Part 5C

In the nineteen-twenties, alcohol is banned. And Chicago's gangsters, like Al Capone, get rich selling it anyway.

## Part 5D

And on December second, nineteen forty-two, under the stands of an old football field at the University of Chicago, scientists led by Enrico Fermi create the first controlled nuclear chain reaction. The atomic age begins. In a squash court.

## Part 6: Today

After the war, in nineteen fifty-five, a milkshake-machine salesman named Ray Kroc opens his first McDonald's in Des Plaines, just outside Chicago, and turns it into a giant chain.

In nineteen seventy-three, the Sears Tower becomes the tallest building in the world.

In two thousand eight, a former Chicago community organiser, Barack Obama, is elected the first Black president of the United States.

And in two thousand sixteen, the Chicago Cubs win the World Series. For the first time in a hundred and eight years.

## Part 6B

So how does a swamp named after a wild onion become one of the biggest cities on Earth in a single lifetime?

It starts with a few miles of mud between two rivers.

That mud joined the Great Lakes to the Mississippi. Illinois moved its border just to get it. A canal followed it, then the railroads, then the whole country's grain, lumber and cattle.

And when the city needed to save itself, it sent its river backwards, down the very same path.

<short pause> Chicago didn't grow because of where it was.

<chuckle> It grew because of where everything else was going. Through the mud.
