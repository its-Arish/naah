# The Entire History of Illinois

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,400 spoken words, so roughly 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen seventy-one"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Curious, then wry. |
| Part 3 | Fast, booming, big-city energy. |
| Part 4 | Tense, then rebuilding with pride. |
| Part 5 | Brisk and cheeky, then serious. |
| Part 6 | Modern, confident; calm at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Cahokia 1050 to 1350 · The Portage 1673 to 1818 · Prairie State 1818 to 1848 · Boom Town 1848 to 1871 · Fire 1871 · White City 1871 to 1900 · Big Shoulders 1900 to 1945 · Today 1945 to now.

## Hook

The Ferris wheel? <short pause> Invented for a fair in Chicago. Twinkies? Invented near Chicago.

The Chicago River flows backwards. <chuckle> On purpose. Engineers reversed it.

And the name "Chicago" probably comes from a Native word for a wild onion. A smelly one.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-one, the whole centre of the city burned to the ground.

And yet, Chicago went from a muddy village of about two hundred people... to more than a million, in less than sixty years.

<short pause> So how does a swamp named after a wild onion become one of the biggest cities on Earth in a single lifetime?

<whispers> It starts with a few miles of mud between two rivers.

## Part 1: Cahokia

Long before Chicago, the biggest city in Illinois is somewhere else.

Around the year eleven hundred, near where St. Louis is today, there's a city called Cahokia. Maybe ten to twenty thousand people. <gasp> The largest city north of Mexico. For a while, bigger than London.

In the middle stands Monks Mound, a giant earthen pyramid, about a hundred feet tall, with a huge wooden building on top. Its base covers more ground than the Great Pyramid of Egypt.

<short pause> Nearby, they build a giant circle of wooden posts. When the sun rises behind certain posts, they know the season has changed. Archaeologists call it Woodhenge.

<long pause> By around thirteen fifty, Cahokia is empty. Nobody is sure why.

Later, the Illinois Confederation, the Potawatomi, the Sauk, the Meskwaki, the Kickapoo and other nations live on the prairies.

<short pause> On a sandstone cliff above the Illinois River, there's a place called Starved Rock. <sigh> The legend says a band of Illinois people, trapped on top by their enemies in the seventeen-sixties, held out there until they starved.

## Part 1B

And the prairies are something else. Tall grass, taller than a man on horseback. Flowers. Endless sky. <short pause> And under it, some of the richest soil in the world. Black, deep and heavy.

## Part 2: The Portage

In sixteen seventy-three, two Frenchmen, the explorer Louis Jolliet and the priest Jacques Marquette, paddle down the Mississippi River.

On the way back, their Native guides show them a shortcut.

Near Lake Michigan, there's a little river. And a few miles away, through a muddy marsh, there's another river that flows down to the Mississippi.

<gasp> Drag your canoe across those few miles of mud... and you can go from the Great Lakes all the way to the Gulf of Mexico. Half a continent. By water.

The local people call the place Shikaakwa. After the wild onions growing there.

<short pause> Jolliet says: dig a canal here, and you could sail from Canada to the Gulf.

<chuckle> It takes almost two hundred years for anyone to do it.

## Part 2B

French villages grow up along the Mississippi. Kaskaskia. Cahokia village. A stone fort called Fort de Chartres.

And in the seventeen-eighties, the first non-Native person to settle at the onion place builds a trading post at the mouth of the river. His name is Jean Baptiste Point du Sable. A Black man, probably born in Haiti. <short pause> He's considered the founder of Chicago.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In eighteen-oh-three, the army builds Fort Dearborn there.

<breath> In the War of eighteen twelve, the soldiers and families are ordered to leave. As they march out along the lake shore, Potawatomi warriors, allied with the British, attack.

<long pause> Dozens are killed. The fort is burned.

## Part 2D

In eighteen eighteen, Illinois becomes the twenty-first state.

<chuckle> And here's a little-known trick. The original plan put Illinois's northern border at the bottom tip of Lake Michigan. That would have left Chicago in what became Wisconsin.

But the territory's delegate to Congress, Nathaniel Pope, gets the border moved about forty miles north. Why? <short pause> So Illinois gets a shoreline on the lake... and the portage.

<laugh> Wisconsin is still a little annoyed about that.

## Part 2E

In eighteen thirty-two, the Sauk leader Black Hawk crosses back into Illinois to reclaim his people's homeland. The army chases him. One of the young volunteer captains is a tall man from New Salem named Abraham Lincoln.

<chuckle> He never sees a battle. He later jokes that he had "a good many bloody struggles with the mosquitoes".

## Part 2F (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-seven, in Alton, a newspaper editor named Elijah Lovejoy prints articles against slavery. Mobs throw his printing presses in the river. Three times.

<long pause> When a fourth press arrives, a mob attacks the warehouse where he's guarding it. Lovejoy is shot and killed.

## Part 2G (quiet style)

In the eighteen-forties, members of the Latter-day Saints church build a city on the Mississippi called Nauvoo. For a while, it's one of the biggest cities in Illinois.

<breath> In eighteen forty-four, their leader, Joseph Smith, is jailed in the nearby town of Carthage. A mob storms the jail and kills him. The Saints leave Illinois, and walk west.

## Part 3: Boom Town

In eighteen thirty-three, Chicago becomes a town. About two hundred people. Muddy streets. Wild onions.

<short pause> And then, in eighteen forty-eight, Jolliet's dream finally comes true. The Illinois and Michigan Canal opens, linking Lake Michigan to the Illinois River and the Mississippi.

That same year, the first railroad arrives. The first telegraph. The Board of Trade opens to sell grain.

<gasp> Suddenly, Chicago is the place where everything meets. Boats from the east. Rivers to the south. Railroads to the west. Wheat, lumber and cattle pour in. Factories, people and money pour in after them.

Chicago becomes the fastest-growing city in the world.

<short pause> A businessman named George Pullman builds luxury sleeping cars for the trains. Soft beds. Carpets. Fine dining. Thousands of Black men work on them as Pullman porters. <short pause> Later, they form one of the first great Black labour unions in America.

## Part 3B

And the farmers need a new plough. The prairie soil is so thick and sticky that old iron ploughs clog up every few steps.

<short pause> In eighteen thirty-seven, in the little town of Grand Detour, a blacksmith named John Deere makes a plough from a broken steel saw blade. <chuckle> The sticky soil slides right off it.

John Deere's plough breaks open the prairie. And his company still makes tractors in Illinois.

## Part 3C

The city is so muddy and low that in the eighteen-fifties and sixties, Chicago raises itself. Engineers use thousands of jackscrews to lift entire buildings, some made of brick, several feet into the air. <gasp> While people keep working inside.

<laugh> A hotel was lifted while the guests were still staying in it.

## Part 3D

And in Springfield, a tall, funny prairie lawyer is getting into politics. Abraham Lincoln.

In eighteen fifty-eight, he debates Senator Stephen Douglas across Illinois about slavery. Seven debates. Thousands of people stand for hours to listen. <short pause> He loses that Senate race.

<short pause> But a speech he gave in Springfield, at the start of that campaign, is still quoted today. "A house divided against itself cannot stand." <short pause> The country, he says, can't stay half slave and half free forever.

Two years later, the Republicans meet in Chicago, in a wooden hall called the Wigwam, and pick him for president.

<short pause> In the war that follows, the Union's greatest general also comes from Illinois. A former leather-store clerk from Galena, Ulysses S. Grant.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

In April eighteen sixty-five, days after the war ends, Lincoln is assassinated.

<breath> A funeral train carries his body back home, slowly, through city after city. Millions of people line the tracks, day and night.

<long pause> In May, it reaches Springfield. He's buried there, in the prairie he came from.

## Part 3F

In eighteen sixty-five, the Union Stock Yards open in Chicago. Millions of cattle and hogs arrive by train every year, to be slaughtered, packed and shipped east, later in refrigerated rail cars.

<chuckle> The meat packers say they use every part of the pig... except the squeal.

Chicago becomes the "hog butcher for the world".

## Part 4: Fire

But Chicago is built almost entirely of wood. Wooden houses. Wooden sidewalks. Even wooden streets.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

On the night of October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, after a long dry summer, a fire starts in a barn on the West Side.

<breath> Strong winds carry it across the river. For more than a day, it burns through the heart of the city.

<long pause> About three hundred people die. A hundred thousand are left homeless.

A newspaper blamed Mrs. O'Leary's cow for kicking over a lantern. <short pause> Years later, the reporter admitted he made that part up.

## Part 4C

And Chicago rebuilds. Fast. Bigger. And this time, in stone, brick and steel.

In eighteen eighty-five, the Home Insurance Building rises with a steel frame inside. <gasp> It's often called the world's first skyscraper.

In eighteen ninety-three, Chicago throws the world's biggest party. The World's Columbian Exposition. A gleaming "White City", lit by electric lights.

And to beat the Eiffel Tower from Paris, an engineer named George Ferris builds a giant turning wheel, two hundred and sixty-four feet tall.

<short pause> The Ferris wheel.

<chuckle> Visitors also taste a new caramel popcorn, and a new chewing gum. Cracker Jack and Juicy Fruit.

By eighteen ninety, Chicago has more than a million people.

<short pause> The fair's chief architect, Daniel Burnham, then draws up a giant plan for the whole city. "Make no little plans," he says. <short pause> Thanks to that plan, and to a stubborn shopkeeper who sued to stop buildings going up on the shore, most of Chicago's lakefront is still public parks and beaches. Not buildings.

## Part 5: Big Shoulders

But Chicago has a dirty secret. Its sewage flows into the river. The river flows into Lake Michigan. <groan> And Lake Michigan is where the city gets its drinking water.

So in nineteen hundred, Chicago does something nobody had done before. It digs a giant canal and makes the river flow backwards. Away from the lake. Towards the Mississippi.

<short pause> Through the old portage route.

<laugh> The people downstream in St. Louis were not happy. They sued.

## Part 5B

Chicago is also a city of hard work and big fights.

In eighteen eighty-six, at Haymarket Square, a rally for the eight-hour work day ends with a bomb and gunfire. It's one reason why much of the world marks May Day as a day for workers.

<short pause> In eighteen ninety-four, workers at George Pullman's railroad-car company town go on strike. Railroads across the country stop. The president sends in the army.

Jane Addams opens Hull House to help poor immigrant families, and later becomes the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

## Part 5C

In the Great Migration, hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners move to Chicago, for jobs and freedom. A Chicago newspaper, the Chicago Defender, is smuggled into the South by railroad porters, telling people to come north.

<short pause> They bring the blues with them. And they plug in electric guitars.

<short pause> Louis Armstrong arrives from New Orleans in nineteen twenty-two, and makes some of the greatest jazz records ever cut, in Chicago. Later, two brothers start a record label called Chess, and record Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf and Chuck Berry. <chuckle> The Rolling Stones fly over just to record in the same studio.

In nineteen fifty, a Chicago poet, Gwendolyn Brooks, becomes the first Black writer to win the Pulitzer Prize.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In the summer of nineteen nineteen, a Black teenager named Eugene Williams swims across an invisible line at a segregated beach on Lake Michigan. White men throw rocks at him. He drowns.

<long pause> A week of riots follows. Thirty-eight people are killed.

## Part 5E

In the nineteen-twenties, alcohol is banned. And Chicago's gangsters, like Al Capone, get rich selling it anyway. <chuckle> Capone is finally sent to prison... for not paying his taxes.

<sigh> Before that, in February nineteen twenty-nine, his rivals are lined up against a garage wall and shot. Seven men. The Saint Valentine's Day Massacre.

<short pause> Chicago's food gets famous too. In nineteen thirty, a bakery manager in a Chicago suburb invents a little sponge cake with cream inside, to sell during the strawberry off-season. <chuckle> The Twinkie. And in nineteen forty-three, a Chicago restaurant bakes pizza in a deep pan, piled high with cheese and sauce. Deep dish.

<chuckle> People say Chicago is called the Windy City because of the wind off the lake. But many historians think it's really because of its boastful politicians. Full of hot air.

## Part 5F

And on December second, nineteen forty-two, under the stands of an old football field at the University of Chicago, scientists led by Enrico Fermi create the first controlled nuclear chain reaction. <short pause> The atomic age begins. In a squash court.

## Part 6: Today

After the war, in nineteen fifty-five, a milkshake-machine salesman named Ray Kroc opens his first McDonald's in Des Plaines, just outside Chicago, and turns it into a giant chain.

<gasp> In nineteen seventy-three, the Sears Tower becomes the tallest building in the world.

In the nineteen-nineties, Michael Jordan and the Chicago Bulls win six championships.

In two thousand eight, a former Chicago community organiser, Barack Obama, is elected the first Black president of the United States.

<short pause> Forty years earlier, in nineteen sixty-eight, Chicago hosted a very different political moment. During the Democratic convention, police and anti-war protesters clash in the streets, live on television. <short pause> The whole world is watching.

<short pause> For twenty-five years, Oprah Winfrey films her talk show in Chicago, and becomes one of the most famous people on Earth.

<chuckle> And every Saint Patrick's Day since nineteen sixty-two, Chicago dyes its river bright green. The same river it once turned around.

<laugh> And in two thousand sixteen, the Chicago Cubs win the World Series. For the first time in a hundred and eight years.

<short pause> Today, the old Route sixty-six still starts in downtown Chicago. And out on the prairie, Illinois grows more soybeans than almost any state on Earth.

## Part 6B

So how does a swamp named after a wild onion become one of the biggest cities on Earth in a single lifetime?

<short pause> It starts with a few miles of mud between two rivers.

That mud joined the Great Lakes to the Mississippi. Illinois moved its border just to get it. A canal followed it, then the railroads, then the whole country's grain, lumber and cattle.

And when the city needed to save itself, it sent its river backwards, down the very same path.

<long pause> Chicago didn't grow because of where it was.

<chuckle> It grew because of where everything else was going. Through the mud.
