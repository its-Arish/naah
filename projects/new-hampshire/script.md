# The Entire History of New Hampshire

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,300 spoken words, so roughly 12 to 13 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen eighty-six"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, granite-and-pine storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Folksy, stubborn town-meeting voice, with a wink. |

| Part 4 | Busy mill-town energy, then windswept wonder. |

| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

## Hook

New Hampshire's motto is "Live Free or Die". <short pause> It's on every licence plate. <chuckle> And the licence plates are made by prisoners.

New Hampshire has four hundred state representatives. One of the biggest legislatures in the English-speaking world. They're paid a hundred dollars a year.

For decades, the fastest wind ever recorded on Earth was measured on a New Hampshire mountain. Two hundred and thirty-one miles an hour.

<long pause> In nineteen eighty-six, a New Hampshire schoolteacher, Christa McAuliffe, set out to become the first teacher in space. Seventy-three seconds after launch, the shuttle broke apart.

<short pause> So how does a small state of rocky farms end up with one of the biggest governments on Earth, and the first say in choosing a president?

<whispers> It starts with a town meeting.

## Part 1: First Peoples

New Hampshire is granite. Mountains, forests, lakes, and lots and lots of rocks. <chuckle> Farmers pull so many stones out of their fields that they pile them into walls. Thousands of miles of old stone walls still wind through New Hampshire's woods.

That's why it's called the Granite State.

<short pause> The glaciers of the Ice Age scraped the mountains, and left behind deep, narrow valleys called notches. And, high on one cliff, a set of granite ledges that, from just the right angle, look like the profile of an old man's face.

<short pause> Surveyors first notice it in eighteen-oh-five.

## Part 1B

For thousands of years, New Hampshire is home to the Abenaki and the Pennacook. They fish for salmon and shad at the falls of the Merrimack River, and hunt in the White Mountains.

<short pause> A great Pennacook leader named Passaconaway is said to live for nearly a hundred years. Legend says he could make water burn and dead leaves turn green. <sigh> When English settlers arrive, he tells his people that fighting them would be hopeless, and to keep the peace.

## Part 2: Town Meetings

In sixteen twenty-three, English fishermen settle at Portsmouth and Dover, on the short stretch of New Hampshire coast. <chuckle> It's only about eighteen miles long. The shortest coastline of any ocean state.

One of the colony's founders, John Mason, names it after his home county in England. Hampshire. <tsk> He never even visits.

## Part 2B

The settlers live in small, separate towns. Each one runs itself.

Once a year, every man in town gathers in the meeting house. They argue. They vote. <laugh> On roads, schools, taxes, and whose pigs are running loose.

The town meeting. No king, no governor, just neighbours deciding together.

<chuckle> Many New Hampshire towns still do it today. Every March.

## Part 2C

For a while, New Hampshire is ruled by Massachusetts. In sixteen seventy-nine, it becomes its own royal colony.

<gasp> Its forests are full of giant white pines. Some more than two hundred feet tall. Perfect for the masts of the Royal Navy's warships.

So the king's men mark the biggest trees with an arrow, cut into the bark. The King's Broad Arrow. Those trees belong to the king. <tsk> Anyone who cuts one down gets fined.

New Hampshire's loggers hate it. In seventeen seventy-two, when a sheriff comes to fine some sawmill owners in the town of Weare, a group of men with blackened faces drag him out of his inn, and beat him with tree switches. One lash for every log. <chuckle> Then they shave his horse's mane and tail, and send him riding out of town. The Pine Tree Riot.

## Part 3: Live Free or Die

In December seventeen seventy-four, Paul Revere makes a ride that's much less famous than his other one. <chuckle> He gallops to Portsmouth, through the snow, to warn that British soldiers are coming to guard the fort there.

<gasp> So hundreds of New Hampshire men raid that British fort, Fort William and Mary, and carry away its gunpowder. Months before Lexington and Concord. One of the very first acts of rebellion.

In January seventeen seventy-six, New Hampshire becomes the first colony to set up its own government, completely independent of Britain.

<short pause> And in Portsmouth, shipbuilders launch the Ranger, a warship for a young captain named John Paul Jones.

## Part 3B

One of its heroes is a tough frontier soldier named John Stark. <short pause> As a young man, he was captured by Abenaki warriors, and made to run between two lines of men hitting him with sticks. <laugh> He grabbed a stick and started hitting them back. They were so impressed that they adopted him.

He fights at Bunker Hill. And in seventeen seventy-seven, he wins the Battle of Bennington.

Years later, old General Stark is invited to an anniversary celebration. He's too sick to go. So he sends a toast to be read out.

<whispers> "Live free or die. Death is not the worst of evils."

<short pause> In nineteen forty-five, New Hampshire makes it the state motto.

## Part 3C

In seventeen eighty-eight, the new U.S. Constitution needs nine states to approve it before it can take effect. Eight have said yes.

<gasp> On June twenty-first, New Hampshire votes yes. The ninth state. The one that makes the Constitution real.

## Part 3D

And when New Hampshire writes its own government, every town wants its own voice in the state House of Representatives. Even the smallest.

So the House just keeps growing. <laugh> At one point, it has more than four hundred and forty members. Today it has four hundred. For a state of about one point four million people. That's roughly one lawmaker for every three and a half thousand people.

<chuckle> And they get paid a hundred dollars a year. Not a hundred dollars a day. A year.

<short pause> New Hampshire also has no sales tax, and no tax on wages. <laugh> It's very proud of that.

## Part 4: Mills and Mountains

In seventeen sixty-nine, a minister founds a college in the New Hampshire woods, he says to educate young Native Americans. Dartmouth. <short pause> In fact, it teaches mostly young white men.

<gasp> In eighteen eighteen, New Hampshire tries to take it over. A Dartmouth graduate, Daniel Webster, argues the case at the Supreme Court. He wins. At the end, it's said, he chokes up. "It is, sir, a small college. And yet there are those who love it."

## Part 4B

In the eighteen hundreds, New Hampshire's rivers power giant mills. In Manchester, the Amoskeag mills grow into one of the biggest cotton-textile factories in the world. Miles of brick buildings along the river. Tens of thousands of workers. Young women from farms, then immigrants from Quebec, Ireland and Greece.

<short pause> In Concord, a company builds the toughest stagecoaches in the world. The Concord coach. Its body hangs on thick leather straps, so it rocks instead of bouncing. <chuckle> Concord coaches carry mail and passengers across the whole American West. The ones in cowboy movies are usually Concord coaches.

Franklin Pierce, from New Hampshire, becomes president in eighteen fifty-three.

<sigh> In nineteen twenty-two, Amoskeag cuts wages and lengthens hours. The workers strike for nine months. <short pause> In nineteen thirty-five, the giant mill shuts down for good. Manchester's whole economy is shaken.

## Part 4C

<short pause> In the little town of Newport, New Hampshire, a widow named Sarah Josepha Hale, raising five children, starts writing. <chuckle> She writes a little poem about a girl and a lamb that follows her to school. "Mary Had a Little Lamb."

<gasp> Then she becomes the editor of the most popular women's magazine in America. And for seventeen years, she writes letter after letter to presidents, asking for a national day of thanks. <short pause> In eighteen sixty-three, Abraham Lincoln finally agrees. Thanksgiving.

## Part 4D

In the White Mountains, that rock face becomes famous. The Old Man of the Mountain. <short pause> Daniel Webster supposedly says that God hung it out as a sign. In New Hampshire, He makes men.

And on top of Mount Washington, the highest peak in the Northeast, the weather can be the worst in the world. Storms from three directions collide there.

<chuckle> In eighteen sixty-nine, a railway with a toothed middle rail starts dragging little steam engines up the mountain. The first mountain-climbing cog railway in the world. In places, it's one of the steepest railway tracks on Earth.

## Part 4E

<gasp> On April twelfth, nineteen thirty-four, scientists at the summit observatory measure a gust of two hundred and thirty-one miles an hour. For sixty-two years, it's the fastest wind ever recorded on Earth.

<laugh> Their wooden building has to be tied to the mountain with chains.

<sigh> More than a hundred and fifty people have died on Mount Washington. Hikers set out on a sunny morning, and are caught by snow and hurricane winds by afternoon. Even in summer.

## Part 4F

In nineteen forty-four, leaders from forty-four countries meet at a grand old hotel in Bretton Woods, in the shadow of Mount Washington. They plan how the world's money will work after the war. <short pause> They create the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

<short pause> Forty years earlier, in nineteen-oh-five, Russia and Japan send diplomats to Portsmouth to end a war between them. President Theodore Roosevelt brings them together. <gasp> The Treaty of Portsmouth wins him the Nobel Peace Prize. The first American to win it.

## Part 5: Granite State

On a New Hampshire farm, the poet Robert Frost writes about birches, stone walls and snowy woods. "Good fences make good neighbours."

<chuckle> In nineteen sixty-one, a New Hampshire couple, Betty and Barney Hill, say they were taken aboard a UFO on a lonely mountain road. It becomes one of the first famous "alien abduction" stories in America.

<gasp> The same year, a Navy pilot from Derry, New Hampshire, Alan Shepard, becomes the first American in space. <laugh> Ten years later, on the Moon, he hits two golf balls.

And a New Hampshire inventor named Earl Tupper makes plastic bowls with a lid that burps. Tupperware.

<gasp> In nineteen sixty-four, at Dartmouth, two professors invent a simple programming language, so that ordinary students, not just scientists, can use a computer. BASIC. <short pause> A generation of kids later learn to program with it, including the founders of Microsoft.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-seven, more than fourteen hundred protesters are arrested for occupying the building site of a nuclear power plant at Seabrook, on the coast. <tsk> They're held in National Guard armouries for two weeks. The plant gets built anyway.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In nineteen eighty-five, NASA chooses a social studies teacher from Concord, New Hampshire, to be the first teacher in space. Christa McAuliffe. She plans to teach lessons from orbit.

<breath> On January twenty-eighth, nineteen eighty-six, millions of students watch the launch of the space shuttle Challenger.

<long pause> Seventy-three seconds after liftoff, it breaks apart. All seven astronauts are killed.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In May two thousand three, the Old Man of the Mountain, the face that had watched over the valley for thousands of years, collapses into rubble.

<breath> It's still on New Hampshire's road signs. And its quarter.

## Part 6: First Primary

Now, the town meeting, again.

In nineteen-sixteen, New Hampshire holds its first presidential primary, so voters can choose the parties' candidates. To save money, it's held on the same day as the March town meetings. <short pause> By nineteen-twenty, that makes it the first presidential primary in the country.

<chuckle> And New Hampshire loves being first. So much that it passes a law. The primary must be held before any other similar contest. If another state moves earlier... New Hampshire moves earlier too.

## Part 6B

So candidates come to New Hampshire. And they have to do things the New Hampshire way. Small diners. Living rooms. Town halls. <short pause> Answering hard questions from people who are used to arguing at town meetings.

<gasp> In nineteen sixty-eight, an anti-war senator almost beats the sitting president, Lyndon Johnson, in New Hampshire. Weeks later, Johnson announces he won't run again.

<chuckle> In nineteen-eighty, at a debate in Nashua, an organiser tries to switch off Ronald Reagan's microphone. Reagan grabs it. "I am paying for this microphone!" he says. The crowd roars. He wins New Hampshire, and the presidency.

<laugh> In the tiny village of Dixville Notch, everyone votes at the stroke of midnight, and the results are announced minutes later.

## Part 6C

<tsk> But not everyone loves the tradition. For twenty twenty-eight, the national Democratic Party puts South Carolina and Nevada ahead of New Hampshire. <short pause> New Hampshire's law still says it goes first. The Republicans still start there, with Iowa. The tug of war goes on.

## Part 6D

So how does a small state of rocky farms end up with one of the biggest governments on Earth, and the first say in choosing a president?

<whispers> It starts with a town meeting.

For four hundred years, New Hampshire's towns have governed themselves, face to face. Every town wanted its own voice in the State House, so the House grew to four hundred. <short pause> And when America started choosing presidents with primaries, New Hampshire made sure its town-meeting voters got to ask first.

<breath> Live free or die. And, apparently, vote first.

<chuckle> Even at midnight.
