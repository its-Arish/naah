## Hook

The pineapple on Hawaiian pizza? <short pause> Invented in Canada. And the pineapple isn't from Hawaii either.

The ukulele? Brought to the islands by Portuguese immigrants, in eighteen seventy-nine.

Surfing? That one really IS Hawaiian. Kings did it.

And in Honolulu, there's a royal palace that had electric lights... years before the White House.

<long pause> Because Hawaii was a kingdom. With a queen. Until eighteen ninety-three, when a group of businessmen, backed by American Marines, took it from her.

<short pause> So how does an island kingdom, in the middle of the biggest ocean on Earth, become the fiftieth state?

<whispers> It starts with a spoonful of sugar.

## Part 1: First Voyagers

Look at a globe. Hawaii is the most isolated group of islands on the planet. More than two thousand miles from the nearest continent.

The islands were born from fire. Volcanoes, rising from the sea floor, one after another, over millions of years. They're still doing it. The Big Island is still growing.

For most of their history, no human had ever stood on them.

Then, roughly a thousand years ago, double-hulled canoes appear on the horizon.

The people on board are Polynesians. They've sailed thousands of miles of open ocean, from islands to the south, without a compass, without a map. They read the stars. The swells. The birds. The colour of the clouds.

It's one of the greatest feats of navigation in human history.

They bring everything they need for a new life: taro, sweet potato, coconut, bananas, pigs, dogs, chickens.

And they build a whole civilisation. Chiefs called aliʻi. A strict system of sacred laws called kapu. Fishponds. Farms terraced down the valleys from the mountains to the sea.

And on the waves, they ride boards. He'e nalu. Wave sliding. The chiefs ride the longest boards of all.

## Part 2: Strangers

In January seventeen seventy-eight, two British ships appear off the island of Kauaʻi. The commander is Captain James Cook.

He names them the Sandwich Islands, after an English earl.

A year later, Cook returns, and arrives at Kealakekua Bay during a long festival for the god Lono. He's welcomed with enormous ceremony.

But when he comes back again, after a broken mast, things go wrong. A boat is stolen. Cook tries to take a high chief hostage. A fight breaks out on the beach.

In February seventeen seventy-nine, Captain Cook is killed.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The ships bring something far deadlier than cannons.

Diseases the islanders have never met. Measles. Influenza. Smallpox.

Before Cook arrived, there may have been three hundred thousand people in the islands. Maybe many more.

A century later, fewer than forty-five thousand Native Hawaiians are left.

## Part 3: Kingdom

In those same years, a young chief from the Big Island is rising. Kamehameha.

He's a warrior and a strategist. He buys Western guns and ships, and he hires Western sailors.

Island by island, he wins. And by eighteen ten, Kamehameha the Great rules all of the islands. One kingdom. The Kingdom of Hawaiʻi.

It's recognised by Britain, by France, by the United States. It signs treaties. It has embassies. It has a flag... which still has a British Union Jack in the corner today.

After Kamehameha dies, change comes fast. In eighteen nineteen, his widow Kaʻahumanu and his son sit down and eat together, men and women at the same meal. Under the old kapu laws, that was forbidden.

The old system falls. And the very next year, American missionaries arrive.

They create a written Hawaiian alphabet. Within a few decades, Hawaii has one of the highest literacy rates in the world.

Then the whalers come. Hundreds of ships a year stop in Lahaina and Honolulu. Sailors. Merchants. And businessmen who notice that the soil and the sun here are perfect for one crop.

## Part 4: Sugar

Sugar.

In eighteen forty-eight, a land reform called the Great Māhele lets land be bought and sold for the first time. Foreigners buy huge plantations.

But sugar needs two things: workers, and customers.

The workers come from across the world. Chinese. Then Japanese. Portuguese from Madeira and the Azores, who bring a little guitar that the islanders nickname the ukulele. Later Koreans and Filipinos.

That's why Hawaii today is a mix like nowhere else.

The customers are in America. But America taxes foreign sugar.

So in eighteen seventy-five, King Kalākaua signs a deal with the United States. Hawaiian sugar can enter America tax-free.

The planters get rich. Sugar exports explode.

Kalākaua is a fascinating king. The first reigning monarch in history to travel around the world. He meets Thomas Edison. And in eighteen eighty-six, he lights up his brand new palace, ʻIolani Palace, with electricity. Years before the White House.

He brings back the hula, which the missionaries had pushed underground. They call him the Merrie Monarch.

## Part 4B

But the sugar deal comes with a price.

When it's time to renew it, America wants something more. A harbour on Oʻahu, perfect for a navy. Its Hawaiian name is Puʻuloa. The Americans call it... Pearl Harbor.

The king doesn't want to give it.

So in eighteen eighty-seven, a group of mostly American businessmen and their armed militia force Kalākaua to sign a new constitution. It strips most of his power. It takes the vote away from all Asians and many Native Hawaiians... and gives it to wealthy foreigners who aren't even citizens.

People call it the Bayonet Constitution. Because he signed it at the point of one.

And that same year, the sugar treaty is renewed. With Pearl Harbor.

A spoonful of sugar. Traded for a harbour.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

When Kalākaua dies, his sister becomes queen. Liliʻuokalani.

She wants to restore the power of the throne, and the vote for her people.

In January eighteen ninety-three, the businessmen move against her. American Marines land in Honolulu from the warship Boston.

The queen gives up her throne. Not to the businessmen... but, she writes, to the United States, hoping America will put things right.

Two years later, after a failed uprising, she's arrested and held in a room in her own palace.

There she writes music, as she always has. Years earlier, she had written the most famous Hawaiian song of all, Aloha ʻOe. It's still sung at goodbyes all over the islands.

## Part 5: Territory

The American president, Grover Cleveland, investigates. His report says the overthrow was wrong. He asks for the queen to be restored.

The businessmen refuse. They declare a republic.

And in eighteen ninety-eight, during the war with Spain, America decides it wants Hawaii after all. Congress annexes it.

Thousands of Hawaiians had signed petitions against it.

In eighteen ninety-six, the new government had made English the language of the schools. Hawaiian children were punished for speaking Hawaiian. Within a couple of generations, the language was close to dying.

Meanwhile, a man named James Dole starts growing pineapples. In nineteen twenty-two, he buys almost the entire island of Lānaʻi and turns it into the biggest pineapple plantation in the world.

That's how a fruit from South America became the symbol of Hawaii.

And in Waikīkī, a young Native Hawaiian swimmer named Duke Kahanamoku wins Olympic gold. Then he travels the world showing people how to surf.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

On the morning of December seventh, nineteen forty-one, Japanese planes attack the American fleet at Pearl Harbor.

More than two thousand four hundred Americans are killed.

The battleship Arizona sinks with more than eleven hundred men aboard. It's still there, under the water. Oil still rises from it.

The next day, America enters the Second World War.

## Part 5C

Hawaii is placed under martial law.

More than a third of the people in Hawaii are of Japanese ancestry. On the mainland, Japanese Americans are sent to camps. In Hawaii, there are too many to lock up. Fewer than two thousand are held.

And thousands of young Japanese Americans from Hawaii volunteer to fight for the country that distrusts them.

Their unit, the four hundred forty-second, becomes one of the most decorated in American history.

## Part 6: Fiftieth State

After the war, those veterans come home and go into politics. Jets start bringing tourists. Hotels rise along Waikīkī.

And on August twenty-first, nineteen fifty-nine, Hawaii becomes the fiftieth state.

Two years later, a boy named Barack Obama is born in Honolulu. He'll become the forty-fourth president.

## Part 6B

But something else is happening too. A Hawaiian renaissance.

People start teaching the language again. Hula schools fill up.

And in nineteen seventy-six, a group builds a traditional double-hulled voyaging canoe, the Hōkūleʻa, and sails it from Hawaii to Tahiti. No compass. No instruments. Just stars, waves and birds.

They prove what the experts doubted. The first voyagers really could have done it on purpose.

In nineteen ninety-three, a hundred years after the overthrow, the United States Congress passes a resolution. It formally apologises to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow of their kingdom.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

In August twenty twenty-three, a wildfire, driven by hurricane winds, sweeps through the old royal town of Lahaina on Maui.

More than one hundred people die. Most of the town is lost.

The people of Lahaina are still rebuilding.

## Part 7: Today

Today, the sugar mills are almost all gone. The last one closed in twenty sixteen. The pineapple fields are mostly gone too.

What's left is a place with no ethnic majority. Hawaiian, Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, Korean, Samoan, white... mixed together in one word. Local.

Children can go to school in Hawaiian again.

And on the Big Island, the volcano is still building new land.

## Part 7B

So how does an island kingdom, in the middle of the biggest ocean on Earth, become the fiftieth state?

A spoonful of sugar. Sugar brought the planters. The planters wanted the treaty. The treaty cost Pearl Harbor. And Pearl Harbor brought the war that brought the world to Hawaii.

<short pause> But the story didn't start with sugar. It started with a canoe, a sky full of stars, and people brave enough to sail toward an island nobody had ever seen.

And it still hasn't ended.

<chuckle> Oh, and the Hawaiian pizza? Still Canadian.
