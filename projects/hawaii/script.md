# The Entire History of Hawaii

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,600 spoken words, so roughly 19 to 20 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.
- Hawaiian words: the ʻokina (ʻ) is a glottal stop, a tiny catch in the voice, as in "uh-oh". Gemini usually handles it; if a word comes out wrong, regenerate that part.

## Hook

The pineapple on Hawaiian pizza? <short pause> Invented in Canada. And the pineapple isn't from Hawaii either.

The ukulele? Brought to the islands by Portuguese immigrants, in eighteen seventy-nine.

Surfing? <chuckle> That one really IS Hawaiian. Kings did it.

And in Honolulu, there's a royal palace that had electric lights... years before the White House.

<long pause> Because Hawaii was a kingdom. With a queen. Until eighteen ninety-three, when a group of businessmen, backed by American Marines, took it from her.

<short pause> So how does an island kingdom, in the middle of the biggest ocean on Earth, become the fiftieth state?

<whispers> It starts with a spoonful of sugar.

## Part 1: First Voyagers

Look at a globe. <short pause> Hawaii is the most isolated group of islands on the planet. More than two thousand miles from the nearest continent.

The islands were born from fire. Deep under the Pacific, there's a hot spot, where melted rock pushes up through the sea floor. Over millions of years, it builds a volcano, then another, then another, as the ocean floor slowly slides over it. Like a conveyor belt making islands.

<gasp> It's still doing it. The Big Island is still growing. And under the sea, just off its coast, a brand new volcano is rising. In tens of thousands of years, it will break the surface.

For almost all of their history, no human had ever stood on these islands.

<short pause> Birds got here first. Blown across the ocean, or flying on purpose. Over millions of years, one kind of finch turned into dozens of different birds, with beaks shaped for every job. <chuckle> Some sip nectar. Some crack seeds. Some pry bugs out of bark.

Plants arrive as seeds, on the wind, on the waves, in the feathers of birds. Insects ride along.

<breath> Most of the plants and animals that live here are found nowhere else on Earth.

<long pause> Then, roughly a thousand years ago, double-hulled canoes appear on the horizon.

## Part 1B

The people on board are Polynesians. They've sailed thousands of miles of open ocean, from islands far to the south, without a compass, without a map.

They read the stars as they rise and set. The long ocean swells, and how they bend around islands they can't yet see. The birds that fly out to fish in the morning and home at night. <short pause> Even the colour on the underside of clouds, tinted green by a lagoon below.

It's one of the greatest feats of navigation in human history.

And they don't come once. For generations, canoes sail back and forth between Hawaii and Tahiti. Two and a half thousand miles. Each way.

<breath> They bring everything they need for a new life. Taro, sweet potato, coconut, bananas, sugar cane. Pigs, dogs, chickens.

## Part 1C

And they build a whole civilisation.

Each island is cut into wedge-shaped land divisions, from the mountaintop down to the reef. Every community gets forest, farmland, streams, and sea. <short pause> Everything it needs, top to bottom.

They build stone fishponds along the shore, hundreds of them, where young fish swim in through gates and grow too fat to swim back out. <chuckle> Fish farming. Centuries ago.

Society is ruled by chiefs called aliʻi, and a strict system of sacred laws called kapu. Break a kapu, even by letting your shadow fall on a high chief, and you could be put to death.

<short pause> But once a year, everything changes. For about four months, from late autumn, the god Lono, god of rain and harvest, is honoured in a festival called Makahiki. <chuckle> War is forbidden. People hold games, boxing matches, races and feasts.

Remember Makahiki. It's about to matter.

<short pause> And on the waves, they ride boards. Heʻe nalu. Wave sliding.

The chiefs ride the longest boards of all. Some are more than sixteen feet long, carved from a single tree.

## Part 1D

And they tell stories about the fire under their feet.

<short pause> The volcano goddess Pele, the stories say, travelled from island to island, looking for a home. Every time she dug a fire pit, the sea goddess flooded it. <breath> So she kept moving south and east. Until she reached the Big Island. And there, in the crater of Kīlauea, she stayed.

<gasp> Here's the amazing part. That's the same order the islands were really born in. Oldest in the northwest. Youngest, and still burning, in the southeast.

<short pause> The old stories remembered the geology.

## Part 2: Strangers

In January seventeen seventy-eight, two British ships appear off the island of Kauaʻi. The commander is Captain James Cook.

<chuckle> He names them the Sandwich Islands. After an English earl. The one the sandwich is named after too.

A year later, Cook returns, and arrives at Kealakekua Bay, on the Big Island, during a long festival for the god Lono. He's welcomed with enormous ceremony.

But when his ships come back again, after breaking a mast, the mood has changed. A boat is stolen. Cook tries to take a high chief hostage. A fight breaks out on the beach.

<long pause> In February seventeen seventy-nine, Captain Cook is killed.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The ships bring something far deadlier than cannons.

<breath> Diseases the islanders have never met. Measles. Influenza. Smallpox.

Before Cook arrived, there may have been three hundred thousand people in the islands. Maybe many more.

<long pause> A century later, fewer than forty-five thousand Native Hawaiians are left.

## Part 2C

More ships come. And in seventeen ninety-three, another British captain, George Vancouver, brings a gift for a rising young chief. <short pause> Cattle.

The chief places a kapu on them. Nobody may touch them.

<chuckle> So the cattle are left alone. For years. They multiply. They run wild. Thousands of them, trampling crops across the Big Island.

So in the eighteen-thirties, the king brings in cowboys from Mexico, Spanish-speaking vaqueros, to teach Hawaiians how to ride and rope.

Hawaiians call them paniolo, from the word for Spanish. And Hawaii has cowboys on the range before Texas has even joined the United States.

## Part 2D

The foreigners want something else, too. Sandalwood.

Its sweet-smelling wood sells for a fortune in China. And Hawaii's forests are full of it.

<tsk> So the chiefs buy ships, silk, guns, even a royal yacht, on credit. And they pay in sandalwood. They send thousands of ordinary people up into the mountains, for weeks at a time, to cut it and carry it down on their backs. Farms are left untended. People go hungry.

To measure how much they owe, they dig pits in the ground, the exact size of a ship's hold, and fill them with logs.

<sigh> Within twenty years, the sandalwood is almost all gone. Some of those pits are still there, empty, in the hills.

## Part 3: Kingdom

That young chief who got the cattle? <short pause> His name is Kamehameha.

He's a warrior and a strategist. He buys Western guns and ships. He hires Western sailors as advisers.

Island by island, he wins.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

In seventeen ninety-five, his army lands on Oʻahu. The defenders are driven up the Nuʻuanu Valley, to the edge of the cliffs at the top.

<long pause> Hundreds of warriors are forced over the edge.

## Part 3C

By eighteen ten, Kamehameha the Great rules all of the islands. The last one, Kauaʻi, joins without a battle.

One kingdom. The Kingdom of Hawaiʻi.

<short pause> And Kamehameha makes one of his most famous laws after an embarrassing moment. Years earlier, chasing some fishermen, his foot got stuck in the rocks, and one of them hit him over the head with a paddle.

<chuckle> Instead of punishing the man, Kamehameha makes a law. The Law of the Splintered Paddle. Ordinary people, the old and the young, must be able to sleep safely by the side of the road.

It's still in Hawaii's state constitution today.

## Part 3D

The kingdom is recognised by Britain, by France, by the United States. It signs treaties. It has embassies. It has a flag... <short pause> which still has a British Union Jack in the corner, even today.

After Kamehameha dies, change comes fast. In eighteen nineteen, his powerful widow Kaʻahumanu and his son sit down and eat together. Men and women, at the same meal. Under the old kapu laws, that was forbidden.

<gasp> The old system falls. Almost overnight.

And the very next year, American missionaries arrive.

They create a written Hawaiian alphabet. Just five vowels and eight consonants. Within a few decades, Hawaii has one of the highest literacy rates in the world.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

In eighteen twenty-four, the young king, Kamehameha the Second, sails to London with his queen to meet King George the Fourth.

<breath> Before they can meet him, both of them catch measles.

<long pause> They die in London, thousands of miles from home. A British warship carries their bodies back to Hawaii.

## Part 3F

In eighteen forty-three, a British naval captain sails into Honolulu and simply takes over the kingdom. <tsk> On his own.

For five months, the British flag flies over Hawaii. Then a British admiral arrives, says this was never authorised, and hands the kingdom back.

At the ceremony, the king, Kamehameha the Third, speaks words that become Hawaii's motto. Roughly: the life of the land is preserved in righteousness.

<short pause> Remember that. Because fifty years later, it won't be a British captain. And nobody will hand it back.

## Part 4: Sugar

Then the whalers come. Hundreds of ships a year stop at Lahaina and Honolulu. Sailors. Merchants. And businessmen who notice that the soil and the sun here are perfect for one crop.

<long pause> Sugar.

In eighteen forty-eight, a land reform called the Great Māhele lets land be bought and sold for the first time. <sigh> Many ordinary Hawaiians don't understand the new paperwork, or can't afford it. Foreigners buy huge plantations.

But sugar needs two things. Workers, and customers.

## Part 4B

The workers come from across the world. Chinese. Then Japanese. Portuguese from Madeira and the Azores, who bring a tiny four-stringed guitar. <chuckle> The islanders say the players' fingers jump around like fleas. Ukulele. Jumping flea.

Later Koreans and Filipinos. Puerto Ricans.

On the plantations, they work in the hot fields under strict bosses, for very little money. Each group lives in its own camp. But they share food, words and jokes. And they invent a new way of talking, so they can understand each other. Pidgin.

That's why Hawaii today is a mix like nowhere else.

## Part 4C

The customers are in America. But America taxes foreign sugar.

So in eighteen seventy-five, King Kalākaua signs a deal with the United States. Hawaiian sugar can enter America tax-free.

<gasp> The planters get rich. Sugar exports explode.

And Kalākaua is a fascinating king. In eighteen eighty-one, he becomes the first reigning monarch in history to travel all the way around the world. He meets the Pope, the Emperor of Japan, Queen Victoria.

He meets Thomas Edison too. And soon after, he lights up his brand new palace, ʻIolani Palace, with electricity. <short pause> Years before the White House. It even has telephones.

He brings back the hula, which the missionaries had pushed underground. <chuckle> They call him the Merrie Monarch.

<short pause> A king with electric lights, a telephone, and a dance revival. Hawaii looks like a modern kingdom.

<breath> But the sugar planters are getting richer and more powerful every year. And they want something the king won't give.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In these same years, a disease called leprosy spreads in the islands.

<breath> The kingdom sends people who catch it to a remote peninsula on Molokaʻi, called Kalaupapa, cut off by sea cliffs. Families are torn apart. Many never see home again.

A Belgian priest, Father Damien, goes there to care for them. He catches the disease himself, and dies there.

## Part 4E

But the sugar deal comes with a price.

When it's time to renew it, America wants something more. A harbour on Oʻahu, perfect for a navy. <short pause> Its Hawaiian name is Puʻuloa. The Americans call it... Pearl Harbor.

The king doesn't want to give it.

So in eighteen eighty-seven, a group of mostly American businessmen and their armed militia force Kalākaua to sign a new constitution. It strips most of his power. It takes the vote away from all Asians and many Native Hawaiians... <tsk> and gives it to wealthy foreigners who aren't even citizens.

People call it the Bayonet Constitution. Because he signed it under the threat of one.

<long pause> And that same year, the sugar treaty is renewed. With Pearl Harbor.

A spoonful of sugar. Traded for a harbour.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

When Kalākaua dies, his sister becomes queen. Liliʻuokalani.

She's a musician, a writer, and fiercely determined. She wants to restore the power of the throne, and the vote for her people.

<breath> In January eighteen ninety-three, the businessmen move against her. American Marines land in Honolulu from the warship Boston.

To avoid bloodshed, the queen gives up her throne. Not to the businessmen... but, she writes, to the United States, hoping America will put things right.

<long pause> Two years later, after a failed uprising by her supporters, she's arrested and held in a room in her own palace. For months.

There she sews a quilt. And she writes music, as she always has. Years earlier, she had written the most famous Hawaiian song of all, Aloha ʻOe. <short pause> It's still sung at goodbyes all over the islands.

## Part 4G (quiet style)

The queen's niece, the young Princess Kaʻiulani, is the heir to the throne. She's at school in England when the kingdom is taken.

<breath> At seventeen, she sails to America. She goes to Washington, meets President Cleveland, and pleads for her people.

<short pause> Americans who expected a "savage princess" meet a poised, brilliant young woman. Newspapers adore her.

It isn't enough. She goes home to the islands. <long pause> In eighteen ninety-nine, she dies at just twenty-three.

## Part 5: Territory

The American president, Grover Cleveland, investigates. His report says the overthrow was wrong. He asks for the queen to be restored.

<tsk> The businessmen refuse. They declare a republic.

Native Hawaiians fight back with paper. In eighteen ninety-seven, more than twenty thousand of them sign petitions against joining the United States. That's more than half of all Native Hawaiians alive.

<long pause> It doesn't matter. In eighteen ninety-eight, during the war with Spain, America decides it wants Hawaii's harbour after all. Congress annexes it.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In eighteen ninety-six, the new government had made English the language of the schools.

<breath> Hawaiian children are punished for speaking Hawaiian. Within a couple of generations, the language is close to dying.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In December eighteen ninety-nine, plague appears in Honolulu's crowded Chinatown.

<breath> Health officials decide to burn infected buildings. In January nineteen hundred, one of the fires blows out of control.

<long pause> Most of Chinatown burns to the ground. Thousands of people, mostly Chinese and Japanese families, lose everything they own.

## Part 5D

Meanwhile, a man named James Dole starts growing pineapples. In nineteen twenty-two, he buys almost the entire island of Lānaʻi and turns it into the biggest pineapple plantation in the world.

<chuckle> That's how a fruit from South America became the symbol of Hawaii.

And on the beach at Waikīkī, a young Native Hawaiian swimmer named Duke Kahanamoku is breaking records. In nineteen twelve, he wins Olympic gold.

Then he travels the world. Australia. California. Everywhere he goes, he picks up a big wooden board and paddles out.

<short pause> The world had almost forgotten surfing. Duke gave it back.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

On the morning of December seventh, nineteen forty-one, Japanese planes attack the American fleet at Pearl Harbor.

<long pause> More than two thousand four hundred Americans are killed.

The battleship Arizona sinks with more than eleven hundred men aboard. It's still there, under the water. <short pause> Oil still rises from it. A few drops a day.

The next day, America enters the Second World War.

## Part 5F

Hawaii is placed under martial law.

More than a third of the people in Hawaii are of Japanese ancestry. On the mainland, Japanese Americans are sent to camps. In Hawaii, there are too many to lock up. About two thousand are held.

<short pause> And thousands of young Japanese Americans from Hawaii volunteer to fight for the country that distrusts them.

Their units, the one hundredth Battalion and the four hundred forty-second, become some of the most decorated in American history.

One of them, Daniel Inouye, loses his right arm in battle in Italy. He comes home and becomes one of Hawaii's first congressmen, and later a senator for almost fifty years.

## Part 5G

Back home, on the plantations, the workers are getting organised.

At first, the planters keep them divided. Japanese in one camp. Filipinos in another. Different pay for different groups. <tsk> If one group strikes, another is brought in to replace them.

<short pause> In nineteen forty-six, that finally fails. Workers of every background walk out together. Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, Hawaiian. Twenty-one thousand of them. Every sugar plantation stops.

<gasp> They win.

For the first time, the families who cut the cane have real power. Within a few years, their sons and daughters are winning elections, and the old planter government is voted out.

## Part 6: Fiftieth State

After the war, those veterans come home and go into politics. Jets start bringing tourists. Hotels rise along Waikīkī.

<chuckle> Visitors buy brightly patterned shirts, made in Honolulu. Aloha shirts. Today, millions of people come every year. More visitors, many years, than people who live in the islands.

In nineteen fifty-nine, Hawaii's voters are asked if they want statehood. <short pause> More than ninety percent say yes.

And on August twenty-first, nineteen fifty-nine, Hawaii becomes the fiftieth state.

Two years later, a boy named Barack Obama is born in Honolulu. He'll become the forty-fourth president.

## Part 6B

But something else is happening too. A Hawaiian renaissance.

People start teaching the language again. Hula schools fill up. Musicians sing in Hawaiian.

<short pause> And some experts are still saying something insulting: that the first Polynesians probably found Hawaii by accident. Drifting. Lost.

So in nineteen seventy-six, a group builds a traditional double-hulled voyaging canoe. They name it Hōkūleʻa. Star of Gladness.

But no Hawaiian alive still knows the old way to navigate. So they find a master navigator from a tiny island in Micronesia, Mau Piailug, one of the last people on Earth who still knows how.

<breath> No compass. No instruments. Just stars, waves and birds.

After thirty-four days at sea... <gasp> they reach Tahiti. Thousands of people are waiting on the beach.

They prove what the experts doubted. The first voyagers really could have done it. On purpose.

## Part 6C

In nineteen seventy-eight, Hawaiian becomes an official language of the state. Children can go to school in Hawaiian again. Today, some families speak it at home for the first time in generations.

<short pause> And Hawaiian music finds the whole world. In the nineteen-nineties, a big-voiced singer from Niʻihau and Oʻahu, Israel Kamakawiwoʻole, sits down with a ukulele and records a gentle medley in one late-night take. <chuckle> It becomes one of the most played Hawaiian recordings of all time.

In nineteen ninety-three, a hundred years after the overthrow, the United States Congress passes a resolution. It formally apologises to Native Hawaiians for the overthrow of their kingdom.

<short pause> And the next year, the Navy hands back an island it had used for bombing practice for fifty years. Kahoʻolawe.

## Part 6D (quiet style)

In August twenty twenty-three, a wildfire, driven by hurricane winds, sweeps through the old royal town of Lahaina on Maui.

<long pause> More than one hundred people die. Most of the town is lost.

The people of Lahaina are still rebuilding.

## Part 7: Today

Today, the sugar mills are almost all gone. The last one closed in twenty sixteen. The pineapple fields are mostly gone too.

What's left is a place with no ethnic majority. Hawaiian, Japanese, Filipino, Chinese, Portuguese, Korean, Samoan, white... mixed together in one word. <short pause> Local.

And on the Big Island, the volcano is still building new land.

<short pause> On top of Mauna Kea, the tallest mountain on Earth when you measure from the sea floor, astronomers have built giant telescopes, in some of the clearest air on the planet. <breath> To many Native Hawaiians, the summit is sacred, and they've protested new telescopes there. It's still being argued about today.

<short pause> The same questions keep coming back. Whose land is it? Who decides?

## Part 7B

So how does an island kingdom, in the middle of the biggest ocean on Earth, become the fiftieth state?

<short pause> A spoonful of sugar.

Sugar brought the planters. The planters wanted the treaty. The treaty cost Pearl Harbor. The planters took the kingdom. And Pearl Harbor brought the war that brought the world to Hawaii.

<long pause> But the story didn't start with sugar. It started with a canoe, a sky full of stars, and people brave enough to sail toward an island nobody had ever seen.

And it still hasn't ended.

<chuckle> Oh, and the Hawaiian pizza? <short pause> Still Canadian.
