## Hook

North Dakota makes more honey than any other state.

In nineteen thirty-six, North Dakota hit sixty below zero in February... and a hundred and twenty-one degrees in July. The same year.

It has the only state-owned bank in America.

And it's often called the least-visited state in the country.

<long pause> In nineteen ninety-seven, the Red River flooded the city of Grand Forks. And while its downtown was underwater... it caught fire.

And yet, this quiet, frozen state became one of the biggest oil producers in America.

<short pause> So how does the least-visited state end up pumping more oil than almost anywhere in the country?

<whispers> It starts with a farmer named Henry Bakken.

## Part 1: Earth Lodges

North Dakota is the top of the Great Plains. Flat farmland in the east. Rolling hills, the Missouri River, and the colourful Badlands in the west.

For hundreds of years, along the Missouri, the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara live in towns of big, round earth lodges. They grow corn, beans, squash and sunflowers. Their villages are great trading centres for the whole northern plains.

The Lakota, the Dakota, the Ojibwe and others live on the prairies around them.

## Part 2: Fort Mandan

In the winter of eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark build a fort next to the Mandan villages, and stay for five freezing months. The Mandan feed them and help them survive.

There, they hire a French-Canadian trader, and his young Shoshone wife. Her name is Sacagawea. She will help guide them to the Pacific.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-seven, a steamboat brings smallpox up the Missouri River.

It sweeps through the Mandan villages. Within months, most of the Mandan people are dead. Perhaps nine out of ten.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-three, after the Dakota War in Minnesota, the army pursues Dakota people into North Dakota. At Whitestone Hill, soldiers attack a large camp. Many are killed, including women and children.

## Part 3: Railroads

In the eighteen-seventies, the Northern Pacific Railroad pushes west across the plains. And it needs money from Europe.

So when the railroad founds a town on the Missouri River, it names it after the German leader, Otto von Bismarck. Hoping German investors will be flattered.

<chuckle> Bismarck becomes the capital. There's no record Otto von Bismarck ever cared.

## Part 3B

The railroads sell land cheap. Settlers pour in. Norwegians. Germans from Russia. Swedes. Many of them farming wheat on giant "bonanza farms" in the Red River Valley.

## Part 3C

And in eighteen eighty-three, a young New Yorker comes to the Badlands to hunt buffalo. His name is Theodore Roosevelt.

A few months later, his wife and his mother die on the same day.

Heartbroken, he returns to North Dakota. He buys a ranch. He rides, ropes cattle, and chases down boat thieves.

Years later, he says, "I never would have been president if it had not been for my experiences in North Dakota."

## Part 3D

In November eighteen eighty-nine, North Dakota and South Dakota become states on the same day. President Harrison shuffles the papers so nobody knows which he signed first.

<chuckle> North Dakota is listed as thirty-ninth. Mostly because N comes before S in the alphabet.

## Part 4: Prairie Rebels

North Dakota's farmers grow lots of wheat. But they feel cheated. Railroads, grain companies and banks in Minneapolis and Chicago set the prices. And the farmers always seem to lose.

In nineteen fifteen, angry farmers start a political movement. The Nonpartisan League. It takes over the state government.

And in nineteen nineteen, North Dakota creates its own bank, owned by the state. The Bank of North Dakota. And a state-owned flour mill.

<chuckle> More than a hundred years later, it's still the only state-owned bank in America.

## Part 4B

And North Dakota weather is... extreme.

In February nineteen thirty-six, the town of Parshall drops to sixty degrees below zero. In July, the town of Steele hits a hundred and twenty-one degrees.

Both state records. Same year.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In the nineteen-fifties, the government builds the giant Garrison Dam on the Missouri. Its lake floods the best land of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara. Towns, farms and burial grounds, under water.

A photo shows the tribal chairman, George Gillette, weeping as the land is signed away.

## Part 5: Missiles

During the Cold War, the Air Force buries hundreds of nuclear missiles in the North Dakota prairie, pointed at the Soviet Union.

<chuckle> People joked that if North Dakota were a country, it would have been one of the biggest nuclear powers on Earth.

Near the town of Nekoma, the army builds a giant concrete pyramid to guard the missiles. It costs billions. It operates for only a few months before it's shut down.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In April nineteen ninety-seven, after a winter of record snow, the Red River rises higher than anyone expected. It breaks through the dikes at Grand Forks. Almost the whole city has to evacuate.

Then, with the streets under water, a fire breaks out downtown. Fire trucks can't reach it. Buildings burn above the flood.

## Part 6: Oil Boom

Now, the farmer.

In nineteen fifty-one, oil is discovered in western North Dakota. A little while later, a well on a farm owned by a man named Henry Bakken drills into a thick layer of rock, deep underground.

The layer is named after him. The Bakken.

Geologists know it's full of oil. But the rock is tight. Like stone. The oil can't flow out. For fifty years, there's no good way to get it.

## Part 6B

Then, in the two thousands, drillers combine two techniques. They drill down two miles, then turn and drill sideways through the rock layer. And they pump water, sand and chemicals in at high pressure to crack the rock open. Fracking.

The oil flows.

North Dakota explodes. Workers pour in from across America. Small towns like Williston double in size. People sleep in their cars, in trailers, in camps. Wages soar.

The gas flares burning off the wells are so bright they can be seen from space.

Within a few years, North Dakota is pumping over a million barrels of oil a day. For a while, second only to Texas.

## Part 6C

So how does the least-visited state end up pumping more oil than almost anywhere in the country?

It starts with a farmer named Henry Bakken.

The oil was there all along, two miles under his farm and thousands of others, locked inside rock. It took fifty years for anyone to figure out how to get it out. And when they did, the quiet prairie became one of the busiest oil fields on Earth.

<short pause> North Dakota was never empty. It was just waiting.

<chuckle> Hardly anyone visits. But a lot of people come to work.
