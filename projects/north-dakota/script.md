# The Entire History of North Dakota

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,100 spoken words, so roughly 11 to 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In nineteen ninety-seven"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, wide prairie storyteller, with wonder. |
| Part 2 | Frozen-winter frontier voice, then grave. |
| Part 3 | Brisk railroad-boom energy, warm with Roosevelt. |
| Part 4 | Feisty prairie populist, then weather-beaten and wry. |
| Part 5 | Cold War hush, then grave. |
| Part 6 | Gritty, booming oil-field energy; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): Earth Lodges 10,000 BC to 1804 · Fort Mandan 1804 to 1863 · Railroads 1870 to 1889 · Statehood 1889 to 1919 · Prairie Rebels 1915 to 1950 · Missiles 1950 to 1990 · Oil Boom 2006 to now.

## Hook

North Dakota makes more honey than any other state.

<chuckle> In nineteen thirty-six, North Dakota hit sixty below zero in February... and a hundred and twenty-one degrees in July. The same year.

It has the only state-owned bank in America. And it's often called the least-visited state in the country.

<long pause> In nineteen ninety-seven, the Red River flooded the city of Grand Forks. And while its downtown was underwater... it caught fire.

And yet, this quiet, frozen state became one of the biggest oil producers in America.

<short pause> So how does the least-visited state end up pumping more oil than almost anywhere in the country?

<whispers> It starts with a farmer named Henry Bakken.

## Part 1: Earth Lodges

North Dakota is the top of the Great Plains. Flat farmland in the east. Rolling hills, the Missouri River, and the colourful Badlands in the west.

<gasp> At the end of the Ice Age, the east was the bottom of a giant lake, Lake Agassiz. When it drained, it left behind one of the flattest places on Earth. The Red River Valley. <short pause> And the Red River does something unusual. It flows north, into Canada. <sigh> In spring, the southern part thaws first, while the north is still frozen. The water has nowhere to go. Remember that.

<short pause> In the west, the Badlands are wild and broken, home to bison, prairie dogs and wild horses. Today, they're Theodore Roosevelt National Park. <chuckle> The only national park named after a single person.

## Part 1B

Sixty-six million years ago, this was swampy coastland, full of dinosaurs. <chuckle> In nineteen ninety-nine, a teenager hunting fossils on his uncle's land in North Dakota finds a duck-billed dinosaur that's been mummified. Its skin, its muscles, even the shape of its scales... turned to stone. <short pause> Scientists call it Dakota.

## Part 1C

For hundreds of years, along the Missouri, the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara live in towns of big, round earth lodges. They grow corn, beans, squash and sunflowers. They cross the river in round boats made of buffalo hide stretched over willow frames. Bull boats.

<gasp> Their villages are great trading centres for the whole northern plains. Guns and metal pots from Canada. Horses from the south. Shells from the Pacific.

<short pause> For eleven thousand years, people have dug a glassy brown stone from quarries near the Knife River, to make knives and spear points. Knife River flint has been found as far away as the Atlantic coast.

<short pause> In eighteen thirty-two, the painter George Catlin visits the Mandan, and paints their leaders, their villages and their ceremonies. Some of the only pictures we have of them.

The Lakota, the Dakota, the Ojibwe and others live on the prairies around them.

## Part 2: Fort Mandan

In the winter of eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark build a fort next to the Mandan villages, and stay for five freezing months. <groan> It gets to forty-five degrees below zero. The Mandan feed them and help them survive.

There, they hire a French-Canadian trader, and his young Shoshone wife. Her name is Sacagawea. <short pause> In February, at the fort, she gives birth to a baby boy. Two months later, she carries him with her, all the way to the Pacific.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

In eighteen thirty-seven, a steamboat brings smallpox up the Missouri River.

<breath> It sweeps through the Mandan villages.

<long pause> Within months, most of the Mandan people are dead. Perhaps nine out of ten. The survivors join with the Hidatsa and the Arikara.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-three, after the Dakota War in Minnesota, the army pursues Dakota people into North Dakota.

<breath> At Whitestone Hill, soldiers attack a large camp. Many are killed, including women and children.

## Part 2D

In May eighteen seventy-six, Lieutenant Colonel George Custer rides out of Fort Abraham Lincoln, near today's Bismarck, with the Seventh Cavalry. <short pause> The band plays. Wives wave goodbye. <sigh> Six weeks later, at the Little Bighorn, most of the men who rode out with him are dead.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

In July eighteen eighty-one, the great Lakota leader Sitting Bull, starving in Canada with his last followers, rides into Fort Buford, in North Dakota, to surrender.

<breath> He hands his rifle to his young son, and has the boy give it to the commanding officer.

<long pause> He says he wants it remembered that he was the last man of his tribe to surrender his rifle.

## Part 3: Railroads

In the eighteen-seventies, the Northern Pacific Railroad pushes west across the plains. And it needs money from Europe.

<chuckle> So when the railroad founds a town on the Missouri River, it names it after the German leader, Otto von Bismarck. Hoping German investors will be flattered.

<laugh> Bismarck becomes the capital. There's no record Otto von Bismarck ever cared.

## Part 3B

The railroads sell land cheap. Settlers pour in. Norwegians. Germans from Russia. Swedes. Icelanders.

<chuckle> The Germans from Russia bring their own recipes. Kuchen, a sweet custard pie, becomes North Dakota's official state dessert.

<gasp> In the Red River Valley, giant "bonanza farms" grow wheat on thousands of acres at once. Some of them cover more land than a city. Teams of horses pull ploughs in long lines across the flat land. <short pause> Some fields are so big, it's said, that a ploughman goes down one furrow in the morning, and comes back up the next in the afternoon.

## Part 3C

And in eighteen eighty-three, a young New Yorker comes to the Badlands to hunt buffalo. His name is Theodore Roosevelt. <chuckle> The cowboys laugh at his glasses and his fancy way of talking. They call him "Four Eyes".

<breath> A few months later, back in New York, his wife and his mother die on the same day, in the same house.

Heartbroken, he returns to North Dakota. He buys a ranch. He rides, ropes cattle, and once chases boat thieves downriver for days, through the snow, to bring them to justice.

<short pause> Years later, he says, "I never would have been president if it had not been for my experiences in North Dakota."

## Part 3D

In November eighteen eighty-nine, North Dakota and South Dakota become states on the same day. <laugh> President Harrison shuffles the papers so nobody knows which he signed first.

<chuckle> North Dakota is listed as thirty-ninth. Mostly because N comes before S in the alphabet.

## Part 4: Prairie Rebels

North Dakota's farmers grow lots of wheat. But they feel cheated. <tsk> Railroads, grain companies and banks in Minneapolis and Chicago set the prices. And the farmers always seem to lose.

In nineteen fifteen, angry farmers start a political movement. The Nonpartisan League. <gasp> Within a few years, it takes over the state government.

And in nineteen nineteen, North Dakota creates its own bank, owned by the state. The Bank of North Dakota. And a state-owned flour mill, so farmers can grind their own wheat.

<chuckle> More than a hundred years later, it's still the only state-owned bank in America. And the mill is still grinding.

<chuckle> And the honey? Every summer, beekeepers from all over America truck their hives to North Dakota, where the prairie blooms with clover, canola and sunflowers. <short pause> That's how a frozen state ends up making more honey than any other.

## Part 4B

And North Dakota weather is... extreme.

<groan> In February nineteen thirty-six, the town of Parshall drops to sixty degrees below zero. In July, the town of Steele hits a hundred and twenty-one degrees.

Both state records. Same year.

<sigh> In the nineteen-thirties, drought, dust storms and the Great Depression crush North Dakota's farmers. <gasp> In nineteen thirty-three, the governor, William Langer, tries something wild. He bans any wheat from being shipped out of the state, to force prices up. <short pause> The ban doesn't last. But tens of thousands of people give up and leave.

<chuckle> After the old Capitol burns down in nineteen thirty, North Dakota builds a new one. Not a dome. A nineteen-storey office tower, rising straight out of the prairie. People call it the Skyscraper on the Prairie.

## Part 4C

<chuckle> North Dakota also sends out some surprising talent. Lawrence Welk, the bandleader, grows up on a farm near Strasburg, speaking German. <short pause> The singer Peggy Lee grows up in Jamestown. The western writer Louis L'Amour, in Jamestown too. <gasp> And Roger Maris, raised in Fargo, hits sixty-one home runs in nineteen sixty-one, breaking Babe Ruth's record.

<laugh> The little town of Rugby puts up a stone monument claiming to be the geographic centre of North America. Another North Dakota town later argues that it's really them.

## Part 4D (quiet style)

In the nineteen-fifties, the government builds the giant Garrison Dam on the Missouri.

<breath> Its lake floods the best land of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara. Towns, farms and burial grounds, under water.

<long pause> A photo shows the tribal chairman, George Gillette, weeping as the land is signed away.

## Part 5: Missiles

During the Cold War, the Air Force buries hundreds of nuclear missiles in the North Dakota prairie, pointed at the Soviet Union.

<chuckle> People joked that if North Dakota were a country, it would have been one of the biggest nuclear powers on Earth.

<whispers> Near the town of Nekoma, the army builds a giant concrete pyramid to guard the missiles. It costs billions. <tsk> It operates for only a few months before it's shut down. It's still standing there, in the middle of the fields.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

In April nineteen ninety-seven, after a winter of record snow, the Red River rises higher than anyone expected. Remember, it flows north, into ice.

<breath> It breaks through the dikes at Grand Forks. Almost the whole city has to evacuate.

<long pause> Then, with the streets under water, a fire breaks out downtown. Fire trucks can't reach it. Buildings burn above the flood.

## Part 5C

<short pause> Weeks later, a mysterious donor gives fifteen million dollars to help the people of Grand Forks. Two thousand dollars to every family that lost its home. <gasp> She's later revealed to be Joan Kroc, the widow of the man who built McDonald's.

<chuckle> The year before, a dark comedy film called "Fargo" made North Dakota famous for its accents. <laugh> Most of it was actually set in Minnesota.

<laugh> In Fargo today, the North Dakota State University football team wins national championship after championship, at their level. More than almost any college team in America.

## Part 6: Oil Boom

Now, the farmer.

In nineteen fifty-one, oil is discovered in western North Dakota. A little while later, a well on a farm owned by a man named Henry Bakken drills into a thick layer of rock, deep underground.

<short pause> The layer is named after him. The Bakken.

Geologists know it's full of oil. But the rock is tight. Like stone. The oil can't flow out. <sigh> For fifty years, there's no good way to get it.

## Part 6B

Then, in the two thousands, drillers combine two techniques. They drill down two miles, then turn and drill sideways through the rock layer. And they pump water, sand and chemicals in at high pressure to crack the rock open. Fracking.

<gasp> The oil flows.

North Dakota explodes. Workers pour in from across America. Small towns like Williston double in size. <groan> People sleep in their cars, in trailers, in camps. Rents soar higher than in big cities. Wages soar.

<whispers> The gas flares burning off the wells are so bright they can be seen from space.

Within a few years, North Dakota is pumping over a million barrels of oil a day. For a while, second only to Texas.

<groan> Then, in twenty fifteen, oil prices crash. Rigs shut down. Workers leave as fast as they came. <short pause> The boom slows. But the wells keep pumping.

## Part 6C

<sigh> In twenty sixteen, a new pipeline to carry Bakken oil is routed under the Missouri River, just north of the Standing Rock Sioux Reservation. The tribe says it threatens their water and sacred sites. <short pause> Thousands of protesters camp out for months, in the cold. The company says the pipeline is safe. <tsk> The pipeline is finished in twenty seventeen. The court fights over it go on for years.

## Part 6D

So how does the least-visited state end up pumping more oil than almost anywhere in the country?

<whispers> It starts with a farmer named Henry Bakken.

The oil was there all along, two miles under his farm and thousands of others, locked inside rock. <short pause> It took fifty years for anyone to figure out how to get it out. And when they did, the quiet prairie became one of the busiest oil fields on Earth.

<breath> North Dakota was never empty. It was just waiting.

<chuckle> Hardly anyone visits. But a lot of people come to work.
