# The Entire History of Wyoming

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,200 spoken words, so roughly 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen eighty-five"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Windswept, ancient, awed storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Tall-tale mountain man energy, then serious. |
| Part 3 | Brisk, wry, then proud and defiant. |
| Part 4 | Dusty cowboy drawl, rough-and-tumble. |
| Part 5 | Plain-spoken and steady. |
| Part 6 | Wide open and calm; proud at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 11,000 BC to 1807 · Mountain Men 1807 to 1840 · Trails 1840 to 1867 · Equality 1867 to 1890 · Cowboys 1890 to 1920 · Oil and Coal 1920 to 1990 · Today 1990 to now.

## Hook

Yellowstone? <short pause> The first national park in the world. Mostly in Wyoming. Devils Tower? America's first national monument. Also Wyoming.

<chuckle> Wyoming has fewer people than any other state. Fewer than Washington, D.C.

<long pause> In eighteen eighty-five, a mob of miners in Rock Springs, Wyoming, killed at least twenty-eight Chinese workers.

And yet, this rough, wild territory of cowboys and miners was the first place in America to give women the vote for good. Fifty years before the rest of the country.

<short pause> So why would the Wild West be the first to let women vote?

<whispers> It starts with a territory that had six men for every woman.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Wyoming is high country. Mountains, sagebrush plains, and wind. <groan> Lots of wind. Wyoming has some of the windiest towns in America. Snow fences line the highways, and trucks get blown over.

Long ago, it was very different. <gasp> Fifty million years ago, a warm lake covered southwest Wyoming, full of fish, turtles, palm trees and crocodiles. Their fossils are so perfect that you can count the bones in a fish's fin.

And at a long ridge called Como Bluff, railroad workers in the eighteen-seventies find giant dinosaur bones lying everywhere. <chuckle> So many that a local man later builds a whole cabin out of them. The Fossil Cabin.

## Part 1B

For thousands of years, hunters follow buffalo and pronghorn here.

<short pause> At a place called the Colby site, about thirteen thousand years ago, Clovis hunters kill mammoths, and pile up the meat under stacks of bones, like a freezer, to come back for in the winter. <chuckle> One pile was never collected. Archaeologists found it, still neatly stacked. Pronghorn are the fastest land animals in North America. <short pause> They can outrun almost anything except a cheetah, and there are no cheetahs here any more.

High in the Bighorn Mountains, someone lays out a giant circle of stones with twenty-eight spokes. The Medicine Wheel. Some of its stones line up with the sunrise on the longest day of the year.

Later, the land is home to the Shoshone, the Crow, the Arapaho, the Cheyenne and the Lakota.

<whispers> The Lakota have a sacred place, a giant tower of stone rising out of the plains, grooved like claw marks. In one story, a giant bear clawed at the rock, trying to reach girls who had climbed up to escape it. They call it Bear Lodge. <short pause> Settlers later call it Devils Tower.

## Part 2: Mountain Men

In eighteen-oh-seven, a fur trapper named John Colter wanders alone through the mountains of northwest Wyoming, in the middle of winter. When he comes back, he talks about boiling mud, steaming ground, rivers of hot water, and fountains shooting into the sky.

<chuckle> Nobody believes him. They call it "Colter's Hell".

He's describing Yellowstone.

## Part 2B

For the next twenty years, mountain men trap beavers all over Wyoming. <laugh> Every summer, they gather with Native traders for a giant party and market called the Rendezvous. Trading furs, gambling, racing horses, wrestling, and telling tall tales.

The best tall-tale teller is Jim Bridger. <chuckle> He swears he once saw a petrified forest, where petrified birds sat in petrified trees, singing petrified songs.

He also really did see Yellowstone, and the Great Salt Lake. And in eighteen forty-three, he builds a trading post for pioneers in southwest Wyoming. Fort Bridger.

## Part 2C

Then come the trails. The Oregon Trail, the California Trail and the Mormon Trail all cross Wyoming, through South Pass.

<short pause> Pioneers try to reach a giant granite rock by the Fourth of July. If they're there by then, they're on schedule to beat the snow. Thousands carve their names into it. Independence Rock.

At a place called Register Cliff, you can still read names scratched into the sandstone in the eighteen-forties and fifties. <whispers> Some of those people never made it to the end of the trail.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

In eighteen fifty-one, near Fort Laramie, the government signs a treaty with the plains nations. It recognises their huge homelands, and promises peace.

<breath> Then gold is found in Montana, and a new trail cuts straight through the Lakota's best hunting grounds. The army builds forts along it.

The Lakota leader Red Cloud fights back. In December eighteen sixty-six, warriors lure a troop of soldiers out of their fort into an ambush. <long pause> All eighty-one soldiers are killed.

In eighteen sixty-eight, the government gives up the forts, and the Lakota burn them. <short pause> It's one of the only wars the United States ever lost to a Native nation.

## Part 3: Equality

In eighteen sixty-seven, the transcontinental railroad arrives. <gasp> Tent towns pop up along the tracks overnight, full of saloons, gamblers and gunfights. People call them "Hell on Wheels". When the tracks move on, so do the towns.

One of them, Cheyenne, grows so fast it's called "the Magic City of the Plains".

In eighteen sixty-eight, Wyoming becomes a territory.

<sigh> And here's the problem. It's full of men. Railroad workers. Soldiers. Miners. Cowboys. In the first census, there are about six adult men for every adult woman.

Wyoming needs families. It needs women.

## Part 3B

So, in eighteen sixty-nine, Wyoming's first legislature passes a law.

<short pause> Women in Wyoming may vote. And hold office.

Why? There are a few reasons. Some lawmakers truly believe it's right. Some hope the news will bring women and families to Wyoming. <chuckle> And some, people said, just want to embarrass the governor, who they thought would veto it.

<laugh> He signs it.

Wyoming becomes the first place in America where women have a lasting right to vote. And among the very first in the world.

## Part 3C

The next year, in Laramie, a sixty-nine-year-old grandmother named Louisa Swain gets up early, puts on her apron, walks into town to buy yeast, and drops off her ballot on the way. <short pause> She's the first woman to vote in a general election in America under a law like this.

Esther Morris becomes a justice of the peace. One of the first women judges in America.

<chuckle> And in Laramie, women serve on juries, among the first in the world. Newspapers across America laugh. Cartoonists draw jurors knitting and rocking babies.

The juries do just fine.

## Part 3D

In eighteen seventy-two, Congress makes Yellowstone the world's first national park. <short pause> Colter's Hell is real after all.

Old Faithful, a geyser that shoots hot water a hundred feet into the air, more than a dozen times a day. Pools in every colour of the rainbow. Wolves, bears, buffalo.

<whispers> And underneath it all, a supervolcano. Its last giant eruption, more than six hundred thousand years ago, covered much of North America in ash.

## Part 3E

In eighteen ninety, Wyoming asks to become a state. Some in Congress say: fine, but only if you drop women's voting.

<tsk> Wyoming's lawmakers send a telegram back.

"We will remain out of the Union a hundred years, rather than come in without the women."

<short pause> Congress gives in. On July tenth, eighteen ninety, Wyoming becomes the forty-fourth state. And it picks a nickname. The Equality State.

<chuckle> And Wyoming's women keep going. In nineteen-twenty, the little town of Jackson elects a town council made up entirely of women. The newspapers call it the "petticoat government". <short pause> They fix the roads, build a cemetery, and collect the town's unpaid taxes.

<short pause> And Wyoming people keep a fierce independent streak. There's no state income tax.

## Part 4: Cowboys

Wyoming becomes cattle country. Giant herds graze the open range. <chuckle> Rich ranchers from the East, and even British aristocrats, build a fancy club in Cheyenne, with a wine cellar and a chef. Cheyenne becomes, for a while, one of the richest towns for its size in the world.

And in nineteen-oh-two, a novel set in Wyoming, "The Virginian", invents the cowboy hero we still know. The quiet man in the white hat, the showdown at sundown.

<short pause> Buffalo Bill Cody, the famous showman, founds his own town in Wyoming. Cody.

<laugh> In eighteen ninety-seven, Cheyenne holds its first Frontier Days. Bucking broncos, roping, wild horse races. It becomes one of the biggest outdoor rodeos in the world. They call it "the Daddy of 'em All".

<short pause> One horse, named Steamboat, is so hard to ride that he becomes a legend. A cowboy on a bucking horse has been on Wyoming's licence plates since nineteen thirty-six.

## Part 4B

But the range is getting crowded. In eighteen ninety-two, big ranchers accuse small ranchers of stealing their cattle.

<gasp> So they hire a private army of gunmen from Texas, ride north, and kill two men they accuse of rustling. But the whole county rises up against the invaders, and surrounds them at a ranch.

<groan> The army has to ride in and rescue the hired gunmen. The Johnson County War. None of them is ever convicted.

<chuckle> And outlaws like Butch Cassidy hide in a red-walled canyon called Hole-in-the-Wall. Cassidy even spends a couple of years in Wyoming's state prison, for stealing a horse.

## Part 4C (quiet style)

In September eighteen eighty-five, in the coal town of Rock Springs, white miners, angry that the coal company hires Chinese workers for lower pay, and that the Chinese won't join a strike, attack the Chinese part of town.

<breath> They burn it down.

<long pause> At least twenty-eight Chinese miners are killed. Hundreds more flee into the hills.

<breath> The army escorts the survivors back to Rock Springs, and soldiers stay in the town for thirteen years, to protect them.

## Part 4D

In nineteen-oh-six, President Theodore Roosevelt makes Devils Tower America's first national monument.

<chuckle> Thirteen years earlier, on the Fourth of July, two ranchers had climbed it, by hammering wooden pegs into a crack, like a giant ladder. A thousand people came to watch. They planted a flag on top.

<short pause> In nineteen seventy-seven, the tower becomes famous all over again, as the landing site for aliens in the movie "Close Encounters of the Third Kind".

## Part 5: Oil and Coal

Under Wyoming lies oil. And coal. A lot of it.

In the nineteen-twenties, a government official secretly leases a federal oil field in Wyoming, named after a rock shaped like a teapot, to his friends. In exchange for bribes.

<tsk> The Teapot Dome scandal. He becomes the first cabinet member ever sent to prison.

<short pause> Meanwhile, in nineteen-oh-two, in the little mining town of Kemmerer, a young shopkeeper opens a store called the Golden Rule. He sells only for cash, at fair prices. <chuckle> He and his family live in the attic above the shop.

His name is James Cash Penney. J.C. Penney. Within thirty years, he has more than a thousand stores.

## Part 5B

Also in the nineteen-twenties, Wyoming's governor dies. His widow, Nellie Tayloe Ross, runs for the job. And wins.

<short pause> In nineteen twenty-five, she becomes the first woman governor in American history. Later, she runs the U.S. Mint for twenty years. Her face is even on a medal.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

During the Second World War, more than ten thousand Japanese Americans are forced into Heart Mountain, a camp of barracks in northern Wyoming. For a while, it's the third-biggest town in the state.

<breath> Some young men there refuse to be drafted into the army while their families are locked behind barbed wire. They are sent to federal prison.

## Part 6: Today

Today, the Powder River Basin produces a huge share of America's coal. <gasp> Trains more than a mile long carry it away, one after another, all day.

<whispers> And under the prairie near Cheyenne, nuclear missiles sit in underground silos, ready since the Cold War. Wind turbines spin across the plains. And tourists flood into Yellowstone and Grand Teton.

<chuckle> And Grand Teton National Park exists partly thanks to a secret. In the nineteen-twenties, the enormously rich John D. Rockefeller Junior quietly buys up ranches in Jackson Hole, through a company with a bland name, so nobody knows it's him. <short pause> Then he gives the land to the nation. Local ranchers are furious. Today, it's one of the most beautiful parks in America.

<sigh> In nineteen eighty-eight, fires burn about a third of Yellowstone. Smoke fills the sky for months. <short pause> But the forest grows back, thicker than before.

And Wyoming still has fewer than six hundred thousand people. The fewest of any state.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In nineteen ninety-eight, near Laramie, a gay university student named Matthew Shepard is beaten and left tied to a fence, in the freezing night.

<long pause> He dies days later.

His death helps lead to a federal hate crimes law.

## Part 6C

So why would the Wild West be the first to let women vote?

<whispers> It starts with a territory that had six men for every woman.

Wyoming needed women. Some of its lawmakers thought it was right. Some thought it was good advertising. <chuckle> Some thought it was a prank.

But once women could vote, Wyoming refused to give it up. Even for statehood.

<breath> The Equality State didn't get everything right. But it got that right first.

<chuckle> And it was willing to wait a hundred years to prove it.
