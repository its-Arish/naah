# The Entire History of Minnesota

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,200 spoken words, so roughly 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen sixty-two"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, icy, awed storyteller of lakes and forests. |
| Part 2 | Paddling, singing voyageur energy, then serious. |
| Part 3 | Industrious and booming, a little cheeky. |
| Part 4 | Rugged and inventive, with a grin. |
| Part 5 | Warm, quirky and proud; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1680 · Fur and Forts 1680 to 1858 · Statehood and War 1858 to 1865 · Mill City 1865 to 1930 · Iron and Ideas 1900 to 1960 · Today 1960 to now.

## Hook

Cheerios? <short pause> Minnesota. Spam? Minnesota. Scotch tape and Post-it notes? Both Minnesota.

<chuckle> "The Land of Ten Thousand Lakes"? It actually has more than eleven thousand.

The biggest shopping mall in America is in Minnesota. With a theme park inside it.

<long pause> In eighteen sixty-two, the United States hanged thirty-eight Dakota men in Minnesota, on a single day. The largest mass execution in American history.

<short pause> So how does a frozen state, far from any ocean, end up feeding America its breakfast?

<whispers> It starts with the only great waterfall on the Mississippi River.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Twenty thousand years ago, Minnesota is buried under ice, more than a mile thick. <gasp> When the glaciers melt, they leave a lake bigger than all of today's Great Lakes put together. Lake Agassiz.

And they leave thousands of holes in the ground, scraped out and dropped by the ice. They fill with water. Lakes. Everywhere.

<chuckle> Minnesota has more shoreline than California, Florida and Hawaii put together.

The Mississippi River starts here, as a little stream flowing out of Lake Itasca. You can walk across it on stepping stones.

<chuckle> In eighteen thirty-two, an explorer named Henry Schoolcraft is led to that lake by an Ojibwe guide. He wants a grand name for the source of the great river. So he takes two Latin words, "veritas caput", meaning "true head", chops off the ends, and glues the middle together. Itasca.

## Part 1B

For thousands of years, this is the land of the Dakota. Later, the Ojibwe move in from the east. <short pause> In late summer, they gather wild rice from the lakes, two people in a canoe, one pushing with a pole, the other knocking the rice grains into the boat with sticks. In spring, they tap maple trees and boil the sap into sugar.

In the southwest, there's a quarry of soft red stone. Pipestone. For centuries, nations from all over the plains come to carve sacred pipes from it. <whispers> It's considered a place of peace.

<laugh> And in eighteen ninety-eight, a Swedish farmer near Kensington claims he's dug up a stone, tangled in the roots of a tree, carved with Viking runes. It says Norse explorers reached Minnesota in thirteen sixty-two. <tsk> Most experts say it's a hoax. But it's still on display, in a museum in Alexandria, next to a giant Viking statue.

## Part 1C

Where the Minnesota River flows into the Mississippi, there's a place the Dakota call Bdote. It's sacred. For many Dakota, it's where their people began.

And a few miles upstream, the Mississippi drops over a wide ledge of rock. A roaring waterfall.

<short pause> In sixteen eighty, a French priest named Louis Hennepin is captured by the Dakota, and travels with them. He sees the falls and names them after his patron saint. The Falls of Saint Anthony.

<chuckle> Back in Europe, he writes a best-selling book about his adventures. He makes the falls sound about twice as tall as they really are. And claims a few discoveries that weren't his.

## Part 2: Fur and Forts

French and British fur traders paddle through the lakes. <laugh> Voyageurs, in giant birch-bark canoes, paddling sixty strokes a minute, for fourteen hours a day, singing French songs to keep the rhythm.

<groan> When the river ends, they carry everything over land. Each man carries two bundles of fur on his back, about ninety pounds each. At Grand Portage, on Lake Superior, they carry them nine miles uphill.

## Part 2B

In eighteen nineteen, the U.S. Army builds Fort Snelling on the bluff above Bdote. <short pause> Among the people who live there for a while: an enslaved man named Dred Scott. He meets and marries an enslaved woman named Harriet at the fort. Years later, they'll point to their time at Fort Snelling, in free territory, when they sue for their freedom.

<sigh> In eighteen fifty-one, the Dakota are pressured to sign treaties giving up most of their land, in exchange for promised money and food.

And in eighteen fifty-eight, Minnesota becomes the thirty-second state.

## Part 2C

When the Civil War begins, Minnesota's governor happens to be in Washington. He's the very first to offer troops to President Lincoln.

<gasp> At Gettysburg, the First Minnesota is ordered to charge a much bigger Confederate force, to buy a few minutes of time while reinforcements arrive. <short pause> They do it. Most of the men who charge are killed or wounded. But the line holds.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

In the summer of eighteen sixty-two, the Dakota are starving. The government's promised payments are late. Traders refuse to give them food on credit. <breath> One trader is quoted saying, if they're hungry, "let them eat grass".

In August, some Dakota warriors attack. War spreads across southern Minnesota. Hundreds of settlers are killed.

<long pause> When the war ends, military trials last just minutes each. More than three hundred Dakota men are sentenced to death. President Lincoln reduces the list to thirty-nine. One more is spared.

On December twenty-sixth, eighteen sixty-two, in Mankato, thirty-eight Dakota men are hanged together.

<breath> Around sixteen hundred Dakota, mostly women and children, are held in a camp below Fort Snelling through the winter. Many die. Then the Dakota are expelled from Minnesota.

## Part 2E

<gasp> In September eighteen seventy-six, the most famous outlaw gang in America, Jesse James and the Younger brothers, ride into the little town of Northfield, Minnesota, to rob the bank.

<chuckle> They pick the wrong town. The cashier refuses to open the safe. And the townspeople grab their hunting rifles, and shoot back from the windows and street corners.

<groan> In minutes, the gang is shattered. Two outlaws are dead. Jesse and Frank James escape. The Younger brothers are caught in a swamp two weeks later. <short pause> Northfield still celebrates every September. Defeat of Jesse James Days.

## Part 3: Mill City

Now, the waterfall.

St. Anthony Falls is the only great natural waterfall on the whole Mississippi. <short pause> And falling water means power.

At first, it powers sawmills. Minnesota's huge pine forests are cut down, and the logs are floated down the rivers to the falls. <chuckle> Lumberjacks tell tall tales about a giant logger named Paul Bunyan and his blue ox, Babe. Minnesota towns still have giant statues of them.

<gasp> In eighteen sixty-nine, the river eats a tunnel under the falls, and they almost wash away completely. The whole city turns out to save them. Today, the falls are held in place by a concrete apron.

## Part 3B

Then come flour mills.

Minnesota's farms grow mountains of wheat. And the mills at the falls grind it into flour. <short pause> Minnesota's hard spring wheat used to make dark, speckled flour. So millers invent new machines, with rollers and air blowers, that make it pure white.

<gasp> By the eighteen-eighties, Minneapolis is the flour-milling capital of the world. Its mills grind enough flour every day to make millions of loaves of bread.

People call it the Mill City.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

But flour dust can explode. On an evening in May eighteen seventy-eight, the giant Washburn "A" Mill blows up.

<breath> The blast is heard for miles. The roof flies into the sky. Fire spreads to the mills around it.

<long pause> Eighteen workers are killed.

## Part 3D

The mills rebuild, safer and bigger. Two rival companies grow giant. One run by the Pillsbury family. The other, Washburn-Crosby, becomes General Mills.

<chuckle> And they start making breakfast.

General Mills invents a cooking expert to answer customers' letters. She signs every reply. She gets her own radio show. <laugh> She doesn't exist. Her name is Betty Crocker.

Then cereals. Wheaties, "the Breakfast of Champions", in nineteen twenty-four. And in nineteen forty-one, a little puffed oat "O". At first it's called Cheerioats. Today we call them Cheerios.

## Part 3E (quiet style)

In September eighteen ninety-four, in a dry summer, forest fires join together around the logging town of Hinckley.

<breath> The firestorm destroys the town in hours. Hundreds of people crowd onto a train. The engineer drives it backwards through the flames, to a swamp and a lake.

<long pause> More than four hundred people die. Hundreds more are saved by that train.

## Part 4: Iron and Ideas

In the north, miners discover a giant range of iron ore. The Mesabi Range. <gasp> Its ore lies so close to the surface that they can dig it with steam shovels, in giant open pits.

The town of Hibbing finds out it's sitting right on top of the ore. <laugh> So in the nineteen-twenties, the whole town moves. Houses, a hotel, a church... rolled on logs and wheels, two miles down the road.

<short pause> The miners need to get to work. So in nineteen fourteen, a Hibbing car salesman starts driving them in a big car, for fifteen cents a ride. His little bus line grows into Greyhound.

During the Second World War, much of America's iron comes from Minnesota.

<short pause> The mines are worked by immigrants from all over Europe. Finns, Slovenes, Italians, Croats, Swedes, dozens of languages in one town. <chuckle> Next to Hibbing, the Hull-Rust mine grows so huge that people call it the Grand Canyon of the North.

## Part 4B

In nineteen-oh-two, five businessmen in the town of Two Harbors start a company to mine a hard mineral for making sandpaper. The Minnesota Mining and Manufacturing Company.

<groan> The mineral turns out to be worthless.

<chuckle> So they figure out how to make sandpaper anyway. Then, in nineteen twenty-five, a young employee named Richard Drew watches car painters struggle with tape that ruins their paint. He invents masking tape. Then clear, sticky Scotch tape. Later, the company invents Post-it notes.

Today, you know the company as 3M.

## Part 4C

In nineteen thirty-seven, the Hormel company in Austin, Minnesota, launches a canned meat. Spam. During the Second World War, it feeds millions of soldiers around the world.

In Rochester, a country doctor and his two sons build a small clinic into one of the most famous hospitals on Earth. The Mayo Clinic.

<gasp> And in nineteen fifty-two, surgeons at the University of Minnesota perform the first successful open-heart surgery, on a five-year-old girl, cooling her body with ice to slow her heart.

<short pause> And in nineteen fifty-seven, in a Minneapolis garage, an engineer named Earl Bakken builds a small, battery-powered heart pacemaker, so a child with a heart problem doesn't have to be plugged into the wall. His company, Medtronic, becomes one of the biggest medical device makers in the world.

## Part 5: Today

Minnesota also gives the world some remarkable people. <chuckle> Judy Garland, who sings "Over the Rainbow". Bob Dylan, a kid from Hibbing, who wins the Nobel Prize for Literature. Prince, from Minneapolis. The writer F. Scott Fitzgerald. And Charles Schulz, who draws Charlie Brown and Snoopy.

And the pilot Charles Lindbergh, who grows up on a farm by the Mississippi.

## Part 5B

In nineteen sixty-two, a Minneapolis department store company opens a new discount store, with a red bullseye. Target.

<short pause> In nineteen ninety-two, the Mall of America opens near Minneapolis. More than five hundred stores. And a theme park with roller coasters, indoors.

<laugh> In Minnesota, you need an indoor roller coaster. It's minus twenty outside.

<chuckle> Every summer, almost two million people visit the Minnesota State Fair, where nearly everything comes on a stick. Pork chop on a stick. Pickle on a stick. Deep-fried candy bar on a stick. <short pause> And there's a giant butter sculpture contest. The winner's head is carved out of a ninety-pound block of butter.

<laugh> Minnesotans are crazy about hockey, too. In nineteen-eighty, a team of young American college players, coached by a man from St. Paul, Herb Brooks, and full of Minnesotans, beats the mighty Soviet Union at the Olympics. The Miracle on Ice.

<chuckle> In nineteen ninety-one, on Halloween, a blizzard dumps more than two feet of snow on Minneapolis. Kids go trick-or-treating in snowsuits.

## Part 5C (quiet style)

In August two thousand seven, the I-thirty-five-W bridge over the Mississippi in Minneapolis collapses during rush hour.

<long pause> Cars fall into the river. Thirteen people are killed.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

In May two thousand twenty, in Minneapolis, a police officer kneels on George Floyd's neck for more than nine minutes.

<breath> He dies. Protests spread around the world.

## Part 5E

So how does a frozen state, far from any ocean, end up feeding America its breakfast?

<whispers> It starts with the only great waterfall on the Mississippi River.

The falls powered the mills. The mills turned Minnesota's wheat into the world's flour. <short pause> And the flour companies turned into General Mills and Pillsbury, putting cereal in bowls across America.

<breath> And the waterfall is still there, in the middle of Minneapolis, next to the ruins of the old mills.

<chuckle> Probably surrounded by people eating Cheerios.
