## Hook

Kool-Aid? <short pause> Invented in Nebraska. The TV dinner? Nebraska.

Arbor Day? Also Nebraska. On the very first one, people planted about a million trees.

And Nebraska's legislature has just one house. On the ballot, nobody's even listed as a Republican or a Democrat.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-nine, a Ponca chief named Standing Bear had to go to court to prove that a Native American was a person.

And on a prairie once called the Great American Desert, people planted a whole forest. By hand.

<short pause> So how does a treeless land become one of the biggest farm states in America... and grow a forest by hand?

<whispers> It starts with a newspaper editor who missed his trees.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Nebraska is prairie. Grass, sky, and wind. In the west, the Sandhills, a giant sea of grass-covered sand dunes.

The Platte River runs across it. Wide and shallow. The Otoe word for it means "flat water". That's where the name Nebraska comes from.

The Pawnee live in big round earth lodges along the rivers, farming corn and hunting buffalo. The Omaha, the Ponca and the Otoe live along the Missouri. The Lakota, the Cheyenne and the Arapaho ride the western plains.

## Part 2: The Desert

In eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark paddle up the Missouri along Nebraska's eastern edge.

In eighteen-twenty, another army explorer, Stephen Long, crosses the plains and writes that this land is "almost wholly unfit for cultivation". His map labels it "the Great American Desert".

<chuckle> For decades, people believe him. They just pass through.

And they pass through by the hundreds of thousands. The Oregon Trail, the Mormon Trail and the California Trail all follow the Platte River across Nebraska. Pioneers steer by a tall rock spire called Chimney Rock.

## Part 2B

Then, in eighteen sixty-two, Congress passes the Homestead Act. Live on a piece of land for five years, farm it, and a hundred and sixty acres are yours. Almost free.

On January first, eighteen sixty-three, just after midnight, a Union soldier named Daniel Freeman files one of the very first claims. Near Beatrice, Nebraska.

In eighteen sixty-seven, Nebraska becomes the thirty-seventh state. And the Union Pacific Railroad builds west from Omaha, carrying even more settlers.

## Part 2C

But there's a problem. There are almost no trees.

No trees means no wood. No wood for houses, fences or fires.

So settlers build houses out of the ground itself. They cut the tough prairie sod into bricks, and stack them into walls. Sod houses. "Soddies".

<chuckle> They're cool in summer, warm in winter... and sometimes, a snake falls out of the ceiling.

For fuel, they burn dried buffalo and cow dung.

## Part 2D

And in eighteen seventy-four, the sky goes dark. Not with clouds. With grasshoppers.

Billions of Rocky Mountain locusts sweep across the plains, eating crops, laundry and even the wooden handles of tools.

<chuckle> Then, mysteriously, within a few decades, that species of locust disappears completely. It's now extinct.

## Part 3: Standing Bear (quiet style)

The settlers' land comes from Native nations.

In eighteen seventy-seven, the government forces the Ponca from their homeland on the Niobrara River, in northern Nebraska, to Indian Territory, in Oklahoma. Many die.

Among them is the teenage son of a Ponca chief named Standing Bear. The boy asks to be buried at home.

In the winter of eighteen seventy-nine, Standing Bear and a small group set out, walking hundreds of miles back to Nebraska to bury him.

The army arrests them.

## Part 3B

But some people in Omaha take up his case. Lawyers sue the army on his behalf. The government argues that an Indian is not a "person" under the law, and has no right to sue.

In court, Standing Bear raises his hand and says, "That hand is not the colour of yours, but if I prick it, the blood will flow, and I shall feel pain. I am a man."

The judge rules for him. A Native American is a person under the law.

Standing Bear goes home. And buries his son by the Niobrara.

## Part 4: Trees and Water

Now, the editor.

In eighteen fifty-four, a young man named J. Sterling Morton moves to Nebraska from Michigan, a state of forests. He runs a newspaper. And he misses trees. Terribly.

He plants them around his house. And he keeps telling everyone else to plant them too. For shade. For fuel. For windbreaks against the endless wind.

In eighteen seventy-two, he convinces Nebraska to hold a special day just for planting trees.

On April tenth, eighteen seventy-two, Nebraskans plant about a million trees. Arbor Day.

## Part 4B

The idea spreads across the country. Congress even offers settlers extra land if they plant trees on it.

And in nineteen-oh-two, a Nebraska botanist named Charles Bessey convinces the government to try something crazy. Plant a forest in the Sandhills. In sand.

Workers plant seedlings by hand, row by row. It works. The Nebraska National Forest becomes the biggest hand-planted forest in the United States.

## Part 4C

And the water? Under Nebraska lies part of a giant underground lake. The Ogallala Aquifer.

In the nineteen-forties, a Nebraska farmer named Frank Zybach invents a long sprinkler pipe on wheels that rolls slowly around a field in a circle. Centre-pivot irrigation.

Today, fly over the Great Plains, and you'll see thousands of giant green circles. Many of them started with his idea.

The "Great American Desert" becomes one of America's biggest producers of corn and beef.

## Part 5: Today

Nebraska also gives America its own way of doing government. In nineteen thirty-seven, at the urging of Senator George Norris, it switches to a single-house legislature, with no party labels on the ballot. It's still the only one.

In nineteen twenty-seven, in Hastings, a man named Edwin Perkins turns a fruit syrup into a powder, so it's cheaper to mail. Kool-Aid.

In nineteen fifty-three, an Omaha company called Swanson puts a whole turkey dinner in a tray. The TV dinner.

And in Omaha lives one of the richest men in the world, Warren Buffett. In the same house he bought in nineteen fifty-eight.

## Part 5B

So how does a treeless land become one of the biggest farm states in America... and grow a forest by hand?

It starts with a newspaper editor who missed his trees.

Morton taught Nebraskans that you don't have to accept the land the way you found it. They planted windbreaks, then a whole forest. Then they pulled water up from under the plains and turned the desert green.

<short pause> The explorers called it a desert. Nebraskans just got to work.

<chuckle> And on hot days, they had Kool-Aid.
