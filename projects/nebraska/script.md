# The Entire History of Nebraska

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,150 spoken words, so roughly 11 to 12 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first four facts; slower and serious from "In eighteen seventy-nine"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Wide, windy, open-sky storyteller. |
| Part 2 | Dusty trail voice, sceptical, then hardy and wry. |
| Part 3 | Dignified, serious and moving. |
| Part 4 | Hopeful and green, practical and inventive. |
| Part 5 | Cheerful, quirky, Midwestern pride; smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1804 · The Desert 1804 to 1862 · Homesteaders 1862 to 1880 · Standing Bear 1877 to 1879 · Trees and Water 1872 to 1950 · Today 1950 to now.

## Hook

Kool-Aid? <short pause> Invented in Nebraska. The TV dinner? Nebraska.

<chuckle> Arbor Day? Also Nebraska. On the very first one, people planted about a million trees.

And Nebraska's legislature has just one house. On the ballot, nobody's even listed as a Republican or a Democrat.

<long pause> In eighteen seventy-nine, a Ponca chief named Standing Bear had to go to court to prove that a Native American was a person.

And on a prairie once called the Great American Desert, people planted a whole forest. By hand.

<short pause> So how does a treeless land become one of the biggest farm states in America... and grow a forest by hand?

<whispers> It starts with a newspaper editor who missed his trees.

## Part 1: First Peoples

Nebraska is prairie. Grass, sky, and wind.

<gasp> Twelve million years ago, a volcano far to the west, near where Idaho is now, explodes. Ash drifts a thousand miles, and settles over a watering hole in Nebraska. <sigh> Rhinos, camels and three-toed horses breathe the dust, and die where they stand. Today, at Ashfall Fossil Beds, you can see their whole skeletons, still lying together. Mothers with their babies.

<chuckle> Later, mammoths roam here too. More mammoth fossils have been found in Nebraska than almost anywhere. The mammoth is the state fossil. And a giant skeleton nicknamed Archie stands in the state museum in Lincoln.

In the west, there's the Sandhills. A giant sea of grass-covered sand dunes, one of the biggest areas of dunes in the Western Hemisphere.

## Part 1B

The Platte River runs across Nebraska. Wide and shallow. <chuckle> People joke that it's "a mile wide and an inch deep". The Otoe word for it means "flat water". That's where the name Nebraska comes from.

<gasp> Every spring, the Platte hosts one of the greatest bird gatherings on Earth. Hundreds of thousands of sandhill cranes stop there to rest and feed, on their way north. Most of the sandhill cranes in the world. <short pause> At dawn, the sky is full of their rattling calls.

The Pawnee live in big round earth lodges along the rivers, farming corn and hunting buffalo. They watch the stars closely, and their lodges are built to line up with them. The Omaha, the Ponca and the Otoe live along the Missouri. The Lakota, the Cheyenne and the Arapaho ride the western plains.

## Part 2: The Desert

In eighteen-oh-four, Lewis and Clark paddle up the Missouri along Nebraska's eastern edge.

<tsk> In eighteen-twenty, another army explorer, Stephen Long, crosses the plains and writes that this land is "almost wholly unfit for cultivation". His map labels it "the Great American Desert".

<chuckle> For decades, people believe him. They just pass through.

And they pass through by the hundreds of thousands. The Oregon Trail, the Mormon Trail and the California Trail all follow the Platte River across Nebraska. <short pause> Pioneers steer by a tall rock spire called Chimney Rock. Many of them write that it's the most amazing thing they've ever seen. Then they reach the giant bluffs of Scotts Bluff.

<short pause> In the winter of eighteen forty-six, thousands of Latter-day Saints, driven out of Illinois, camp on the Missouri River, near today's Omaha. Winter Quarters. <sigh> Hundreds die of cold and disease before they head west in the spring.

## Part 2B

Then, in eighteen sixty-two, Congress passes the Homestead Act. Live on a piece of land for five years, farm it, and a hundred and sixty acres are yours. Almost free.

<gasp> On January first, eighteen sixty-three, just after midnight, a Union soldier named Daniel Freeman files one of the very first claims. Near Beatrice, Nebraska.

In eighteen sixty-seven, Nebraska becomes the thirty-seventh state. And the Union Pacific Railroad builds west from Omaha, carrying even more settlers.

<short pause> Omaha booms. Its stockyards grow into some of the biggest in the world. Cattle arrive by the trainload, and leave as beef. <chuckle> Omaha even claims to have invented the Reuben sandwich, at a hotel poker game.

## Part 2C

But there's a problem. <sigh> There are almost no trees.

No trees means no wood. No wood for houses, fences or fires.

So settlers build houses out of the ground itself. They cut the tough prairie sod into bricks, and stack them into walls. Sod houses. "Soddies".

<chuckle> They're cool in summer, warm in winter... <laugh> and sometimes, a snake falls out of the ceiling.

For fuel, they burn dried buffalo and cow dung. <chuckle> Children are sent out with baskets to collect it. They call them "cow chips".

<short pause> Among the homesteaders are Black families, too. In Custer County, a photographer takes a picture of the Speese family, standing proudly in front of their sod house. <short pause> And in nineteen-oh-four, a new law offers six hundred and forty acres in the dry Sandhills. The settlers who take it are called "Kinkaiders".

## Part 2D

And in eighteen seventy-four, the sky goes dark. Not with clouds. With grasshoppers.

<gasp> Billions of Rocky Mountain locusts sweep across the plains, eating crops, laundry and even the wooden handles of tools. <groan> Trains slide to a stop on tracks greasy with crushed insects.

<chuckle> Then, mysteriously, within a few decades, that species of locust disappears completely. It's now extinct.

## Part 2E

<laugh> In eighteen eighty-two, in North Platte, the famous scout and buffalo hunter Buffalo Bill Cody organises a Fourth of July celebration, with riding, shooting and roping contests. The Old Glory Blowout.

It's such a hit that he turns it into a travelling show. Buffalo Bill's Wild West. <short pause> It tours America and Europe for thirty years, with cowboys, Native performers, sharpshooters, and even a staged stagecoach robbery. <chuckle> Queen Victoria comes to watch.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

On January twelfth, eighteen eighty-eight, the morning is unusually warm. Children walk to school without coats.

<breath> In the afternoon, a blizzard hits out of nowhere. Temperatures fall to far below zero. Snow so thick you can't see your hand.

<long pause> Across Nebraska and the Dakotas, hundreds of people die. Many of them children, trying to walk home. It's remembered as the Children's Blizzard.

## Part 3: Standing Bear (quiet style)

The settlers' land comes from Native nations.

<breath> In eighteen seventy-seven, the government forces the Ponca from their homeland on the Niobrara River, in northern Nebraska, to Indian Territory, in Oklahoma. Many die.

Among them is the teenage son of a Ponca chief named Standing Bear. <long pause> The boy asks to be buried at home.

In the winter of eighteen seventy-nine, Standing Bear and a small group set out, walking hundreds of miles back to Nebraska to bury him.

The army arrests them.

## Part 3B

But some people in Omaha take up his case. A newspaper editor spreads the story. Lawyers sue the army on his behalf. <tsk> The government argues that an Indian is not a "person" under the law, and has no right to sue.

In court, Standing Bear raises his hand and says, "That hand is not the colour of yours, but if I prick it, the blood will flow, and I shall feel pain. I am a man."

<gasp> The judge rules for him. A Native American is a person under the law.

<breath> Standing Bear goes home. And buries his son by the Niobrara.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

<breath> Two years earlier, in September eighteen seventy-seven, at Fort Robinson in northwest Nebraska, the Lakota war leader Crazy Horse, who had surrendered months before, is led toward a guardhouse.

<long pause> In a struggle at the door, he is stabbed with a soldier's bayonet. He dies that night.

## Part 3D

His interpreter at the trial is a young Omaha woman named Susette La Flesche, called Bright Eyes. She later tours the East with Standing Bear, speaking for Native rights.

<short pause> Her younger sister, Susan La Flesche, goes to medical school, and in eighteen eighty-nine becomes the first Native American woman to earn a medical degree. <gasp> She comes home and becomes the only doctor for the whole Omaha reservation. Hundreds of square miles. She makes house calls on horseback, through snow, day and night.

## Part 4: Trees and Water

Now, the editor.

In eighteen fifty-four, a young man named J. Sterling Morton moves to Nebraska from Michigan, a state of forests. He runs a newspaper. <sigh> And he misses trees. Terribly.

He plants them around his house. And he keeps telling everyone else to plant them too. For shade. For fuel. For windbreaks against the endless wind.

He convinces Nebraska to hold a special day just for planting trees.

<gasp> On April tenth, eighteen seventy-two, Nebraskans plant about a million trees. Arbor Day.

<chuckle> Nebraska even used to call itself the Tree Planters State.

## Part 4B

The idea spreads across the country. Congress even offers settlers extra land if they plant trees on it.

And in nineteen-oh-two, a Nebraska botanist named Charles Bessey convinces the government to try something crazy. Plant a forest in the Sandhills. <laugh> In sand.

Workers plant seedlings by hand, row by row. It works. <short pause> The Nebraska National Forest becomes the biggest hand-planted forest in the United States.

## Part 4C

<short pause> A girl who moves to the Nebraska prairie as a child, Willa Cather, grows up to write novels about the immigrant pioneers she knew there. Czechs, Swedes, Germans, Danes, struggling with the land. "My Ántonia". <chuckle> She wins the Pulitzer Prize.

And in nineteen seventeen, in Omaha, a priest named Father Edward Flanagan starts a home for boys with nowhere else to go. Boys Town. <short pause> It becomes so big that it's its own village, with its own post office.

## Part 4D

And the water? Under Nebraska lies part of a giant underground lake. The Ogallala Aquifer.

<gasp> In the nineteen-forties, a Nebraska farmer named Frank Zybach invents a long sprinkler pipe on wheels that rolls slowly around a field in a circle. Centre-pivot irrigation.

Today, fly over the Great Plains, and you'll see thousands of giant green circles. Many of them started with his idea.

## Part 4E

<gasp> In December nineteen forty-one, right after Pearl Harbor, the people of North Platte hear that a troop train is coming through. They gather at the station with food and gifts. <short pause> And they keep doing it. Every train. Every day. For more than four years.

The North Platte Canteen. Volunteers from more than a hundred towns bake cakes, make sandwiches, and pour coffee. <chuckle> Some soldiers are handed birthday cakes by strangers. <breath> By the end of the war, they've greeted about six million service members.

<chuckle> The "Great American Desert" becomes one of America's biggest producers of corn and beef.

## Part 5: Today

Nebraska also gives America its own way of doing government. In nineteen thirty-seven, at the urging of Senator George Norris, it switches to a single-house legislature, with no party labels on the ballot. <short pause> It's still the only one.

<chuckle> In nineteen twenty-seven, in Hastings, a man named Edwin Perkins turns a fruit syrup into a powder, so it's cheaper and lighter to mail. Kool-Aid.

<laugh> In nineteen fifty-three, an Omaha company called Swanson, the story goes, has too much turkey left over after Thanksgiving. Hundreds of thousands of pounds. So it puts a whole turkey dinner in an aluminium tray. The TV dinner.

## Part 5B

Omaha is the birthplace of a president, Gerald Ford. And of the civil rights leader Malcolm X. <short pause> And of two giants of the movies, Fred Astaire and Marlon Brando.

<chuckle> And in Omaha lives one of the richest men in the world, Warren Buffett. In the same house he bought in nineteen fifty-eight.

<laugh> On autumn Saturdays, Nebraska football fans in red fill the stadium in Lincoln. Every game has been sold out since nineteen sixty-two. <chuckle> On game days, the stadium holds more people than all but a couple of Nebraska's cities.

<laugh> In the western town of Alliance, an artist builds a full-size copy of England's Stonehenge. Out of old cars, painted grey. Carhenge.

<chuckle> Nebraskans love a Runza. Bread dough baked around beef, cabbage and onion, brought over by German families from Russia.

<short pause> Near Omaha, at Offutt Air Force Base, is the command centre for America's nuclear forces. On September eleventh, two thousand one, President George W. Bush is flown there, for safety.

## Part 5C

So how does a treeless land become one of the biggest farm states in America... and grow a forest by hand?

<whispers> It starts with a newspaper editor who missed his trees.

Morton taught Nebraskans that you don't have to accept the land the way you found it. <short pause> They planted windbreaks, then a whole forest. Then they pulled water up from under the plains and turned the desert green.

<breath> The explorers called it a desert. Nebraskans just got to work.

<chuckle> And on hot days, they had Kool-Aid.
