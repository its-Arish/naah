# The Entire History of California

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,600 spoken words, 13:50 as recorded.

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. The headings are paste breaks: they are never spoken and never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list. Emotion words such as `<amazed>` are read aloud, so they are not used.
- CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

## Hook

The entire history of California is sitting in your room right now.

<short pause> The jeans you're wearing. The phone in your hand. The app you're watching this on. The movies you grew up with. <whispers> Even the almonds in your kitchen.

<short pause> All of it came from one strip of coast. A coast named after an island that NEVER existed.

<chuckle> No, really. The name comes from a Spanish adventure novel from fifteen ten, about an island of warrior women where the only metal was gold.

<gasp> And for more than a hundred years, real mapmakers drew California as an island. A king of Spain finally had to announce it officially. California is NOT an island.

Today that not-island has one of the five biggest economies on Earth.

<short pause> But the man who found California's gold... died poor. So did the man who owned the land. So did the first millionaire the gold ever made.

<long pause> So who actually got rich? <whispers> The answer is the same in every century, and it's closer to you than you think.

## Part 1: First Californians

Let's go back thirteen thousand years. Long before the name, people were already here.

By the time Europe showed up, there were around three hundred thousand of them, speaking close to a hundred different languages.

<gasp> A HUNDRED! In one state.

No kings. No empire. Just hundreds of small nations. On the coast, the Chumash built plank canoes and paddled out to the islands. In the oak valleys, families ground acorns into flour. And they lit small, careful fires to keep the forest healthy.

<short pause> Hold on to those fires. California is going to need them.

And the gold? It was lying right there in the riverbeds. For thirteen thousand years people stepped over it, and nobody rushed anywhere.

<sigh> That was about to change.

## Part 2: Spain

Fifteen forty-two. A captain named Cabrillo sails up the coast for Spain. He is the first European to see it.

<short pause> And Spain's reaction? Nice... we'll get back to you.

<laugh> They did. Two hundred and twenty-seven years later.

In between, the English sea captain Francis Drake stopped by in fifteen seventy-nine. He fixed his ship, claimed the whole place for his queen, and left.

So what finally woke Spain up? Russia. Russian fur hunters were creeping down from Alaska, and suddenly that quiet coast looked worth keeping.

So in seventeen sixty-nine, soldiers and priests marched north. Over the next fifty-four years they built twenty-one missions, from San Diego to Sonoma, linked by one road. El Camino Real. The Royal Road.

<short pause> San Diego. Santa Barbara. San Francisco. Every one of those cities began as a mission. You have been reading Spain's to-do list on road signs your WHOLE life.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

But inside the mission walls it was a different story. Native families were brought in to work and to pray. Many were not free to leave. And the diseases that came with the newcomers moved faster than any priest.

<long pause> Tens of thousands died.

<long pause> Rush number one was for souls and for empire. It would not be the last.

## Part 3: Mexico

Eighteen twenty-one. Mexico wins its independence from Spain, and California changes flags without a battle.

Mexico closes the missions and hands out the land as giant ranches. The business is cattle. Cow hides were worth so much that sailors called them California banknotes.

Then the Americans began to trickle in. First by ship. Then, in eighteen forty-one, by wagon, straight over the mountains.

<short pause> And in June of eighteen forty-six, about thirty of them marched into the little town of Sonoma, arrested a Mexican general in his own house, and declared a brand new country. The California Republic.

<laugh> They needed a flag. So they painted a grizzly bear on a piece of cotton. People said it looked more like a pig.

<short pause> The republic lasted twenty-five days. Then the United States Navy arrived and said, thanks, we'll take it from here.

<whispers> By the way, that bear is still on the state flag. And the California grizzly? Extinct. The last one was shot in nineteen twenty-two.

<long pause> Now watch the dates, because this is the worst timing in history.

February second, eighteen forty-eight. Mexico signs a treaty and hands California to the United States.

<long pause> Nine days earlier, on a river in the California hills, a carpenter had looked down into the water.

## Part 4: Gold

<whispers> January twenty-fourth, eighteen forty-eight. James Marshall is building a sawmill for his boss, John Sutter. In the water, something shines.

<gasp> GOLD.

Remember the novel? The island where the only metal was gold? Three hundred and thirty-eight years later, the made-up story came true.

Sutter begged everyone to keep it secret. <chuckle> It stayed secret for about five minutes.

Because a shopkeeper named Sam Brannan heard the news. And before he told a single person, he quietly bought up every pick, pan and shovel he could find.

Then, the story goes, he ran through the streets of San Francisco waving a bottle of gold dust and shouting, GOLD! Gold from the American River!

<short pause> San Francisco had about a thousand people. Two years later it had twenty-five thousand. Sailors jumped off their ships and ran for the hills. Hundreds of empty ships sat rotting in the bay. Some were dragged ashore and turned into hotels.

They came from everywhere. China. Chile. Mexico. Australia. France. Around three hundred thousand people in seven years. An egg could cost a dollar. A shovel could cost a small fortune.

California skipped the waiting line completely. In eighteen fifty it became the thirty-first state.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

But the hills were not empty. Around a hundred and fifty thousand Native people lived in California when the gold was found.

<long pause> By eighteen seventy, about thirty thousand were left.

<long pause> Disease. Hunger. And killing that the new state paid for. Its first governor told lawmakers to expect a war of extermination.

In twenty nineteen, the governor of California stood in front of tribal leaders and called it what it was. A genocide. And he apologised.

## Part 4C

And the men who started it all? Sutter's workers ran off to dig. Strangers took over his land. He died still asking Congress for help. Marshall, the man who saw the gold first, ended up selling his own autograph to get by.

<short pause> So if the finder didn't get rich, and the landowner didn't get rich... who did? <whispers> Remember the shopkeeper. We'll come back to him.

## Part 5: Railroad

But first, everyone had the same problem. Getting there.

By wagon it took four to six months. By ship around the bottom of South America, about the same. People died doing both.

<short pause> So America decided to do something crazy. Build a railroad across the ENTIRE continent.

On the California side, the bosses were four men from Sacramento. <whispers> Four shopkeepers. They had made their money selling supplies to gold miners.

<short pause> Starting to see a pattern?

The men who actually built it were mostly Chinese. At the peak, about nine out of every ten workers on the western half. More than twelve thousand men, cutting through solid mountain granite, through winters with snow deeper than a house. For less pay than the white workers beside them.

<short pause> May tenth, eighteen sixty-nine. The two tracks meet. Six months becomes ONE week.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

And the thanks? In eighteen eighty-two, the United States passed a law banning Chinese workers from entering the country.

<long pause> It was the first time America shut its door on one nationality.

## Part 5C

One of those four shopkeepers was named Leland Stanford. He spent part of his fortune on a new university, built on his horse farm south of San Francisco.

<whispers> Remember that farm. It is going to matter.

## Part 6: Sunshine

Now the trains needed passengers. So the railroads did something new. They advertised a place.

Posters. Orange crates painted like paradise. Come to California, where it is summer all year. <chuckle> During one price war, a ticket from Kansas City to Los Angeles dropped to one dollar.

In eighteen seventy, Los Angeles had under six thousand people. By nineteen thirty it had over a MILLION.

<long pause> Up north, San Francisco learned what the ground could do. April eighteenth, nineteen oh six. An earthquake, then three days of fire. About three thousand dead. Most of the city gone.

<short pause> They rebuilt it in under ten years.

Down south, Los Angeles had a different problem. A million people were coming to a place with almost no water.

So the city's water chief, William Mulholland, built an aqueduct. Two hundred and thirty-three miles long. It reached into a farming valley called Owens Valley and drank the river.

<short pause> When the water arrived in nineteen thirteen, Mulholland gave a speech. Five words. There it is. Take it.

<sigh> Owens Lake dried into a bowl of dust. And a small group of insiders, who had quietly bought the empty land where the water would arrive? <whispers> They got very, very rich.

<short pause> Around the same time, some people with cameras showed up. They wanted sunshine almost every day, with mountains, desert and ocean all a short drive apart. They set up in a quiet suburb.

<short pause> It was called Hollywood.

<long pause> New rush. Same rule. And the next crowd arrived with nothing left to lose.

## Part 7: Dust and war

The nineteen thirties. The middle of America turns to dust. Farms blow away. More than three hundred thousand people load everything onto old cars and drive west, to the place from the posters.

<sigh> California did not roll out a welcome mat. In nineteen thirty-six, Los Angeles sent its own police to the state border to turn poor families around.

<short pause> The state built by newcomers... tried to close the door.

Those who got in picked fruit for pennies, on land owned by somebody else.

<long pause> Then, in nineteen thirty-seven, an impossible bridge opened across the Golden Gate. And four years later, everything changed again.

<short pause> War. California became America's factory on the Pacific. Planes in the south. Ships in the north. The shipyards of one town near San Francisco built seven hundred and forty-seven ships. <gasp> One of them was put together in under FIVE days.

Millions came for the work. Between nineteen forty and nineteen fifty, the population jumped from under seven million to over ten.

## Part 7B (quiet style)

But in nineteen forty-two, the government forced about a hundred and twenty thousand people of Japanese descent out of their homes on the West Coast, and into camps behind barbed wire.

<long pause> Two out of three were American citizens.

<long pause> They lost farms, shops and homes. The official apology came forty-six years later.

## Part 7C

After the war, the soldiers who had passed through remembered the weather. They came back with families. Freeways. Suburbs. A theme park in an orange grove. By the early nineteen sixties, California had more people than any other state.

## Part 8: Silicon

Remember Stanford's farm?

By the nineteen thirties, the valley around it was famous for one thing. Fruit. Plums, cherries, apricots. People called it the Valley of Heart's Delight.

<whispers> Then two Stanford graduates started a company in a garage. Hewlett and Packard.

In nineteen fifty-six, one of the inventors of the transistor moved to the valley, to be near his mother. He hired eight brilliant young engineers.

<chuckle> He was such a difficult boss that all eight quit together. They started their own company. Then their people left and started more. One of those was Intel.

The chips were made of silicon, which comes from sand. Somebody called the place Silicon Valley, and the fruit trees never came back.

<short pause> October, nineteen sixty-nine. A computer in Los Angeles sends the first message on the network that will become the internet. The message was supposed to be the word login. <laugh> The system crashed after two letters. So the first word ever sent online was... lo.

<short pause> Nineteen seventy-six. Two friends named Steve, and another garage. Apple. Nineteen ninety-eight. Two students, and another garage. Google.

<short pause> California had found gold again. This time it was made of sand.

## Part 9: Today

Add up every one of those rushes, and today about one in every nine Americans lives in California.

<short pause> And the bill has arrived. A typical home costs about twice the American average. More people sleep on the street here than in any other state. And the fires keep getting bigger. In twenty eighteen, a single fire erased a town called Paradise. In January twenty twenty-five, fires burned into Los Angeles itself and took more than sixteen thousand buildings.

<long pause> Remember the small, careful fires from the very beginning? <whispers> California is now working with the tribes to bring those fires back.

<long pause> And there is a new rush. Artificial intelligence. Once again, the world is running to San Francisco.

<short pause> So. The question I promised you at the start. Who actually gets rich in California?

Not the man who found the gold. Not the man who owned the land.

Sam Brannan, the shopkeeper with the shovels? He became California's first millionaire. <sigh> Then he lost it all, and died with almost nothing.

The railroad? Four shopkeepers. The water? The men who bought the land first.

<short pause> And one more shopkeeper. He arrived in San Francisco in eighteen fifty-three. He never dug for gold. Not once. He sold cloth. Then, in eighteen seventy-three, he and a tailor put metal rivets on a pair of work pants.

<whispers> His name was Levi Strauss.

<short pause> And in today's rush? One of the most valuable companies on Earth is not digging for A.I. gold at all. It sits in Santa Clara, and it sells the chips. <chuckle> The shovels.

<long pause> The gold ran out. The grizzly is gone. The island never existed.

<short pause> But look down.

<short pause> The jeans are still here.

That is the entire history of California. Everybody comes for the gold. <short pause> The smart ones sell the shovels.
