# The Entire History of South Carolina

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,550 spoken words, so roughly 13 to 14 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused for the first three facts; slower and serious from "Before the Civil War"; leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm storyteller of marsh and river, with a wink. |
| Part 2 | Serious, steady and honest. |
| Part 3 | Brave and fiery, a little cheeky. |
| Part 4 | Tense and heavy, then bold. |
| Part 5 | Measured, hopeful, then determined. |
| Part 6 | Lively and warm; thoughtful at the end, with a smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Peoples 10,000 BC to 1670 · Charles Town 1670 to 1700 · Carolina Gold 1700 to 1775 · Palmetto State 1775 to 1830 · Secession 1830 to 1865 · Reconstruction 1865 to 1900 · Long Road 1900 to 2015 · Today 2015 to now.

## Hook

The first golf club in America? <short pause> Charleston, South Carolina. The dance called the Charleston? Named after it. And a South Carolina town calls itself the birthplace of sweet tea.

<sigh> The first shots of the Civil War were fired in Charleston Harbour.

<long pause> Before the Civil War, most of the people living in South Carolina were enslaved. More than half.

And Charleston was one of the richest cities in America. Built on a grain of rice.

<short pause> So how does one small state become one of the richest colonies in America... and then start the deadliest war in American history?

<whispers> It starts with a handful of seed rice from the other side of the world.

## Part 1: First Peoples

South Carolina rises in steps. Flat salt marshes and sea islands on the coast. Then pine forests and sandy hills. Then red clay. Then the Blue Ridge Mountains.

For thousands of years, it's home to many nations. The Catawba, the Cherokee, the Yamasee, the Cusabo, and many others.

<gasp> On the coast, more than four thousand years ago, people pile up oyster shells into giant rings, some of them hundreds of feet across. Nobody is quite sure why. Feasts? Ceremonies? Villages?

In fifteen forty, the Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto marches through, looking for gold. He meets a powerful woman who rules a chiefdom called Cofitachequi. <short pause> She welcomes him, and hangs a string of pearls around his neck.

<tsk> He repays her by taking her prisoner. She escapes.

## Part 1B

Europeans keep trying to build colonies on this coast. And they keep failing.

In fifteen sixty-two, French Protestants build a little fort called Charlesfort. Their leader sails home for supplies, and doesn't come back. <groan> The men left behind build a boat out of local wood, with sails made of their own shirts, and set off across the Atlantic. Out at sea, the food runs out. It's a horrible voyage. Only some of them make it home.

In fifteen sixty-six, the Spanish build a town called Santa Elena, on what's now Parris Island. It's the capital of Spanish Florida for a while. It lasts about twenty years.

## Part 1C

Then, in sixteen seventy, English settlers arrive. Many come from Barbados, a small Caribbean island where planters have grown rich on sugar, worked by enslaved Africans.

They found Charles Town, named after King Charles the Second. Today, Charleston. Ten years later, they move it onto a narrow peninsula, between two rivers, the Ashley and the Cooper.

<chuckle> Charlestonians like to say it's where the Ashley and the Cooper rivers meet... to form the Atlantic Ocean.

<sigh> And the settlers from Barbados bring the Barbados way of doing things with them.

## Part 2: Carolina Gold

At first, the colony struggles. It trades deerskins with Native nations. Hundreds of thousands of them, shipped to England to make leather. <sigh> And it buys Native people captured in wars, and sells them as slaves to the Caribbean.

Then, around sixteen eighty-five, as the story goes, a ship's captain blown off course from Madagascar gives a Charleston settler a small bag of seed rice.

<short pause> The rice grows beautifully in the warm, wet lowlands. It's so profitable that people call it "Carolina Gold".

But growing rice in swamps is brutal, dangerous, skilled work. Clearing cypress swamps full of snakes and alligators. Building earth banks and wooden floodgates. Flooding and draining the fields at exactly the right time, with the rising and falling tides.

## Part 2B

And the planters know who has that skill.

People from the rice-growing coast of West Africa. Today's Senegal, Gambia, Sierra Leone.

<breath> So the planters buy enslaved Africans by the tens of thousands. Many experts believe that African knowledge is what made Carolina rice work.

Charleston becomes the busiest port for enslaved people in North America. Historians estimate that around four in ten enslaved Africans brought into what became the United States came through Charleston.

<long pause> By the early seventeen-hundreds, enslaved Africans outnumber white settlers in the colony.

## Part 2C (quiet style)

In seventeen fifteen, the Yamasee and their allies, tired of cheating traders and of their people being captured and sold, rise up against the colony.

<breath> Hundreds of settlers are killed. Traders are killed in their villages. Refugees crowd into Charleston.

The colony almost ends. When the war is over, the Yamasee are driven south, out of their homeland.

## Part 2D

On the sea islands, cut off from the mainland, enslaved people keep much of their African culture. Their words, their food, their stories, their music, and their sweetgrass baskets, woven the same way as in West Africa.

It becomes the Gullah Geechee culture. <short pause> It's still alive today. Some of the words you know, like "goober" for peanut, and maybe even "gumbo", come from West African languages, through people like the Gullah.

## Part 2E (quiet style)

In seventeen thirty-nine, near the Stono River, about twenty enslaved men rise up. They march south, toward Spanish Florida, which promises freedom. More people join them, beating drums.

<breath> The militia catches them. Dozens are killed.

It's the largest slave revolt in the British colonies. Afterward, the colony passes even harsher laws. It even bans enslaved people from playing drums.

## Part 2F

Meanwhile, Charleston's harbour has another problem. <chuckle> Pirates.

In seventeen eighteen, Blackbeard blockades the harbour and holds Charleston's people hostage, until the town hands over... a chest of medicine.

The town gets its revenge. That same year, it hangs the "gentleman pirate" Stede Bonnet, a rich landowner who bought a ship and went pirating, apparently out of boredom. <tsk> Dozens of other pirates are hanged in Charleston that year.

## Part 2G

In the seventeen-forties, a sixteen-year-old girl named Eliza Lucas is left in charge of her father's three plantations, while he's away in the Caribbean.

She experiments with indigo, a plant that makes deep blue dye. Year after year, frost and failure. <phew> Finally, it works. Soon indigo is the colony's second great crop, after rice.

By the time of the Revolution, the planters around Charleston are some of the richest people in British America.

## Part 3: Palmetto State

In June seventeen seventy-six, a fleet of British warships attacks a little fort on Sullivan's Island, at the mouth of Charleston Harbour. The fort is made of sand and soft palmetto logs.

<gasp> The cannonballs sink into the spongy palmetto wood, like it's a cushion. The fort holds.

When the fort's flag is shot down, a sergeant named William Jasper jumps out, under fire, grabs it, and puts it back up.

<laugh> The British sail away, beaten by sand and squishy trees. That's why there's a palmetto tree on South Carolina's flag.

## Part 3B

The British come back. In seventeen eighty, they capture Charleston, and more than five thousand American soldiers. <groan> One of the worst American defeats of the war.

But South Carolina doesn't give up. A soldier named Francis Marion hides in the swamps with his men, ambushing British troops and vanishing again. <chuckle> The British call him the "Swamp Fox".

At Kings Mountain, frontiersmen from over the mountains wipe out a Loyalist army. At Cowpens, the Americans trick the British with a fake retreat, and win in under an hour. <short pause> It's the beginning of the end for the British in the South.

In seventeen eighty-eight, South Carolina becomes the eighth state.

## Part 3C

<chuckle> And in seventeen eighty-six, a group of Charleston merchants form the South Carolina Golf Club. The first golf club in America. Golf, in the swamp.

Then, in seventeen ninety-three, the cotton gin makes a new crop rich. Cotton spreads across the red clay hills of the upcountry. <sigh> And slavery spreads with it.

## Part 4: Secession

Everything in South Carolina now rests on slavery. And as the North grows against it, South Carolina's planters grow afraid.

Their leader is John C. Calhoun. He argues that a state can cancel federal laws it doesn't like. In eighteen thirty-two, South Carolina tries it, over a tax on imports.

<tsk> President Andrew Jackson, born right on the Carolina border himself, threatens to send in the army. South Carolina backs down. For now.

## Part 4B

In eighteen fifty-six, in the U.S. Senate, an anti-slavery senator from Massachusetts, Charles Sumner, gives a fierce speech. He insults a South Carolina senator.

<gasp> Two days later, that senator's cousin, a South Carolina congressman named Preston Brooks, walks into the Senate and beats Sumner with a heavy cane, until it breaks. Sumner is so badly hurt he can't return to the Senate for three years.

<tsk> Back home in South Carolina, Brooks is a hero. People send him new canes.

The country is coming apart.

## Part 4C

By eighteen sixty, more than half the people in South Carolina are enslaved.

In November, Abraham Lincoln is elected president. He wants to stop slavery from spreading west.

<short pause> Six weeks later, on December twentieth, eighteen sixty, South Carolina becomes the first state to leave the Union. Its declaration says it plainly. It is leaving to protect slavery.

## Part 4D

In Charleston Harbour, there's a federal fort, still flying the American flag. Fort Sumter.

<long pause> At four-thirty in the morning, on April twelfth, eighteen sixty-one, Confederate cannons open fire on it. People in Charleston climb onto their rooftops to watch.

The fort surrenders the next day. <short pause> Amazingly, nobody is killed by the bombardment.

But the Civil War has begun. It will kill more Americans than any other war.

## Part 4E

In May eighteen sixty-two, an enslaved ship's pilot named Robert Smalls waits until the white officers go ashore for the night.

<whispers> Then, before dawn, he takes the Confederate ship Planter. He picks up his wife, his children and other families. He puts on the captain's straw hat, so that from the shore, he looks like the captain.

And he sails right past the Confederate forts, giving the correct signals. <phew> Out at sea, he raises a white bedsheet, and hands the ship to the U.S. Navy.

<chuckle> He later becomes a congressman. And buys the house of the man who once enslaved him.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

In eighteen sixty-four, a Confederate submarine, the Hunley, sinks a Union warship off Charleston. The first submarine ever to sink a ship in battle.

<breath> It sinks too. All eight men aboard die.

<short pause> For more than a hundred years, the Hunley lies lost on the sea floor. Divers find it in nineteen ninety-five. In two thousand, it's raised. And in two thousand four, its crew is finally buried, in Charleston.

In February eighteen sixty-five, much of the capital, Columbia, burns, as Sherman's army passes through.

## Part 5: Reconstruction

During the war, on the sea islands, teachers arrive from the North and open the Penn School, one of the first schools for people freed from slavery. <short pause> Grown men and women sit beside children, learning to read.

After the war, South Carolina becomes the first state with a legislature where most members are Black. Formerly enslaved men help write a new constitution, with public schools for everyone.

Jonathan Jasper Wright becomes the first Black justice on any state supreme court in America.

<sigh> It doesn't last. Armed white groups terrorise Black voters. By the eighteen-nineties, Black South Carolinians are pushed out of voting, and Jim Crow laws take hold.

## Part 5B (quiet style)

On the night of August thirty-first, eighteen eighty-six, the ground under Charleston starts to shake.

<breath> It's one of the strongest earthquakes ever recorded in the eastern United States. Chimneys fall. Brick walls crack open. Around sixty people are killed.

You can still see the iron rods and bolts that were put into Charleston's old houses afterwards, to hold their walls together.

## Part 5C

In eighteen seventy-five, in Mayesville, South Carolina, a girl named Mary McLeod is born. The fifteenth of seventeen children, most of whom were born into slavery.

<short pause> She picks cotton. And she walks miles to go to school. She grows up to found a college in Florida, and to advise President Franklin Roosevelt. Mary McLeod Bethune. One of the most important teachers in American history.

## Part 5D

Hundreds of thousands of Black South Carolinians move north, looking for a better life.

But one of the cases that ends school segregation starts right here. In Clarendon County, white children ride buses to school. Black children walk, sometimes miles each way.

Black parents ask for a school bus. They're refused. <short pause> Their lawsuit, Briggs versus Elliott, becomes part of Brown versus Board of Education. In nineteen fifty-four, the Supreme Court rules that school segregation is unconstitutional.

<tsk> In nineteen fifty-seven, South Carolina's senator Strom Thurmond talks for twenty-four hours and eighteen minutes, without stopping, to try to block a civil rights bill. The longest one-man speech in Senate history. <short pause> The bill passes anyway.

## Part 5E (quiet style)

In nineteen sixty-eight, in Orangeburg, students protest a bowling alley that won't let Black people in.

<breath> State police fire on them. Three young men are killed.

## Part 6: Music and Machines

<chuckle> But South Carolina also gives America its moves.

In the nineteen-twenties, a dance called the Charleston takes over the world. Its steps are often traced back to Black dancers in South Carolina, and the famous Jenkins Orphanage Band of Charleston plays it on street corners.

Dizzy Gillespie, one of the greatest jazz trumpet players ever, is from Cheraw, South Carolina. <short pause> And James Brown, the Godfather of Soul, grows up in Barnwell and Augusta, just across the river.

On Parris Island, the old Spanish town of Santa Elena, the Marines build a boot camp. <groan> Hundreds of thousands of new Marines learn to march, and suffer, in its sandflies and summer heat.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

In June two thousand fifteen, a young white man joins a Bible study at Emanuel AME Church in Charleston, the oldest Black church of its kind in the South.

<long pause> He sits with them for an hour. Then he opens fire. Nine Black worshippers are killed.

<breath> Families of the victims stand up in court, and speak about forgiveness.

Weeks later, South Carolina removes the Confederate battle flag from its State House grounds.

## Part 6C

Today, South Carolina builds BMWs in Spartanburg, at the company's biggest factory in the world. Boeing builds giant jets near Charleston. <short pause> And millions of tourists come to Myrtle Beach, and to Charleston's old streets of pastel houses.

<chuckle> And here's something Georgia doesn't like to hear. South Carolina, not Georgia, grows more peaches. The town of Gaffney even has a water tower shaped like a giant peach.

## Part 6D

So how does one small state become one of the richest colonies in America... and then start the deadliest war in American history?

<whispers> It starts with a handful of seed rice from the other side of the world.

Rice made the planters rich. To grow it, they enslaved tens of thousands of skilled Africans, until the enslaved were the majority. <short pause> The planters built everything on slavery. And when they feared losing it, they left the Union, and fired the first shots.

<breath> But the same rice fields also gave America the Gullah Geechee, Robert Smalls, and the parents who walked their children toward the end of segregation.

<chuckle> And, somehow, America's very first golf club.
