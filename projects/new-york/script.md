# The Entire History of New York

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 2,450 spoken words, so roughly 17 to 19 minutes when recorded.

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Curious, quick, playful documentary narrator for the first four facts. Then slow and serious from "But that wall". Leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, warm storyteller with a sense of wonder. |
| Part 2 | Light and wry, like telling a story about a messy business deal. |
| Part 3 | Brisk, amused, then serious where the text turns serious. |
| Part 4 | Tense and dramatic, building suspense. |
| Part 5 | Excited and admiring, like describing an impossible engineering bet that pays off. |

## Hook

Wall Street? Named after a real wall. Built by the Dutch.

The word cookie? Dutch too. So is boss. And Santa Claus.

Manhattan, bought for twenty-four dollars? <short pause> The Lenape probably didn't think they were selling anything at all.

And for about a year... New York was called New Orange.

<long pause> But that wall was built partly by enslaved Africans. And on Wall Street, for fifty years, there was a market... where people were sold.

<short pause> So how does a muddy Dutch trading post, built by people who weren't free, become the capital of the world?

<whispers> It starts with a ditch. Four feet deep.

## Part 1: First New Yorkers

Let's go back. Way back.

Twenty thousand years ago, New York is under ice. A sheet of it, taller than any skyscraper you've ever seen. When it melts, it leaves behind rocks, lakes, and a long sandy island we now call Long Island.

People arrive soon after.

In the south, around the harbour, live the Lenape. They fish, they gather oysters, and they have a name for one long rocky island at the river's mouth. Mannahatta. Usually translated as... the island of many hills.

Today, Manhattan has hardly any hills left. They got flattened to build streets.

Upstate, five nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca, have done something remarkable. They've stopped fighting each other and joined into one league. The Haudenosaunee. The Iroquois Confederacy.

They have a constitution, passed down by memory, called the Great Law of Peace. Each nation keeps its own council, but they decide big things together.

Some historians think the men who wrote the American Constitution were paying attention.

## Part 2: New Netherland

In sixteen oh nine, an Englishman named Henry Hudson, working for a Dutch company, sails a ship called the Half Moon into the harbour. He's looking for a shortcut to China.

He sails up a great river, hoping it goes all the way through.

It doesn't. <chuckle> But the river still has his name.

What the Dutch find instead is fur. Beaver fur. In Europe, beaver hats are the height of fashion, and the forests here are full of beavers.

So the Dutch West India Company sets up a trading post at the tip of Mannahatta. New Amsterdam.

And in sixteen twenty-six, the famous deal. A company man named Peter Minuit "buys" the island from the Lenape for goods worth sixty guilders.

Two hundred years later, a historian converts that into dollars and gets... twenty-four. The legend is born.

But the Lenape almost certainly didn't think they were selling the island forever. To them, it was more like an agreement to share it. A gift to seal a deal. Not a price tag.

New Amsterdam is small, muddy, and wonderfully chaotic. Pigs run in the streets. Taverns everywhere. By some counts, eighteen languages are spoken in a town of a few hundred people.

And here's the thing about the Dutch. They're traders first. They don't care much what you believe, as long as you do business. That idea, everyone welcome, as long as you're working, never leaves this city.

The Dutch leave other things too. Words. Cookie, from koekje. Boss, from baas. Coleslaw. And a saint called Sinterklaas... who will one day become Santa Claus.

Even place names. Brooklyn comes from Breukelen. Harlem from Haarlem.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

The Dutch bring something else.

In sixteen twenty-six, the company brings eleven enslaved Africans to New Amsterdam. They clear land. They build roads. They build the fort.

In sixteen fifty-three, the governor, Peter Stuyvesant, orders a wall across the island to keep out attackers.

Enslaved Africans do much of the work.

The street that ran along it is still there.

Wall Street.

## Part 3: New York

The wall doesn't help.

In sixteen sixty-four, English warships sail into the harbour. Stuyvesant wants to fight. The townspeople don't. They're merchants. Fighting is bad for business.

So New Amsterdam surrenders without a shot. The English king gives it to his brother, the Duke of York.

New... York.

Then, nine years later, a Dutch fleet sails back in and takes it again. And this time they rename it after their own prince. New Orange.

<chuckle> It lasts about a year. In the peace treaty, the Dutch give it back to England. In return, they get a colony in South America, Suriname.

So New York could easily have been New Orange. Or still New Amsterdam.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

Under the English, slavery grows.

By the seventeen hundreds, New York City has one of the largest enslaved populations of any city in the colonies.

In seventeen eleven, the city opens an official market for selling human beings. At the bottom of Wall Street.

It stays open for fifty years.

In nineteen ninety-one, workers digging foundations for a government building in Lower Manhattan find a cemetery.

Around fifteen thousand people of African descent were buried there. Forgotten. Built over.

Today, it's a national monument. The African Burial Ground.

New York didn't end slavery until July fourth, eighteen twenty-seven.

## Part 4: Revolution

In seventeen seventy-six, the American colonies declare independence. And Britain sends the largest fleet it has ever sent anywhere. Straight to New York.

Hundreds of ships fill the harbour. People said it looked like a forest of masts.

George Washington loses the Battle of Brooklyn. Badly. He escapes across the East River at night, in fog, and the British take New York City.

They keep it for the whole war. Seven years.

But upstate, in seventeen seventy-seven, at Saratoga, an entire British army surrenders. It's the turning point. France sees that the Americans can actually win, and joins the war.

When the British finally leave New York in seventeen eighty-three, Washington rides back into the city.

And then New York becomes the first capital of the United States.

On April thirtieth, seventeen eighty-nine, on a balcony on... Wall Street, George Washington takes the oath as the first president.

Three years later, a couple of blocks away, twenty-four brokers sign an agreement under a buttonwood tree. It's the beginning of the New York Stock Exchange.

## Part 4B

And a quick one, from the War of eighteen twelve.

In Troy, New York, a meat packer named Samuel Wilson ships barrels of beef to the army, stamped U.S.

Soldiers from Troy know him by his nickname. Uncle Sam.

They joke that the U.S. on the barrels stands for Uncle Sam.

In nineteen sixty-one, Congress officially saluted Sam Wilson of Troy as the original Uncle Sam. That pointing guy on the poster? He started as a New York meat packer.

## Part 5: The Ditch

Now. The ditch.

In the early eighteen hundreds, New York City is not the biggest port in America. Philadelphia, Boston and New Orleans are serious rivals.

And the whole country has a problem. The farms of the West are on the other side of the mountains. Getting a barrel of flour to the coast costs more than the flour is worth.

So the governor of New York, DeWitt Clinton, has an idea that everyone thinks is insane.

Dig a canal. From Lake Erie to the Hudson River. Three hundred and sixty-three miles. Across forests, swamps and cliffs. With shovels, horses and gunpowder.

Thomas Jefferson called the idea little short of madness. People mocked it as Clinton's Ditch.

It's forty feet wide. And just four feet deep.

Workers dig for eight years. They build eighty-three locks to lift boats up and down hills.

And on October twenty-sixth, eighteen twenty-five, the Erie Canal opens. Clinton rides a boat all the way from Buffalo to New York City. And at the end, he pours a barrel of water from Lake Erie into the Atlantic Ocean.

The wedding of the waters.

<short pause> And it works. Better than anyone dreamed. Shipping costs crash. Grain, timber, everything from the West now flows down the canal, down the Hudson, and into one port.

New York City.

Within fifteen years, New York's port moves more freight than Boston, Baltimore and New Orleans combined. The city's population quadruples in twenty-five years. Banks follow the goods. Wall Street follows the banks.

That's how a muddy trading post becomes an empire. A ditch, four feet deep.

The Empire State finally has its empire.

## Part 6: Golden Door

The ditch brings goods. Then it brings people.

Irish families escaping famine. Germans. Then Italians. Jewish families from Eastern Europe. Chinese. Greeks. Poles.

In eighteen eighty-six, France gives America a gift. A copper statue, a hundred and fifty-one feet tall, holding a torch over the harbour. The Statue of Liberty.

Six years later, right next to her, Ellis Island opens.

From eighteen ninety-two to nineteen fifty-four, about twelve million immigrants pass through it. The first one through the door is a teenage girl from Ireland, Annie Moore.

Today, a huge share of all Americans can trace an ancestor through that one small island.

In eighteen ninety-eight, Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island join into one giant city. Overnight, New York becomes the second-largest city on Earth, after London.

And upstate, women gather in a small town called Seneca Falls, back in eighteen forty-eight, and declare that all men AND women are created equal. It's the beginning of the fight for women's right to vote.

## Part 6B (quiet style)

But the city is crowded. And work is dangerous.

On March twenty-fifth, nineteen eleven, a fire breaks out at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory, on the top floors of a building near Washington Square.

Some of the exit doors are locked.

One hundred and forty-six workers die. Most of them are young immigrant women. Some are just fourteen.

A young woman named Frances Perkins watched it from the street.

New York rewrote its labour laws because of that fire. Fire exits. Sprinklers. Limits on hours.

Twenty years later, Frances Perkins becomes America's first female cabinet member. She brings those ideas to the whole country.

## Part 7: Skyline

The nineteen twenties. Jazz. Harlem. The Harlem Renaissance, a burst of Black music, writing and art that changes American culture forever.

A horse-racing writer hears stable hands call New York's races the big apple. The big prize. He starts using it in his columns. The name sticks to the whole city.

And the city goes up.

In nineteen twenty-nine, the stock market crashes. On Wall Street. And the Great Depression begins.

But even then, New York keeps building. The Chrysler Building. And the Empire State Building, finished in nineteen thirty-one, in just over thirteen months. The tallest building in the world, for the next forty years.

Then World War Two. New York's harbour sends soldiers and supplies across the Atlantic. And after the war, the countries of the world choose New York for the headquarters of the United Nations.

The muddy trading post is now... the capital of the world.

## Part 8: Hard times

But capitals can fall.

In the nineteen sixties and seventies, factories close. Families move to the suburbs. The city runs out of money.

In nineteen seventy-five, New York almost goes bankrupt. When it asks Washington for help, a famous newspaper headline sums up the answer: Ford to City: Drop Dead.

Crime rises. Subway cars are covered in graffiti.

And yet... out of the hard times comes some of the most creative work in the world.

In June nineteen sixty-nine, at the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village, gay patrons fight back against a police raid. It sparks the modern movement for gay rights.

Two months later, up in the hills of Bethel, New York, nearly half a million people show up for a music festival called Woodstock.

And in the summer of nineteen seventy-three, at a party in the Bronx, a young DJ called Kool Herc starts playing just the drum breaks of records, over and over, so people can dance. It's one of the first sparks of hip hop.

The city that nearly died... reinvented music.

## Part 8B (quiet style)

On September eleventh, two thousand one, terrorists fly two hijacked planes into the twin towers of the World Trade Center.

Two thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven people are killed. Most of them in New York City.

Firefighters run up the stairs while everyone else runs down.

The city mourns.

And then, it rebuilds.

## Part 9: Today

Today, the tallest building in the Western Hemisphere stands where the towers fell. One World Trade Center. One thousand seven hundred and seventy-six feet tall. For the year of independence.

New York City is home to more than eight million people. More languages are spoken here than almost anywhere on Earth. Just like New Amsterdam... only a lot bigger.

Upstate, Buffalo gave the world buffalo wings. Niagara Falls lit up whole cities with some of the first big hydroelectric power. And the old canal still runs, now mostly for boats on holiday.

## Part 9B

So, how does a muddy Dutch trading post, built by people who weren't free, become the capital of the world?

A wall that didn't work. A deal the Lenape never thought was a sale. A harbour, and a crowd of people who came from everywhere.

But mostly... a ditch. Four feet deep. That everyone said was madness.

<short pause> And the people who built this place? The enslaved Africans who built the wall. The Irish who dug the canal. The women in the factory. The millions through the golden door.

New York never says thank you. It just keeps building.

<chuckle> And next time someone hands you a cookie... say dank je wel.

That's Dutch for thank you.
