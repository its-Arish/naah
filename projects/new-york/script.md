# The Entire History of New York

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,700 spoken words, so roughly 19 to 21 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Curious, quick, playful documentary narrator for the first four facts. Then slow and serious from "But that wall". Leaning in, almost whispering, on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, warm storyteller with a sense of wonder. |
| Part 2 | Light and wry, like telling a story about a messy business deal. |
| Part 3 | Brisk, amused, then serious where the text turns serious. |
| Part 4 | Tense and dramatic, building suspense, then proud. |
| Part 5 | Excited and admiring, like describing an impossible engineering bet that pays off. |
| Part 6 | Warm and energetic, a city filling up with people. |
| Part 7 | Fast, dazzled, a city racing upward. |
| Part 8 | Gritty, then defiant and proud. |
| Part 9 | Bright, then calm and certain for the final lines. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First New Yorkers 10,000 BC to 1609 · New Netherland 1609 to 1664 · New York 1664 to 1775 · Revolution 1776 to 1789 · The Ditch 1817 to 1825 · Empire 1825 to 1865 · Golden Door 1883 to 1911 · Skyline 1920 to 1945 · Hard times 1960 to 1990 · Today 2001 to now.

## Hook

Wall Street? <short pause> Named after a real wall. Built by the Dutch.

The word cookie? Dutch too. So is boss. And Santa Claus.

Manhattan, bought for twenty-four dollars? <short pause> The Lenape probably didn't think they were selling anything at all.

<chuckle> And for about a year... New York was called New Orange.

<long pause> But that wall was built partly by enslaved Africans. And on Wall Street, for fifty years, there was a market... where people were sold.

<short pause> So how does a muddy Dutch trading post, built by people who weren't free, become the capital of the world?

<whispers> It starts with a ditch. Four feet deep.

## Part 1: First New Yorkers

Let's go back. <short pause> Way back.

Twenty thousand years ago, New York is under ice. A sheet of it, taller than any skyscraper you've ever seen. When it melts, it leaves behind rocks, lakes, and a long sandy island we now call Long Island.

People arrive soon after.

<breath> In the south, around the harbour, live the Lenape. They fish, they gather oysters, and they have a name for one long rocky island at the river's mouth. Mannahatta. Usually translated as... the island of many hills.

<chuckle> Today, Manhattan has hardly any hills left. They got flattened to build streets.

The harbour is so full of oysters that, centuries later, New York will be called the oyster capital of the world. Street carts sell them like hot dogs.

## Part 1B

<short pause> Upstate, something even more remarkable is happening.

Five nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca, have been at war with each other for generations. Then, the story goes, a messenger called the Peacemaker and a grieving man named Hiawatha travel from nation to nation, convincing them to bury their weapons.

<short pause> Literally. Under a great white pine tree. The Tree of Peace.

The five nations join into one league. The Haudenosaunee. Later, a sixth nation, the Tuscarora, joins them.

They have a constitution, passed down by memory, called the Great Law of Peace. Each nation keeps its own council, but they decide big things together. And the leaders are chosen... by the women. The clan mothers.

<short pause> Some historians think the men who wrote the American Constitution were paying attention. In nineteen eighty-eight, the United States Congress passed a resolution saying so.

## Part 2: New Netherland

In sixteen-oh-nine, an Englishman named Henry Hudson, working for a Dutch company, sails a ship called the Half Moon into the harbour. He's looking for a shortcut to China.

He sails up a great river, hoping it goes all the way through.

<chuckle> It doesn't. But the river still has his name.

What the Dutch find instead is fur. Beaver fur. In Europe, beaver hats are the height of fashion, and the forests here are full of beavers.

So the Dutch West India Company sets up a trading post at the tip of Mannahatta. New Amsterdam.

<short pause> And in sixteen twenty-six, the famous deal. A company man named Peter Minuit "buys" the island from the Lenape for goods worth sixty guilders.

Two hundred years later, a historian converts that into dollars and gets... twenty-four. <chuckle> The legend is born.

<breath> But the Lenape almost certainly didn't think they were selling the island forever. To them, land couldn't really be owned. It was more like an agreement to share it. A gift to seal a deal. Not a price tag.

## Part 2B

New Amsterdam is small, muddy, and wonderfully chaotic. Pigs run in the streets. Taverns everywhere. <gasp> By some counts, eighteen languages are spoken in a town of a few hundred people.

And here's the thing about the Dutch. They're traders first. They don't care much what you believe, as long as you do business.

The governor, Peter Stuyvesant, a stern man with a wooden leg, does care. In sixteen fifty-four, twenty-three Jewish refugees arrive from Brazil. Stuyvesant wants them gone.

<tsk> His bosses back in Amsterdam tell him no. Some of the company's own investors are Jewish. Let them stay.

<short pause> Three years later, Stuyvesant tries to ban Quaker meetings. The people of the village of Flushing write him a letter of protest. The Flushing Remonstrance. One of the earliest written demands for religious freedom in America.

<chuckle> He arrests the men who deliver it. But the idea doesn't go away.

Everyone welcome, as long as you're working. That idea never leaves this city.

## Part 2C

The Dutch leave other things too. Words. Cookie, from koekje. Boss, from baas. Coleslaw. And a saint called Sinterklaas... who will one day become Santa Claus.

Even place names. Brooklyn comes from Breukelen. Harlem from Haarlem. <chuckle> And the Bronx? Named after a farmer called Jonas Bronck.

## Part 2D (quiet style)

The Dutch bring something else.

<breath> In sixteen twenty-six, the company brings eleven enslaved Africans to New Amsterdam. They clear land. They build roads. They build the fort.

In sixteen fifty-three, Stuyvesant orders a wall across the island to keep out attackers.

<short pause> Enslaved Africans do much of the work.

The street that ran along it is still there.

<long pause> Wall Street.

## Part 3: New York

The wall doesn't help.

In sixteen sixty-four, English warships sail into the harbour. Stuyvesant wants to fight. <chuckle> The townspeople don't. They're merchants. Fighting is bad for business.

So New Amsterdam surrenders without a shot. The English king gives it to his brother, the Duke of York.

<short pause> New... York.

Then, nine years later, a Dutch fleet sails back in and takes it again. And this time they rename it after their own prince. New Orange.

<laugh> It lasts about a year. In the peace treaty, the Dutch give it back to England. In return, they keep a colony in South America. Suriname.

So New York could easily have been New Orange. Or still New Amsterdam.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

Under the English, slavery grows.

<breath> By the seventeen hundreds, New York City has one of the largest enslaved populations of any city in the colonies. In some years, about one person in five in the city is enslaved.

In seventeen eleven, the city opens an official market for selling human beings. At the bottom of Wall Street.

<long pause> It stays open for fifty years.

In seventeen forty-one, after a series of fires, rumours spread of a plot by enslaved people to burn the city. There is little real evidence.

<short pause> Thirty-four people are executed. Some are burned at the stake.

## Part 3C (quiet style)

In nineteen ninety-one, workers digging foundations for a government building in Lower Manhattan find a cemetery.

<short pause> Around fifteen thousand people of African descent were buried there. Forgotten. Built over.

Today, it's a national monument. The African Burial Ground.

<long pause> New York didn't end slavery until July fourth, eighteen twenty-seven.

## Part 3D

But New York also gives America one of its first big freedoms.

In seventeen thirty-four, a German-born printer named John Peter Zenger publishes a newspaper that mocks the royal governor. <tsk> The governor has him arrested and thrown in jail for almost ten months.

At the trial, the law is clearly against Zenger. Criticising the government is a crime. True or not.

<short pause> But his lawyer, Andrew Hamilton, turns to the jury and says, more or less: if what he printed is TRUE... how can it be a crime?

<gasp> The jury agrees. Not guilty.

It's one of the early steps toward freedom of the press in America. Written into the First Amendment, fifty-six years later.

## Part 4: Revolution

In seventeen seventy-six, the American colonies declare independence. And Britain sends the largest fleet it has ever sent anywhere. <short pause> Straight to New York.

Hundreds of ships fill the harbour. <gasp> People said it looked like a forest of masts.

George Washington loses the Battle of Brooklyn. Badly. <breath> He escapes across the East River at night, in fog, and the British take New York City.

They keep it for the whole war. Seven years.

<short pause> But upstate, in seventeen seventy-seven, at Saratoga, an entire British army surrenders. It's the turning point. France sees that the Americans can actually win, and joins the war.

When the British finally leave New York in seventeen eighty-three, Washington rides back into the city.

<chuckle> One last insult on the way out: the British nail their flag to the top of a greased flagpole, so nobody can take it down. A young sailor climbs up anyway, and replaces it with the Stars and Stripes.

## Part 4B

And then New York becomes the first capital of the United States.

On April thirtieth, seventeen eighty-nine, on a balcony on... <short pause> Wall Street, George Washington takes the oath as the first president.

A young New York lawyer named Alexander Hamilton becomes the first Treasury Secretary. He'd already founded a bank in the city, the Bank of New York. It's still in business today.

<short pause> And three years later, a couple of blocks away, twenty-four brokers sign an agreement under a buttonwood tree. <chuckle> It's just a few lines long. It's the beginning of the New York Stock Exchange.

## Part 4C

And a quick one, from the War of eighteen twelve.

In Troy, New York, a meat packer named Samuel Wilson ships barrels of beef to the army, stamped U.S.

Soldiers from Troy know him by his nickname. Uncle Sam.

<chuckle> They joke that the U.S. on the barrels stands for Uncle Sam.

In nineteen sixty-one, Congress officially saluted Sam Wilson of Troy as the original Uncle Sam. That pointing guy on the poster? He started as a New York meat packer.

## Part 5: The Ditch

<short pause> Now. The ditch.

In the early eighteen hundreds, New York City is not the obvious winner. Philadelphia, Boston and New Orleans are serious rivals.

And the whole country has a problem. The farms of the West are on the other side of the mountains. <tsk> Getting a barrel of flour to the coast costs more than the flour is worth.

So a New York politician named DeWitt Clinton has an idea that everyone thinks is insane.

Dig a canal. From Lake Erie to the Hudson River. Three hundred and sixty-three miles. Across forests, swamps and cliffs. <short pause> With shovels, horses and gunpowder.

Thomas Jefferson called the idea little short of madness. <chuckle> People mocked it as Clinton's Ditch.

It's forty feet wide. And just four feet deep.

## Part 5B

On July fourth, eighteen seventeen, the first shovel goes into the ground.

Most of the diggers are local farmers at first. Then thousands of Irish immigrants. They cut down forests, pull out stumps with giant machines, and dig through swamps full of mosquitoes. <groan> Hundreds catch fever.

At Lockport, the canal has to climb a cliff of solid rock. So they blast through it, and build a staircase of locks, five pairs, side by side. One staircase for boats going up. One for boats going down.

<short pause> No one in America had engineering schools yet. So the canal became the school. The men who learned on it went on to build railroads and canals all over the country.

Workers dig for eight years. They build eighty-three locks to lift boats up and down hills.

## Part 5C

On October twenty-sixth, eighteen twenty-five, the Erie Canal opens. Clinton, now governor, rides a boat all the way from Buffalo to New York City. And at the end, he pours a barrel of water from Lake Erie into the Atlantic Ocean.

<short pause> The wedding of the waters.

And it works. Better than anyone dreamed. <gasp> Shipping costs crash by about ninety percent. Grain, timber, everything from the West now flows down the canal, down the Hudson, and into one port.

New York City.

Within fifteen years, New York's port moves more freight than Boston, Baltimore and New Orleans combined. The city's population quadruples in twenty-five years. Banks follow the goods. Wall Street follows the banks.

<short pause> That's how a muddy trading post becomes an empire. A ditch, four feet deep.

<chuckle> The Empire State finally has its empire.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

But the money flowing into New York doesn't reach everyone.

<breath> In July eighteen sixty-three, during the Civil War, the government starts drafting men into the army. Rich men can pay three hundred dollars to get out of it. Poor men can't.

Angry mobs riot for four days. Many of the rioters turn their anger on Black New Yorkers. They beat and kill people in the streets. They burn an orphanage for Black children. The children escape.

<long pause> More than a hundred people die. It's one of the deadliest riots in American history.

## Part 6: Golden Door

The ditch brings goods. Then it brings people.

Irish families escaping famine. Germans. Then Italians. Jewish families from Eastern Europe. Chinese. Greeks. Poles.

In eighteen eighty-three, a giant new bridge opens over the East River, joining Manhattan to Brooklyn. Its engineer, Washington Roebling, falls ill while building it. So his wife, Emily Roebling, carries his instructions to the workers, learns the engineering herself, and helps finish it.

<chuckle> People are nervous it will fall down. So the next year, the showman P.T. Barnum walks twenty-one elephants across the Brooklyn Bridge. It holds.

## Part 6B

In eighteen eighty-six, France gives America a gift. A copper statue, a hundred and fifty-one feet tall, holding a torch over the harbour. <short pause> The Statue of Liberty.

Six years later, right next to her, Ellis Island opens.

From eighteen ninety-two to nineteen fifty-four, about twelve million immigrants pass through it. The first one through the door is a teenage girl from Ireland, Annie Moore. <short pause> She's given a ten-dollar gold coin for being first.

Today, a huge share of all Americans can trace an ancestor through that one small island.

In eighteen ninety-eight, Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island join into one giant city. <gasp> Overnight, New York becomes the second-largest city on Earth, after London.

In nineteen-oh-four, the first subway line opens. Within a few years, millions of people ride under the streets every day.

And upstate, years earlier, in eighteen forty-eight, women gathered in a small town called Seneca Falls, and declared that all men AND women are created equal. It's the beginning of the fight for women's right to vote.

## Part 6D

But for most new arrivals, New York means one thing. The tenement.

<breath> Narrow brick buildings, five or six storeys high, packed with families. Two rooms. Sometimes ten people. Few windows. Shared toilets in the yard.

The Lower East Side becomes one of the most crowded places on Earth.

In eighteen ninety, a reporter named Jacob Riis, an immigrant himself, takes his camera inside, using a dangerous new flash powder to light the dark rooms. He publishes the pictures in a book called How the Other Half Lives.

<short pause> Readers are shocked. New laws follow. Windows. Fire escapes. Indoor plumbing.

## Part 6E

And who runs the city? Often, a political club called Tammany Hall.

Its most famous boss, William Tweed, steals a fortune from the city. Tens of millions of dollars. <chuckle> One new courthouse ends up costing more than the Alaska Purchase.

A cartoonist named Thomas Nast draws him again and again, as a fat crook with a diamond pin. Tweed says he doesn't care what the papers write. His voters can't read. <tsk> But they can see the pictures.

Tweed is arrested, escapes, and flees to Spain. <laugh> And Spanish officials recognise him... from a Thomas Nast cartoon.

## Part 6F

Upstate is inventing too.

In Rochester, in eighteen eighty-eight, a bank clerk named George Eastman sells a simple box camera loaded with film. You take your pictures, mail the whole camera back, and get your photos. His slogan: "You press the button, we do the rest." <short pause> The company is Kodak.

In Schenectady, General Electric. In Endicott, a company that becomes IBM.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

But the city is crowded. And work is dangerous.

On March twenty-fifth, nineteen eleven, a fire breaks out at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory, on the top floors of a building near Washington Square.

<long pause> Some of the exit doors are locked.

One hundred and forty-six workers die. Most of them are young immigrant women. Some are just fourteen.

<short pause> A young woman named Frances Perkins watched it from the street.

New York rewrote its labour laws because of that fire. Fire exits. Sprinklers. Limits on hours.

<breath> Twenty years later, Frances Perkins becomes America's first female cabinet member. She brings those ideas to the whole country.

## Part 7: Skyline

The nineteen twenties. Jazz. Harlem. The Harlem Renaissance, a burst of Black music, writing and art that changes American culture forever. Duke Ellington. Langston Hughes. Zora Neale Hurston.

A horse-racing writer hears stable hands call New York's races the big apple. The big prize. <chuckle> He starts using it in his columns. The name sticks to the whole city.

And the city goes up.

<long pause> In nineteen twenty-nine, the stock market crashes. On Wall Street. And the Great Depression begins.

But even then, New York keeps building. <short pause> The Chrysler Building, with a secret steel spire hidden inside, lifted into place at the last minute to beat a rival tower.

And the Empire State Building, finished in nineteen thirty-one, in just over thirteen months. <gasp> At times, the workers add more than four floors a week. It's the tallest building in the world, for the next forty years.

## Part 7B

Then World War Two. New York's harbour sends soldiers and supplies across the Atlantic. And in an office in Manhattan, the army starts a secret project to build an atomic bomb. They name it after the city. <whispers> The Manhattan Project.

After the war, the countries of the world choose New York for the headquarters of the United Nations.

<short pause> The muddy trading post is now... the capital of the world.

## Part 7C

And one man rebuilds the whole city around cars.

Robert Moses never holds an elected office. But for forty years, he builds. Bridges. Highways. Beaches. Parks. Hundreds of playgrounds. The Triborough Bridge. Jones Beach.

<chuckle> And here's the thing. Robert Moses never learns to drive.

His highways also slice through neighbourhoods, and tens of thousands of people lose their homes. <short pause> When he tries to run an expressway through Lower Manhattan, a writer and mother named Jane Jacobs organises her neighbours, and stops him.

## Part 8: Hard times

But capitals can fall.

In the nineteen sixties and seventies, factories close. Families move to the suburbs. The city runs out of money.

In nineteen seventy-five, New York almost goes bankrupt. When it asks Washington for help, a famous newspaper headline sums up the answer: <short pause> Ford to City: Drop Dead.

<sigh> Crime rises. Subway cars are covered in graffiti. In the summer of nineteen seventy-seven, the power goes out across the city for a whole day, and looting breaks out.

<short pause> And yet... out of the hard times comes some of the most creative work in the world.

## Part 8B

In June nineteen sixty-nine, at the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village, gay patrons fight back against a police raid. It sparks the modern movement for gay rights.

Two months later, up in the hills of Bethel, New York, nearly half a million people show up for a music festival called Woodstock. <chuckle> The roads jam so badly that people abandon their cars and walk.

And in the summer of nineteen seventy-three, at a back-to-school party in the Bronx, a young DJ called Kool Herc starts playing just the drum breaks of records, over and over, so people can dance. <short pause> It's one of the first sparks of hip hop.

The city that nearly died... reinvented music.

## Part 8C (quiet style)

In August nineteen seventy-eight, families in a neighbourhood of Niagara Falls called Love Canal learn that their homes and their children's school were built on top of buried toxic waste.

<short pause> Hundreds of families are moved away. Their story leads to a national law to clean up toxic sites.

## Part 8D (quiet style)

On September eleventh, two thousand one, terrorists fly two hijacked planes into the twin towers of the World Trade Center.

<long pause> Two thousand nine hundred and seventy-seven people are killed that day. Most of them in New York City.

Firefighters run up the stairs while everyone else runs down.

<short pause> The city mourns.

<breath> And then, it rebuilds.

## Part 8E (quiet style)

And in October twenty twelve, Hurricane Sandy pushes the ocean into the city. Seawater floods the subway tunnels and cuts the power to Lower Manhattan.

<long pause> Dozens of New Yorkers are killed.

## Part 9: Today

Today, the tallest building in the Western Hemisphere stands where the towers fell. One World Trade Center. One thousand seven hundred and seventy-six feet tall. <short pause> For the year of independence.

New York City is home to more than eight million people. Hundreds of languages are spoken here. More than almost anywhere on Earth. <chuckle> Just like New Amsterdam... only a lot bigger.

Upstate, Buffalo gave the world buffalo wings. Niagara Falls lit up whole cities with some of the first big hydroelectric power. And the old canal still runs, now mostly for boats on holiday.

## Part 9B

So, how does a muddy Dutch trading post, built by people who weren't free, become the capital of the world?

<short pause> A wall that didn't work. A deal the Lenape never thought was a sale. A harbour, and a crowd of people who came from everywhere.

But mostly... a ditch. Four feet deep. That everyone said was madness.

<long pause> And the people who built this place? The enslaved Africans who built the wall. The Irish who dug the canal. The women in the factory. The millions through the golden door.

New York never says thank you. It just keeps building.

<chuckle> And next time someone hands you a cookie... say dank je wel.

<short pause> That's Dutch for thank you.
