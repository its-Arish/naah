# The Entire History of Nevada

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 3,200 spoken words, so roughly 17 to 18 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md` and `docs/HOOKS.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. Headings are paste breaks: never spoken, never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list: `<short pause>` `<long pause>` `<breath>` `<sigh>` `<exhales>` `<gasp>` `<chuckle>` `<laugh>` `<giggle>` `<phew>` `<tsk>` `<groan>` `<whispers>`. Never two tags side by side. CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

| Part | Style line for the style box |
| --- | --- |
| Hook | Quick, amused, a little sly for the first four facts; slower and serious from "Because in eighteen sixty-four"; leaning in on the last line. |
| Part 1 | Calm, awed storyteller in a desert. |
| Part 2 | Gold-rush excitement, wry humour, sweaty and dangerous underground. |
| Part 3 | Urgent and dramatic for the telegram, then brisk and cheeky, like describing loopholes. |
| Part 4 | Glitzy and fast for the casinos; tense and eerie for the bombs. |
| Part 5 | Smooth, showbiz energy, then calm. |
| Part 6 | Bright and modern, with a sly smile on the very last line. |
| Lettered parts | Use the style line of their number. |
| Any part marked "quiet style" | Quiet, slow and respectful. No smile in the voice. Long gaps between sentences. |

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Nevadans 10,000 BC to 1826 · Trails 1826 to 1859 · Silver 1859 to 1880 · Battle Born 1864 · Bust and Boom 1880 to 1930 · Anything Goes 1931 to 1945 · Atomic 1951 to 1992 · Vegas 1946 to 2000 · Today 2000 to now.

## Hook

Las Vegas? <short pause> It started as a railroad stop. In nineteen-oh-five.

Area fifty-one? The government only admitted it existed... in twenty thirteen.

In the nineteen fifties, tourists in Las Vegas threw parties on hotel roofs, to watch atomic bombs go off on the horizon.

<chuckle> And Nevada became a state by telegram. The longest, most expensive telegram anyone had ever sent.

<long pause> Because in eighteen sixty-four, Nevada didn't have nearly enough people to be a state. Washington let it in anyway.

<short pause> So how does an empty desert, rushed into the Union with too few people, become a place forty million people visit every year?

<whispers> It starts with that telegram.

## Part 1: First Nevadans

Nevada is a land of basins and ranges. Mountain, desert valley, mountain, desert valley. Over and over. Hundreds of times.

<short pause> And here's the strange thing. Most rivers here never reach the sea. They flow down from the mountains... and just stop. They sink into the sand, or spread into salty lakes that dry up in summer.

Scientists call it the Great Basin. A giant bowl, with no drain.

<breath> But it wasn't always dry. At the end of the last Ice Age, much of western Nevada was under a huge lake, almost as big as Lake Erie. Lake Lahontan. You can still see its old beaches, as lines on the sides of the mountains.

## Part 1B

People have lived here for more than ten thousand years.

In a little cave near Fallon, in nineteen-forty, archaeologists found a man buried in a woven mat, wearing moccasins. <gasp> He'd been there for more than ten thousand years. The oldest known mummy in North America. For decades, scientists and the local Paiute and Shoshone people argued over him. In twenty sixteen, DNA tests showed he was an ancestor of Native Americans today. <short pause> He was returned to his people, and reburied.

## Part 1C

Later, this is the land of the Northern Paiute, the Southern Paiute, the Western Shoshone, and the Washoe around Lake Tahoe.

<short pause> Lake Tahoe is so clear you can see down sixty feet or more. And so deep that, if you poured it out, it could cover all of California in more than a foot of water.

Further north, there's a lake in the desert with a giant rock rising from the water, shaped like a pyramid. The Paiute have fished its giant trout and a rare fish called the cui-ui for thousands of years. <chuckle> In eighteen forty-four, the explorer John Frémont sees the rock and names it. Pyramid Lake.

They know where every spring is. When every seed ripens. Where the pine nuts grow, in autumn, on the mountain slopes. They gather them by the basketful. Pine nuts can feed a family through the winter.

<chuckle> And in a cave near Lovelock, archaeologists found duck decoys made of reeds and feathers, about two thousand years old. Some of the oldest decoys in the world. People were hunting ducks in the desert... with fake ducks.

## Part 2: Trails

For most outsiders, Nevada was something to get across.

In eighteen twenty-seven, a fur trapper named Jedediah Smith becomes one of the first Americans to cross it. He nearly dies of thirst.

Then come the wagon trains, heading for California and its gold. And they dread Nevada. <groan> Especially the Forty Mile Desert. No water. No grass. Burning sand. The trail is lined with abandoned wagons and the bones of oxen.

<short pause> People don't come to stay. They come to get through it, as fast as they can.

## Part 2B

Then, in eighteen fifty-nine, miners on the slopes of Mount Davidson find a strange, heavy, blue-grey mud clogging their gold pans. <tsk> They curse it and throw it away.

Somebody finally gets the mud tested.

<gasp> It's silver. Packed with it. The richest silver strike in American history.

A loud-talking prospector named Henry Comstock talks his way into a share of the claim. <chuckle> He sells out cheap. Soon after. But his name stays on the whole thing. The Comstock Lode.

## Part 2C

Virginia City explodes. Mansions, opera houses, saloons, newspapers. In a few years, it's one of the richest towns in America.

And the mines go down. Thousands of feet down.

<breath> At that depth, the rock is hot. Boiling-hot water floods the tunnels. Miners work in heat over a hundred degrees, in short shifts, gulping ice water. The mines go through tons of ice a day.

<short pause> The silver vein is so wide, the old ways of holding up tunnels don't work. So a young German engineer, Philipp Deidesheimer, invents a new system. Square timber frames, stacked like giant building blocks, one on top of another. It's copied by mines all over the world.

Comstock silver helps pay for the Union in the Civil War. And it builds much of San Francisco.

<chuckle> And for a few years, miners haul salt and supplies across the desert... on camels. Real camels, brought from Asia. They work. But they scare the horses and mules so badly that in eighteen seventy-five, Nevada passes a law banning camels from its public roads. <laugh> Some are just let loose in the desert. People keep spotting wild camels for decades.

## Part 2D

In eighteen sixty-three, a young reporter at Virginia City's newspaper signs a story with a new pen name.

<short pause> Mark Twain.

<laugh> He writes made-up stories as if they were news, gets into fights with rival editors, and the next year leaves town in a hurry... after challenging another newspaperman to a duel. Duelling has just been made illegal.

## Part 2E

In eighteen sixty, for eighteen months, young riders gallop across Nevada for the Pony Express, carrying mail from Missouri to California in about ten days.

<breath> In Nevada, the stations are tiny, lonely huts in the desert, a day's ride apart. Riders swap horses every ten or fifteen miles, and keep going. Through snow. Through dust storms. Through war.

Then the telegraph line reaches across the country. <chuckle> Two days later, the Pony Express shuts down.

## Part 2F (quiet style)

As miners and settlers move in, they cut down the piñon pine trees for firewood and mine timber. The Paiute's food disappears.

<breath> In eighteen sixty, near Pyramid Lake, fighting breaks out between Paiute and settlers. Volunteer militias march in. Many are killed on both sides.

Afterward, the Paiute are pushed onto small reservations, often on land nobody else wanted.

## Part 2G

One young Paiute woman fights back with words. Sarah Winnemucca.

She learns English as a girl. She works as a translator for the army. She travels to Washington, meets the president, and gives hundreds of speeches across the country about how her people are being treated.

<gasp> In eighteen eighty-three, she publishes a book. Life Among the Piutes. It's thought to be the first book ever published by a Native American woman.

Today, her statue stands in the United States Capitol.

## Part 3: Battle Born

Then the Civil War.

President Lincoln wants to pass the Thirteenth Amendment, to end slavery forever. He needs votes in Congress. And he faces a tough re-election.

<short pause> Nevada Territory is solidly on his side. But it only has around forty thousand people. Far fewer than a state normally needs. More than a hundred thousand, by the usual rule.

Washington lets it in anyway.

## Part 3B

There's just one problem. The new state constitution has to reach Washington. <short pause> Fast. Before the election.

There's no railroad across the country yet. A rider could take weeks.

So in October eighteen sixty-four, a telegraph operator in Carson City starts tapping out the entire constitution. Word by word. Dot by dot. Sixteen thousand words.

<groan> It takes most of two days.

It costs more than four thousand dollars. A fortune. <gasp> The longest and most expensive telegram ever sent, up to that time.

On October thirty-first, eighteen sixty-four, Nevada becomes the thirty-sixth state. Battle Born. It's still on the flag.

<short pause> And a few months later, when the House votes on the Thirteenth Amendment, Nevada's brand new congressman votes yes.

## Part 3C

But here's the catch.

When the silver runs out in the eighteen eighties, Nevada is a state with almost nobody in it. Towns empty. Mines flood. By nineteen hundred, Nevada has about forty-two thousand people. <tsk> Fewer than many single cities back east.

Some in Congress even suggest taking away its statehood.

<short pause> Then, in nineteen hundred, a prospector named Jim Butler goes looking for his runaway mule. <chuckle> The story goes, he picks up a rock to throw at it... and notices the rock is full of silver.

The town of Tonopah is born. Then gold at Goldfield. A new boom.

<sigh> And then another bust.

## Part 3D

Meanwhile, in the little town of Yerington, a Paiute holy man named Wovoka has a vision during a solar eclipse, in eighteen eighty-nine.

<breath> He teaches a dance. If the people dance it, he says, and live peacefully, the dead will return and the old world will come back.

It's called the Ghost Dance. It spreads across the West, to nations who have lost almost everything. <long pause> Among the Lakota in South Dakota, the army sees it as a threat. And it ends in the massacre at Wounded Knee.

## Part 3E

And Nevada's women are fighting too. In nineteen fourteen, led by a Reno woman named Anne Martin, they win the right to vote. Six years before the national amendment.

<short pause> In nineteen eighteen, Anne Martin runs for the United States Senate. One of the first women ever to try.

## Part 3F

So how does a tiny, empty state survive?

<short pause> It does what bigger states won't. It makes things legal.

First, prize fighting. Most states have banned big boxing matches. In eighteen ninety-seven, Carson City hosts a world heavyweight championship. And in nineteen ten, Reno hosts the "Fight of the Century".

Then, divorce.

Most states make divorce very hard. Nevada makes it easy. In nineteen thirty-one, you only need to live in Nevada for six weeks. <chuckle> Reno becomes the Divorce Capital of the World. Rich people from New York come out to stay at "divorce ranches", waiting out their six weeks with horse rides and cocktails.

And that same year, nineteen thirty-one... <short pause> Nevada legalises gambling.

<chuckle> Reno puts up a glowing arch across its main street. It says: The Biggest Little City in the World. A Reno casino puts up hundreds of signs along highways across the country, as far away as the other side of the world, that just say: Harolds Club or Bust.

<short pause> So far: a state that shouldn't exist, made by telegram. A silver boom, then nothing. And now, a desert that sells what nobody else will.

<breath> All it needs now... is a city.

## Part 4: Anything Goes

Las Vegas began in May nineteen-oh-five, when the railroad auctioned off plots of land in the desert. A water stop between Salt Lake City and Los Angeles. <chuckle> More than a thousand people show up, sweating in the summer sun, and lots sell for hundreds of dollars in a single day.

For years, it's a dusty little railroad town.

Then, in nineteen thirty-one, the federal government starts building a giant dam on the Colorado River, thirty miles away. Thousands of workers pour in. They have paycheques. <short pause> And Las Vegas has casinos.

<chuckle> The government builds a brand new town for the dam workers. Boulder City. And to keep the workers sober and on time, it bans gambling there. <short pause> It's still banned in Boulder City today. In Nevada.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

Building Hoover Dam is brutal. In summer, the canyon heats up to well over a hundred degrees. Workers hang from ropes on the cliffs, blasting rock.

<breath> Officially, ninety-six men die building it.

## Part 4C

Hoover Dam is finished in nineteen thirty-six. <gasp> The biggest dam in the world at the time. Its power lights up Los Angeles, much of the Southwest... and Las Vegas.

In nineteen forty-one, the first resort opens on the highway south of town. <short pause> El Rancho Vegas.

After the war, gangsters notice. In nineteen forty-six, a mobster named Bugsy Siegel opens the Flamingo hotel on that lonely stretch of highway.

<sigh> It loses money. Six months later, Siegel is shot dead in Beverly Hills.

But the Strip has begun.

<short pause> And during the war, the army opens a gunnery school in the desert outside town, to train aircraft gunners. Today, it's Nellis Air Force Base, home of the Thunderbirds.

## Part 4D

And then, the bombs.

In January nineteen fifty-one, the government opens the Nevada Test Site, sixty-five miles from Las Vegas. Over the next decade, it sets off a hundred nuclear explosions above ground.

<laugh> Las Vegas turns it into a tourist attraction. Hotels advertise the best views. People throw dawn "atomic parties" and order "atomic cocktails". A showgirl is crowned Miss Atomic Bomb, with a mushroom cloud stuck to her swimsuit.

<short pause> The windows rattle on the Strip. Sometimes you can see the flash.

Later, the tests move underground. More than eight hundred of them, until nineteen ninety-two.

## Part 4E

Nearby, beside a dry lake bed, the government builds a secret base to test spy planes. <whispers> For decades, it officially doesn't exist.

Area fifty-one.

People see strange shapes in the sky. Very fast. Very high. Some of them think they're seeing aliens. <chuckle> They're mostly seeing spy planes the government won't talk about.

In twenty thirteen, after a records request, the CIA finally releases papers that admit the base is there.

<laugh> In twenty nineteen, a joke online invites people to "storm Area fifty-one". More than a million say they're going. About a hundred and fifty actually show up. Nobody storms anything.

## Part 4F (quiet style)

The winds carried the fallout from the bomb tests east.

<breath> Into Utah. Into small towns in Nevada and Arizona.

Over the following years, many families there developed cancers. They became known as the downwinders.

<long pause> In nineteen ninety, Congress passed a law to compensate some of them.

## Part 5: Vegas

In the nineteen fifties and sixties, Las Vegas means Frank Sinatra and his friends on stage. The Rat Pack.

<short pause> But Las Vegas has an ugly side. For years, it's called "the Mississippi of the West". Black performers, even stars like Sammy Davis Junior, can headline at the casinos, but aren't allowed to stay in the hotels, eat in the restaurants, or swim in the pools.

In nineteen fifty-five, the Moulin Rouge opens. The first casino where everyone is welcome. <short pause> It's a sensation. Then it closes, a few months later.

In March nineteen sixty, local Black leaders threaten a big march on the Strip. The casinos give in. Overnight, Las Vegas desegregates.

## Part 5B

Then a billionaire changes everything.

In nineteen sixty-six, the strange, secretive Howard Hughes moves into the top floors of the Desert Inn, and never leaves. <chuckle> When the hotel asks him to leave so it can give the rooms to gamblers... he buys the hotel.

Then he buys more casinos. Big companies follow him. And slowly, the mob is pushed out.

<short pause> In nineteen sixty-nine, Elvis Presley starts performing in Las Vegas. <gasp> He'll play more than six hundred shows there, in white jumpsuits covered in rhinestones, almost every night of the season, until nineteen seventy-six. Every single one sold out.

## Part 5C

Then come the mega-resorts.

In nineteen eighty-nine, the Mirage opens with a fake volcano out front that erupts every evening. <gasp> Then pyramids. Castles. A fake Venice, with canals and gondolas. A fake Paris, with a half-size Eiffel Tower. A fake New York.

<sigh> And the old casino signs? When they're replaced, many end up in a desert lot called the Neon Boneyard. Giant glowing letters, cowboys and showgirls, lying in the sand. Some of them light up again at night.

Las Vegas becomes one of the fastest-growing cities in America. Families move in for the jobs, and for houses in the sun.

<chuckle> In nineteen fifty, Las Vegas is a town of about twenty-five thousand people. Today, more than two million people live in the Las Vegas area.

<short pause> And every summer since nineteen ninety, tens of thousands of people build a temporary city in the empty Black Rock Desert, north of Reno, for a week of giant art, music and costumes. Burning Man. Then they take it all away, and leave the desert empty again.

## Part 5D (quiet style)

On October first, twenty seventeen, a gunman fires from a hotel window onto a crowd at a country music festival on the Las Vegas Strip.

<long pause> Sixty people are killed. Hundreds are wounded.

It's the deadliest mass shooting by a single gunman in American history.

<breath> The city holds vigils for weeks. The words on the signs: Vegas Strong.

## Part 6: Today

Today, Las Vegas has major league teams. <chuckle> Hockey's Golden Knights, who won the Stanley Cup in their sixth season. Football's Raiders. And a giant glowing sphere that's become the city's newest landmark.

Around forty million people visit every year.

<chuckle> The city even sells itself with a slogan that everyone knows. What happens in Vegas... stays in Vegas.

Near Reno, the desert now holds giant battery factories and data centres.

<short pause> And for decades, Nevada has fought one fight harder than almost any other. The federal government chose Yucca Mountain, about ninety miles from Las Vegas, as the place to bury the country's used nuclear fuel. For thousands of years.

<tsk> Nevada said no. Over and over. Through lawsuits, through Congress, for more than thirty years. Today, the site is still empty. The waste is still stored in other states, waiting.

## Part 6B

But the desert is still the desert.

Las Vegas gets almost all of its water from the Colorado River, through Lake Mead, behind Hoover Dam. <sigh> In years of drought, the lake drops so far that you can see a giant white ring on the rocks, where the water used to be.

<gasp> In twenty twenty-two, as the water fell, a barrel turned up in the mud. Inside was a body. Police think it was a mob killing, from decades ago.

<short pause> And here's one more strange Nevada fact. About eighty percent of the land in Nevada is owned by the federal government. More than any other state.

## Part 6C

So how does an empty desert, rushed into the Union with too few people, become a place forty million people visit every year?

<short pause> It starts with that telegram.

Nevada became a state before it had the people to be one. When the silver ran out, it had to find a way to survive. So it said yes to everything other states said no to. Boxing. Divorce. Gambling.

<long pause> The state that was born in a hurry... learned to make a living out of other people's fun.

<chuckle> And if you ever lose at the tables, just remember. <short pause> Nevada's been betting on itself since eighteen sixty-four.
