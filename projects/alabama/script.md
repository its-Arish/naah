# The Entire History of Alabama

Narration script, written to be voiced with **Gemini TTS** and then animated. About 4,250 spoken words, so roughly 23 to 25 minutes when recorded (California ran 2,600 words to 13:50).

How to use it (the full rules are in `docs/PLAYBOOK.md`):

- Each `##` heading is **one Gemini generation**. Paste only the lines under it, never the heading. The headings are paste breaks: they are never spoken and never shown. The film has no chapter cards.
- Paste the matching style line (table below) into Gemini's style box. Parts marked "quiet style" use the quiet line.
- Tags are angle brackets and only from Gemini's supported list. Emotion words such as `<amazed>` are read aloud, so they are not used.
- CAPITALS stress a word; three dots add a hesitation; years and numbers are spelled out.

On-screen timeline labels (the only sign of a new era): First Alabamians 8000 BC to 1540 · Empires 1540 to 1813 · Statehood 1813 to 1846 · Cotton 1820 to 1860 · War 1861 to 1865 · Iron and peanuts 1865 to 1940 · Rocket City 1941 to 1960 · Five blocks 1955 to 1965 · Moon 1969 to 1979 · Today 1997 to now.

## Hook

The entire history of Alabama is already in your life. You just never noticed.

<short pause> The Mardi Gras beads you caught? America's first Mardi Gras wasn't in New Orleans. Mobile, Alabama, was partying fifteen years before New Orleans even existed.

The suitcase the airline lost? If nobody ever claims it, it ends up for sale... in one store, in a small town in Alabama.

Some of the biggest songs your parents grew up with? Recorded in an Alabama town of fourteen thousand people.

<gasp> And the rocket that carried humans to the Moon? Designed in Alabama.

<long pause> But four years before that rocket flew, in the very same state, people were beaten on a bridge... for trying to vote.

<short pause> So how does one state do BOTH? <whispers> The answer is hiding on one street. Five blocks long.

## Part 1: First Alabamians

Let's go back about ten thousand years.

In the hills of the far north, families are sheltering in the mouth of a huge cave. Today we call it Russell Cave. They came back to it for thousands of years, and they left their campfires behind, layer on top of layer, like a diary written in ash.

Then the people of this land started building.

<short pause> Around a thousand years ago, on the Black Warrior River, near today's Tuscaloosa, a town rose up with twenty-nine earthen mounds around a great plaza. The biggest stood almost sixty feet tall. We call it Moundville.

Around a thousand people lived inside its wooden walls, and maybe ten thousand more farmed the valley around it. They grew corn. They traded shells from the Gulf and copper from the Great Lakes. For a while, it was one of the biggest towns in all of North America.

<sigh> And then, slowly, Moundville emptied out. By the time Europeans arrived, the mounds were quiet.

But the people were not gone. They had become new nations. The Creek. The Cherokee. The Choctaw. The Chickasaw. And one small nation living where two rivers meet, called the Alabama.

<short pause> Hold on to that name. People have been arguing about what it means for nearly two hundred years.

## Part 2: Empires

Fifteen forty. A Spanish conquistador named Hernando de Soto marches into the region with hundreds of soldiers, horses, war dogs, and a herd of pigs. He is looking for gold.

He doesn't find any.

<short pause> In the middle of the region he meets a chief named Tascalusa. Tall. Proud. Taller than any of the Spaniards, the chroniclers said. De Soto takes him hostage and demands porters and food.

Tascalusa says, fine... follow me. And he leads them to a walled town called Mabila.

## Part 2B (quiet style)

October eighteenth, fifteen forty. Inside Mabila, warriors are waiting.

The fighting lasts all day. The Spanish set the town on fire.

<long pause> Thousands of people died at Mabila. It was one of the bloodiest battles ever fought on North American soil.

<long pause> Nobody knows exactly where it happened. The town has never been found.

## Part 2C

After that, Europe went quiet for a hundred and sixty years. Then the French arrived.

Seventeen-oh-two. Two French brothers, Iberville and Bienville, build a fort on the Mobile River. It is the capital of a colony called Louisiana, which in French dreams stretched from the Gulf to Canada.

<chuckle> And what is the very first thing they do? They throw a party.

Seventeen-oh-three. The settlers celebrate Mardi Gras. Masks. Feasts. Within a few years there's a society parading a giant ox through the streets. New Orleans won't even be founded until seventeen eighteen.

<short pause> Then Alabama's coast became a hot potato. France lost it to Britain in seventeen sixty-three. Spain grabbed it in seventeen eighty. And in eighteen thirteen, in the middle of a war with Britain, the United States marched in and took Mobile.

Four flags in one hundred and eleven years. <laugh> Mobile changed owners more often than a rental car.

But the real fight for Alabama was happening inland.

## Part 3: Statehood

The Creek Nation was splitting in two. One side wanted to live alongside the Americans. The other side, called the Red Sticks, wanted the settlers OUT.

It became a civil war inside the Creek Nation, and then it dragged in the United States.

## Part 3B (quiet style)

August eighteen thirteen. Red Stick warriors attack Fort Mims, north of Mobile. Hundreds of people inside are killed.

<long pause> March eighteen fourteen. At a bend in the Tallapoosa River called Horseshoe Bend, an American general named Andrew Jackson attacks the Red Sticks with soldiers, militia, and Cherokee and Creek allies.

More than eight hundred Red Stick warriors die in a single day.

<long pause> Then Jackson made ALL the Creeks sign the peace treaty. Even the ones who had fought on his side. The Creek Nation handed over twenty-three million acres. Much of what is now Alabama.

## Part 3C

Twenty-three million acres of empty map... and America went wild.

They called it Alabama Fever. Farmers sold everything they owned in Georgia, the Carolinas and Tennessee and rushed west for cheap land and one crop. Cotton.

In eighteen-ten, the census counted fewer than ten thousand people here. Ten years later? More than a hundred and twenty-five thousand.

December fourteenth, eighteen nineteen. Alabama becomes the twenty-second state.

<short pause> Then it needed a capital. <chuckle> This took a while.

The first permanent one was a river town called Cahawba. It kept flooding. So they moved to Tuscaloosa. Then they moved again. Alabama had five different capitals in under thirty years.

<short pause> The last one was a cotton town on a bluff over the Alabama River. Montgomery.

And in eighteen forty-six, Alabama built its Capitol on a hill at the top of Montgomery's main street. The street ran straight down the hill to a fountain in a square.

<whispers> Five blocks. Remember this street. The story of Alabama is going to keep walking down it.

<short pause> Oh, and around this time, a newspaper announced that the word Alabama meant "here we rest." It sounded beautiful, and it stuck for generations.

<laugh> Experts in the actual language? They never found any word like it.

## Part 3D (quiet style)

And the Native nations who had lived here for ten thousand years? They were not allowed to rest anywhere.

<long pause> In the eighteen thirties, the government forced the Creek, the Cherokee, the Choctaw and the Chickasaw to leave. Tens of thousands of people were marched west. Thousands died on the way.

<long pause> A few families stayed and held on. Their descendants are the Poarch Band of Creek Indians, and they are still in Alabama today.

## Part 4: Cotton

Here is what all that land was for.

Across the middle of the state runs a strip of dark, rich soil. People called it the Black Belt, because of the colour of the dirt. Cotton grew there like nowhere else.

Steamboats piled with bales floated down the rivers to Mobile, which became one of the biggest cotton ports in the country. That cotton went on to the mills of England and New England. In other words, a shirt worn in England might have started life in an Alabama field.

<long pause> But someone had to pick it.

In eighteen sixty, Alabama had about nine hundred and sixty thousand people. Four hundred and thirty-five thousand of them were enslaved. Almost half the state.

And remember our street in Montgomery? At the bottom of those five blocks, around the fountain in Court Square, enslaved people were sold. Men, women and children, auctioned in the open, a short walk from the Capitol.

<short pause> That is why the Black Belt later got its second meaning. It became the part of Alabama where most Black Alabamians lived.

## Part 4B (quiet style)

By eighteen sixty, bringing people from Africa to the United States had been illegal for more than fifty years.

<long pause> That summer, a ship called the Clotilda slipped into Mobile Bay at night. On board were a hundred and ten people, taken from West Africa. The story goes it was all because of a bet.

The men behind it hid the people in the swamps. Then they burned the ship to hide the crime.

<long pause> It was the last known slave ship to reach America.

## Part 5: War

Six months later, the whole country was coming apart.

January eleventh, eighteen sixty-one. Alabama votes to leave the United States.

<short pause> And now, our street.

February eighteen sixty-one. Delegates from the seceding states gather in Montgomery. They found a new country, the Confederate States of America. And Montgomery is its first capital.

On February eighteenth, at the top of the five blocks, on the steps of the Capitol, Jefferson Davis is sworn in as president of the Confederacy. A bronze star still marks the spot.

<short pause> Then, April eleventh. In an office building at the bottom of that same street, right on Court Square, a telegram is sent to Charleston. It authorises the attack on a fort called Sumter.

<long pause> The next morning, the guns fire. The Civil War has begun. And the order to start it went out from the same five blocks where human beings were sold.

<short pause> Montgomery stayed the capital for about three months. Then the Confederacy moved to Richmond, Virginia, and the war moved away with it.

## Part 5B

For a while, Alabama felt far from the fighting. Its men did not.

<short pause> At sea, a Confederate raider named the CSS Alabama hunted Union merchant ships across the world's oceans, until it was sunk off the coast of France.

August eighteen sixty-four. Union warships force their way into Mobile Bay through a field of underwater mines, which back then were called torpedoes. The story goes that Admiral Farragut shouted, "Damn the torpedoes! Full speed ahead!" Whether he said it or not, he won the bay.

<short pause> And in the spring of eighteen sixty-five, Union cavalry swept across Alabama. They burned ironworks and arsenals. They burned most of the University of Alabama.

Spring, eighteen sixty-five. The war ends. And four hundred and thirty-five thousand people in Alabama are free.

## Part 6: Iron and peanuts

Freedom changed everything, fast.

Within five years, Alabama sent its first Black congressman to Washington. Benjamin Turner of Selma. He had been born enslaved.

<sigh> It did not last.

By nineteen-oh-one, white leaders wrote a new state constitution. They did not hide why. The man in charge of the convention told the delegates the goal was, quote, "to establish white supremacy in this State."

<short pause> Poll taxes and literacy tests followed, and the voting rolls emptied. Most Black Alabamians, and many poor white Alabamians, lost the vote.

And that constitution was amended so many times, nearly a thousand times, that it became the longest constitution in the world. <chuckle> Seriously. It's longer than most novels.

<long pause> But underground, something else was waiting.

## Part 6B

Near the middle of the state, there's a ridge called Red Mountain. It turned out to hold iron ore. Right next to it, coal. And next to that, limestone.

Iron ore, coal and limestone are the three things you need to make iron. Finding all three in one place is incredibly rare.

So in eighteen seventy-one, investors built a brand-new city right on top of them, and named it after the great English industrial city. Birmingham.

It grew so fast that people called it the Magic City.

<short pause> To show off, Birmingham cast a giant iron statue of Vulcan, the Roman god of the forge, for the nineteen-oh-four World's Fair. Fifty-six feet tall. Still the largest cast-iron statue in the world.

<laugh> When the fair ended, Birmingham couldn't pay the shipping bill. So the god of the forge was dumped in pieces beside the railroad tracks.

Today he stands on top of Red Mountain, with his bare backside pointed straight at the neighbouring suburb.

## Part 6C (quiet style)

But the Magic City had a dark engine room.

<long pause> Thousands of Black men were arrested for tiny offences, like vagrancy, and then leased out to coal mines and work camps as prisoners. They worked in terrible conditions. Many died.

Alabama was the last state to end this system. In nineteen twenty-eight.

## Part 6D

Meanwhile, in the town of Tuskegee, a former slave named Booker T. Washington had opened a school for Black students. In eighteen ninety-six, he hired a quiet scientist named George Washington Carver.

Carver looked at the tired cotton fields and said: plant peanuts. They put goodness back into the soil.

<short pause> Then he found hundreds of uses for them. Dyes. Milk. Plastics. Cosmetics.

<chuckle> And no, he did not invent peanut butter. Sorry.

Farmers did not want to listen. Cotton was money. Then, in the nineteen-tens, a tiny beetle crawled in from Mexico. The boll weevil.

It ate the cotton. Field after field. Whole counties were ruined.

<short pause> So in the town of Enterprise, the farmers finally switched to peanuts. And they started making more money than they ever had from cotton.

So in nineteen nineteen, the grateful people of Enterprise did something no town on Earth had ever done.

<laugh> They built a monument... to the bug.

It's still there, in the middle of Main Street. A woman holding a giant boll weevil over her head. The only monument in the world to an agricultural pest.

<short pause> And just one more. In nineteen-ten, the Wright brothers came to an old cotton plantation outside Montgomery and opened America's first civilian flying school. Their students made the first night flights in history.

Alabama farmers were watching airplanes before most Americans had ever seen one.

## Part 6E

And the people. <short pause> Alabama kept producing people the whole world would know.

In eighteen eighty, in the town of Tuscumbia, a baby girl was born who would lose her sight and her hearing before she was two. Years later, at a water pump in her family's garden, her teacher spelled a word into her hand, over and over, while cold water ran across the other one. W, A, T, E, R.

<gasp> That was the moment Helen Keller understood that things have names. She went on to write books and travel the world.

<short pause> And the list keeps going. Jesse Owens, who won four gold medals in Berlin in front of Hitler. Joe Louis, the boxer. Hank Aaron and Willie Mays, two of the greatest baseball players ever. Nat King Cole. Hank Williams. All of them born in Alabama.

<sigh> And many of them grew up somewhere else. Because in the early nineteen hundreds, hundreds of thousands of Black Alabamians packed up and left for the factories of the North, for Chicago, Detroit and Cleveland, looking for work and for a life without Jim Crow.

## Part 6F (quiet style)

Nineteen thirty-one. A freight train in northern Alabama. Nine Black teenagers, the youngest about thirteen, are pulled off it and accused of a crime they did not commit.

<long pause> In the town of Scottsboro, all-white juries sentence eight of them to death.

One of their accusers later took her story back. The case went to the United States Supreme Court twice, and it changed the law on fair trials for everyone in America.

<long pause> But the young men lost years of their lives in prison. Alabama officially pardoned the last of them in twenty thirteen.

<long pause> Remember the name Scottsboro. It comes back, in a very different way.

## Part 6G

Now keep the Wright brothers in mind. Because the sky is about to become Alabama's thing.

In nineteen forty-one, at Tuskegee, the Army began training America's first Black military pilots. Plenty of people in charge expected them to fail.

<short pause> Nearly a thousand pilots earned their wings there. In the war over Europe they painted the tails of their planes red, and bomber crews started asking for the Red Tails by name.

## Part 6H (quiet style)

But Tuskegee holds a darker story too.

<long pause> Starting in nineteen thirty-two, government doctors studied hundreds of Black men in the area who had syphilis. The men were never told what they had.

<long pause> When penicillin became the cure, the men were not given it. The study ran for forty years, until a reporter exposed it in nineteen seventy-two.

<long pause> In nineteen ninety-seven, the President of the United States apologised to the survivors.

## Part 7: Rocket City

Nineteen forty-one. War again. The Army builds a giant weapons plant next to a quiet cotton town in the north called Huntsville. Its name is Redstone Arsenal.

When the war ends, the arsenal goes quiet. Huntsville is about to lose its biggest employer.

<short pause> And then, in nineteen fifty, the Army sends over a hundred German engineers to Huntsville.

Huntsville at the time had about sixteen thousand people, and proudly called itself the Watercress Capital of the World.

<gasp> And these strangers had come to build rockets.

Their leader was Wernher von Braun. In Germany, he had built the V-2, the world's first long-range ballistic missile.

## Part 7B (quiet style)

That is the part of the story Huntsville took years to face.

<long pause> Von Braun had been a member of the Nazi Party and an officer in the SS. His V-2 rockets were built underground by prisoners from a concentration camp. Thousands of those prisoners died. More people died building the V-2 than were killed by it.

<long pause> After the war, the United States wanted his knowledge more than it wanted answers. Questions about his past came much later.

## Part 7C

In Huntsville, the team got to work.

January nineteen fifty-eight. A rocket built by the Huntsville team launches Explorer One, America's very first satellite.

Nineteen sixty. NASA opens the Marshall Space Flight Center at Redstone. Von Braun is its first director. And they start drawing the biggest rocket the world had ever seen.

The Saturn Five. Taller than the Statue of Liberty. Built to take people to the Moon.

<short pause> The watercress town filled up with engineers, mathematicians and machinists. Within ten years, Huntsville's population had more than quadrupled. They called it the Rocket City.

<long pause> But a few hours south, in Montgomery, on our street... something else was about to launch.

## Part 8: Five blocks

Nineteen fifty-four. A young preacher arrives to lead a small brick church on Dexter Avenue, one block down the hill from the Capitol. He is twenty-five years old. His name is Martin Luther King Junior.

<short pause> In Montgomery, the city buses had a rule. Black passengers paid at the front, got off, walked round and got back on at the back door. And if a white passenger needed a seat, a Black passenger had to stand.

In March nineteen fifty-five, a fifteen-year-old girl named Claudette Colvin refused to give up her seat. She was arrested.

Nine months later, on December first, a seamstress finished work at a department store and walked to the bus stop. Right at the bottom of our five blocks. On Court Square. Where people had once been sold.

Her name was Rosa Parks.

When the driver told her to stand up, she said no.

<long pause> Four days later, Black Montgomery stopped riding the buses.

People walked to work. Miles every day. In the rain. They organised car pools. And the boycott was planned in that little church on Dexter Avenue, with a twenty-six-year-old Dr. King as its leader.

<short pause> They kept it up for three hundred and eighty-one days. And in the end, the Supreme Court ruled that Montgomery's segregated buses were against the Constitution.

<long pause> The bus boycott was only the first round.

In nineteen sixty-three, Alabama's new governor, George Wallace, stood on the Capitol steps, on the same spot where Jefferson Davis had been sworn in, and promised, quote, "segregation forever."

That summer, he stood in a doorway at the University of Alabama to block two Black students from enrolling. He stepped aside that same day.

<short pause> In Birmingham, thousands of children, some still in grade school, marched for their rights. The police met them with dogs and fire hoses. The pictures went around the world.

## Part 8B (quiet style)

September fifteenth, nineteen sixty-three. A Sunday morning in Birmingham.

<long pause> A bomb planted by Ku Klux Klan members exploded at the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church.

<long pause> Four girls were killed. Addie Mae Collins. Carole Robertson. Cynthia Wesley. Denise McNair.

<long pause> Denise was eleven. The others were fourteen.

## Part 8C

Then came Selma.

In Dallas County, around Selma, more than half the people were Black. But only about two out of every hundred eligible Black voters were registered. Every time people lined up at the courthouse to register, they were turned away, or arrested.

<short pause> So they decided to march. Fifty-four miles, from Selma all the way to Montgomery. To the Capitol. To the top of our five blocks.

## Part 8D (quiet style)

Sunday, March seventh, nineteen sixty-five. About six hundred marchers walk out of Selma, two by two, and climb the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

<long pause> At the bottom, state troopers are waiting.

<long pause> The troopers charge with clubs and tear gas. A young organiser named John Lewis has his skull fractured.

<long pause> That night, the whole country saw it on television. It became known as Bloody Sunday.

## Part 8E

Two weeks later, they tried again, this time protected by federal troops.

Five days. Fifty-four miles. They slept in fields along the highway. And on March twenty-fifth, around twenty-five thousand people marched up Dexter Avenue.

<short pause> Past the fountain where people were once sold. Past the bus stop where Rosa Parks said no. Past Dr. King's church. Up the hill, to the steps where the Confederacy was born.

That August, the President signed the Voting Rights Act.

<long pause> Five blocks. Slavery at the bottom. The Confederacy at the top. And a hundred years later, the people the Confederacy was built to keep down... walked up those same five blocks and took the steps.

## Part 9: Moon

Now cut back to Huntsville.

July sixteenth, nineteen sixty-nine. In Florida, the Saturn Five, the rocket designed in Alabama, lifts off with three astronauts on top.

<gasp> Four days later, a human being steps onto the Moon.

<short pause> Four years after Bloody Sunday. Same state.

So here is the answer to the question from the very beginning. How does one state do BOTH?

Because the rocket and the bridge were the same kind of story. Ordinary people in Alabama decided that the line everyone said they could never cross... was not the end of the road.

One group did it with engineering. The other did it with their feet.

<short pause> And a third group did it with a song.

Remember that town of fourteen thousand people from the very start? Muscle Shoals, on the Tennessee River in the far north.

In the nineteen sixties, a little studio there called FAME started making hits. Wilson Pickett recorded Mustang Sally there. In nineteen sixty-seven, a young singer named Aretha Franklin walked in, sat down at the piano, and cut the song that made her a star.

<short pause> The house band were local white musicians. They called themselves the Swampers. In a state that was fighting to keep Black and white people apart, they were in a room every day making Black soul music with Black singers.

<chuckle> The story goes that when one famous New York songwriter tried to hire the Black musicians he'd heard on those records, he was told they were four white guys from Alabama.

And in December nineteen sixty-nine, five months after the Moon landing, the Rolling Stones quietly drove into town and recorded Brown Sugar and Wild Horses there. Most of the locals had no idea.

<long pause> And the five blocks have one more stop.

Nineteen seventy-nine. George Wallace, the man who promised segregation forever, now in a wheelchair after an assassination attempt, asked to be wheeled into Dr. King's old church on Dexter Avenue.

<short pause> In front of the congregation, he apologised. He said he had learned what suffering means, and he asked for their forgiveness.

<long pause> Some people forgave him. Some never did. Both are fair. But he went back, and he asked, on that same street.

## Part 10: Today

And Alabama kept moving.

In nineteen ninety-seven, Mercedes-Benz opened its first American car factory, in a small Alabama town called Vance. Then came Honda, Hyundai, Toyota and Mazda. Today Alabama's factories can build more than a million cars a year. The SUV in the parking lot outside? There's a real chance it was built here.

<short pause> And remember Scottsboro? In nineteen seventy, a man there started buying lost luggage that nobody had ever claimed. Today, when the airlines give up on a bag, it ends up at his family's store, Unclaimed Baggage. About a million people a year drive to a small Alabama town to shop for strangers' suitcases.

<laugh> Laptops. Wedding dresses. Somebody's skis. If you ever lost a bag... it might be on a shelf in Alabama right now.

Airbus builds passenger jets in Mobile, the city that still throws America's oldest Mardi Gras. The little town that called itself the Watercress Capital is now Alabama's biggest city. And in twenty twenty-five, the government announced that the military's Space Command headquarters would move to Huntsville too.

<short pause> In twenty nineteen, researchers confirmed they had found the wreck of the Clotilda, buried in the mud of the Mobile River. The descendants of the people it carried still live nearby, in a neighbourhood their ancestors built after the war. It's called Africatown.

<long pause> And in April twenty twenty-six, a new moon rocket lifted off with four astronauts. It was designed and managed at the Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville. They flew around the Moon and came home, farther from Earth than any human beings had ever been.

<short pause> Alabama sent people around the Moon. Again.

<chuckle> And on the ground? Every autumn, half the state still stops talking to the other half for one football game, Alabama against Auburn. They call it the Iron Bowl. Of course they do. Birmingham was built on iron.

<long pause> So let's go back to that name. Alabama.

Remember the newspaper that said it meant "here we rest"? Nobody has ever found those words in the language.

The most common explanation is that it comes from Choctaw words about clearing the land. Clearing the thicket.

<short pause> The people who clear the thicket.

<long pause> Ten thousand years of people clearing a path where there wasn't one. With a cave fire. With a peanut. With a rocket. With a bus seat. With a march across a bridge.

<short pause> That is the entire history of Alabama.

<whispers> Never "here we rest."

<short pause> Always... clear the path.
